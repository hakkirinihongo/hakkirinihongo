**How is “before” expressed in Japanese, and what is the difference between 前に (mae ni), 前は (mae wa), and 前から (mae kara)?**

To begin with, the basic distinctions are these:

- **前に** places one event before another point in time or event and can also mean “previously”; 
- **前は** introduces an earlier situation, often contrasting it with the present or a later one; 
- **前から** means “since before” or “for some time,” emphasizing that something already existed or was happening from an earlier point onward. 

All three expressions share the noun **前 (mae)**, used here in its temporal sense, but the particles **に**, **は**, and **から** change how that idea of “before” is presented.

- With **前に**, the focus is on **when something happens in relation to a point of reference**. In **寝る前に本を読みます**, “I read a book before going to bed,” the reading takes place before bedtime. 
  
  The reference point can be an action, an event, or a specific moment: what matters is placing something earlier than that reference point. 
  
  This does not necessarily mean referring to the past, since the sentence may also describe a habit or something we intend to do before a future event. 
  
  Furthermore, **前に** can be used without naming another event, meaning “before” or “previously,” to refer back to something that has already happened.

- With **前は**, the focus shifts to **the circumstances of an earlier period, presented as the topic of discussion**. In **前はよく本を読みました**, “I used to read a lot,” the speaker describes a former habit, often suggesting a contrast with the present. 
  
  The particle **は** brings that earlier period into focus: the point is to explain what things were like then, what habits people had, or what conditions characterized that phase. 
  
  The contrast may be stated explicitly or left implicit; the comparison may be with the present or with another period established by the context.

- With **前から**, the central idea is **that a situation originated earlier and, typically, continued up to the relevant point in time**. 
  
  In **前からこの本を読みたいと思っていました**, “I'd been wanting to read this book for some time,” the desire already existed earlier and had persisted up to the time being discussed. **から** therefore introduces a different perspective: the emphasis falls on how far back something goes, whether it is a state, a feeling, or an activity. 
  
  The starting point may be left unspecified, and the continuity may extend to the present or to another point in the narrative.

Choosing between **前に・前は・前から** therefore requires identifying the temporal relationship we want to express: placing an event before a reference point, describing an earlier situation, or emphasizing that something was already underway beforehand. The expressions may receive similar translations, but they highlight different information.

The following sections begin with an overview of **the meanings and differences between 前に・前は・前から**, before examining the formation and uses of each expression in turn. 

We will then compare them directly, including **register and usage contexts**, and look at a section of **annotated examples**. The conclusions will bring together the essential criteria for choosing the form that best conveys the intended meaning.

## 前に・前は・前から: Meaning and Differences

To understand the difference between **前に (mae ni)**, **前は (mae wa)**, and **前から (mae kara)**, we must start with what they have in common. In these uses, **前 (mae)** is a noun referring to a time or period **earlier than a reference point**. That reference point may be the present, an event mentioned in the sentence, or a moment already established by the context.

In **会議の前**, “before the meeting,” the reference point is the meeting; in **日本に来る前**, “before coming to Japan,” it is the arrival in Japan. When we simply say **前に**, **前は**, or **前から**, the reference point must instead be inferred from the situation and the surrounding discourse: it may be the moment of speaking, a current state of affairs, or an event being recounted.

The particles add three distinct functions to this shared foundation: **に** links an event to its position in time; **は** presents the earlier period as a topic, often with a sense of contrast; **から** indicates an earlier starting point. Choosing the appropriate expression therefore involves considering both the reference point and the information we want to foreground.

### The Reference Point: “Before” What?

The temporal reference point is essential because **“before” expresses a relationship of anteriority**, which can also involve future events. Consider these two sentences:

*<p class="jp-example">昨日、会議の前に資料を確認しました。</p>*

*Yesterday, I checked the documents before the meeting.*

*<p class="jp-example">明日、会議の前に資料を確認します。</p>*

*Tomorrow, I'll check the documents before the meeting.*

In both cases, **会議の前に** places the document check before the meeting. In the first sentence, both events are in the past; in the second, they are yet to happen. The relationship remains the same: the check precedes the meeting.

We must therefore distinguish **the moment of speaking** from **the moment used as the reference point in the sentence**. To determine whether we are recounting the past, describing a habit, or outlining a future plan, we also need to consider the predicate and any other time expressions.

The reference point may also remain implicit:

*<p class="jp-example">前に、この映画を見たことがあります。</p>*

*I've seen this film before.*

Here, **前に** refers to an occasion earlier than the moment of speaking, without specifying when it occurred. The context allows us to understand it as “before now” or “previously.” The same notion of anteriority can therefore be conveyed with an explicit reference point, such as a meeting, or one inferred from the situation.

### What Changes with に, は, and から?

To see what each particle contributes, imagine someone who is already in Japan talking about their Japanese studies.

*<p class="jp-example">日本に来る前に、日本語を勉強しました。</p>*

*I studied Japanese before coming to Japan.*

With **前に**, the studying is placed **before the arrival in Japan**. The speaker establishes a temporal relationship between studying and arriving: the period of study being discussed occurred earlier than that event.

This wording leaves several possibilities open. The person might have studied for a few weeks or several years; they might have continued studying after arriving or stopped altogether. On its own, the sentence focuses on when the activity took place relative to the chosen reference point.

Moreover, **に** can locate an extended activity in time: its presence does not automatically imply that the event was brief or instantaneous.

*<p class="jp-example">日本に来る前は、日本語を勉強していました。</p>*

*Before coming to Japan, I was studying Japanese.*

With **前は**, the period before the arrival is presented as **the phase whose circumstances are being described**. The meaning is close to “As for the time before I came to Japan, I was studying Japanese.”

The focus thus shifts to what life was like during that period: what activities someone engaged in, what habits they had, or what circumstances prevailed. This choice often invites a comparison with the later period, even when the comparison remains unstated.

Any change, however, must be inferred from the context. **前は** may suggest that things are different now, but exactly what has changed depends on the discourse: the activity, its frequency, the way it was carried out, or other circumstances may have changed.

*<p class="jp-example">日本に来る前から、日本語を勉強しています。</p>*

*I've been studying Japanese since before I came to Japan.*

With **前から**, the emphasis falls on **the fact that studying began before the arrival**. In this example, the activity had already started before coming to Japan and continues in the present.

The role of **から** is crucial: it introduces the point or period from which the situation began. The arrival in Japan serves to place that beginning even further back in time. We do not know exactly when the person started studying, but we do know that they were already studying before arriving.

The three sentences may therefore describe the same personal experience from different angles: **when the studying took place**, **what the earlier period was like**, or **the earlier beginning of an ongoing activity**. The choice of predicate also helps make the intended interpretation explicit.

### Position in Time, Continuity, and Distance

The distinction between locating an event and indicating how long a situation has continued becomes especially clear when a length of time is mentioned:

*<p class="jp-example">三年前に、この町に引っ越しました。</p>*

*I moved to this town three years ago.*

*<p class="jp-example">三年前から、この町に住んでいます。</p>*

*I've lived in this town for three years.*

In the first sentence, **三年前に** places the move three years before the present. In the second, **三年前から** identifies when the person began living in the town and connects that starting point to the present situation. In English, this difference is conveyed by “three years ago” and “for three years.”

The continuity associated with **前から** can also be viewed from a point in the past:

*<p class="jp-example">彼に初めて会ったとき、私は前から彼の名前を知っていました。</p>*

*When I first met him, I already knew his name.*

The reference point here is **the first meeting**. The speaker had known the name before that time and still knew it when they met. The sentence describes this relationship within the narrative without specifying what happened afterward.

The temporal distance must also be considered. 

- **前** expresses anteriority, while the actual interval is specified by other words or inferred from the context;
- **前に** can refer to something that happened shortly before or much earlier; 
- **前から** can refer to a situation that began recently or has lasted for years. 


A translation such as “for some time” must therefore be understood in context rather than automatically interpreted as “for a very long time.”

The full interpretation emerges from the combination of **前**, the particle, and the predicate. The shared element identifies an earlier time; the particle shapes its function in the sentence; and the predicate and context clarify what happens during that time and how it relates to other moments in the discourse.

With this foundation, we can now examine **前に**, **前は**, and **前から** individually, looking more closely at their formation, uses, and nuances.

## 前に (mae ni)

### Meaning and Usage

In its temporal uses, **前に (mae ni)** means **“before…”**, **“before something happens…”**, or, when the reference point is implicit, **“before” or “previously.”** Its essential function is to place an event at a time or within a period earlier than a reference point.

In the construction **A 前に B**, **A** represents the temporal reference point, while **B** expresses the event that precedes it. To interpret the sentence correctly, we must therefore distinguish word order from the order of events: A is mentioned first in the sentence, but B happens first in time.

*<p class="jp-example">夕食を食べる前に、シャワーを浴びます。</p>*
*I take a shower before dinner.*

Dinner is the reference point, and the shower is placed before it. The construction can describe a habit, recount a past event, outline a future plan, or introduce an instruction. Its specific interpretation depends on the predicate and the context.

**前に expresses anteriority without specifying how much time separates the two events.** The shower might take place immediately before dinner or some time earlier. When the interval matters, it can be specified through other time expressions.

The duration and completion of the activity also depend on the predicate. **前に** can place a brief action, an extended activity, or the beginning of something before the reference point: it does not automatically make the event instantaneous.

The construction can also connect events involving different subjects and describe situations beyond the speaker's control. It can therefore be used to recount natural phenomena, accidents, or interruptions.

**When 前に is used without an explicit reference point**, it frequently means “previously” or “on an earlier occasion.” In this use, it refers back to a past event without necessarily specifying when it took place. It commonly occurs in accounts of experiences, recollections, and references to information received earlier.

**When preceded by an expression of elapsed time**, it indicates how far back something occurred relative to the reference point: **三日前に** means “three days ago” or “three days earlier,” depending on the context. If the reference point is the present, English will normally use “ago”; if it is another point in a narrative, “earlier” or “before” will be appropriate.

All these uses share the same foundation: an event is placed before a reference point, whether that point is explicitly stated, inferred from the context, or taken to be the present.

**After a verb, use the affirmative dictionary form**, without inserting **の**: **食べる前に**, **行く前に**, **勉強する前に**, **来る前に**.

This form remains unchanged even when the sentence describes a past event:

*<p class="jp-example">日本に来る前に、ひらがなとカタカナを覚えました。</p>*
*Before coming to Japan, I learned hiragana and katakana.*

The speaker is recounting something that has already happened, but the form before **前に** is **来る**, not **来た**. The verb form in the subordinate clause expresses the relationship with the event placed “before”; the past tense of the sentence is conveyed by **覚えました**.

In the basic temporal construction, forms such as **× 来た前に**, **× 来ます前に**, and **× 来るの前に** should therefore be avoided. The correct form is **来る前に**, even when the main predicate is polite.

If the reference point is the beginning of a new state, that change must be expressed: to say “before it gets dark,” use **暗くなる前に**, literally “before it becomes dark.” The adjective **暗い** on its own describes a state, not the transition into that state.

**After a noun, insert の.** The noun must identify an event, activity, or occasion that can serve as a temporal reference point: **食事の前に**, “before a meal”; **出発の前に**, “before departure”; **試験の前に**, “before an exam.”

A noun that simply denotes an object does not automatically express the action associated with it. To say “before reading a book,” we therefore need **本を読む前に**: the word **本** by itself does not mean “reading a book.”

**Do not insert の after an amount of time**: **一時間前に**, “an hour ago/earlier”; **三日前に**, “three days ago/earlier”; **二年前に**, “two years ago/earlier.” To express “three days ago,” the correct construction is thus **三日前に**, without **の** between **三日** and **前**.

An event can be combined with a precise time interval: **出発の二時間前に** means “two hours before departure,” while **出発する二時間前に** expresses the same reference point using a verb. Here too, **の** links the noun **出発** to the rest of the expression; it is not inserted after **二時間**.

**As for position in the sentence**, the expression with **前に** normally precedes the predicate whose temporal location it specifies. It can appear at the beginning of the sentence or after the topic:

*<p class="jp-example">私は、出かける前に天気予報を確認します。</p>*
*Before going out, I check the weather forecast.*

The topic **私は** is introduced first; **出かける前に** specifies when the forecast is checked. Commas may aid readability, but they are not a required part of the construction.

If the subject of the reference event differs from that of the main clause, it can be expressed in the subordinate clause, usually with **が**: **子どもが寝る前に**, “before the child goes to sleep.” When the subjects are the same or can easily be inferred, they may remain unstated.

In some contexts, **前** also occurs without **に**. Omission is possible, but its appropriateness must be judged in the particular sentence: for basic examples, **前に** provides a clear and common formulation.

### Register

**前に is neutral in register** and can be used in both spoken and written Japanese. It appears in everyday conversations, personal narratives, instructions, notices, and professional communication.

The degree of familiarity or politeness depends primarily on the vocabulary and forms used alongside it:

*<p class="jp-example">寝る前に、窓を閉めてね。</p>*
*Make sure you close the window before going to bed, okay?*

*<p class="jp-example">ご利用になる前に、注意事項をご確認ください。</p>*
*Please read the precautions before using the service.*

In the first sentence, **閉めてね** creates a familiar tone; in the second, **ご利用になる** and **ご確認ください** belong to a respectful style suited to instructions addressed to the public. **前に** retains the same temporal meaning in both cases.

The dictionary form before **前に** is a feature of the construction and is regularly used even in polite sentences. There is no need to change it to a **ます** form to match the register of the main clause.

### Examples

*<p class="jp-example">朝、家を出る前に、天気予報を確認します。</p>*
*In the morning, I check the weather forecast before leaving home.*

A habit: the forecast is checked before leaving.

*<p class="jp-example">昨日、電車に乗る前に、駅で水を買いました。</p>*
*Yesterday, I bought some water at the station before boarding the train.*

The narrative is in the past, but **乗る** remains in the dictionary form.

*<p class="jp-example">明日、出発する前に、ホテルの予約を確認しておきます。</p>*
*Tomorrow, I'll check my hotel reservation before setting off.*

Both the reference event and the main action are in the future. **確認しておきます** presents checking the reservation as preparation.

*<p class="jp-example">会議の前に、必要な資料を全員に送りました。</p>*
*Before the meeting, I sent everyone the necessary documents.*

The reference point is expressed by a noun: **会議 + の + 前に**.

*<p class="jp-example">彼が来る前に、部屋を片付けておこう。</p>*
*Let's tidy up the room before he arrives.*

The subject of the subordinate clause is expressed by **彼が** and differs from the subject of the main action.

*<p class="jp-example">私が話し終わる前に、電話が切れてしまいました。</p>*
*The call got cut off before I finished speaking.*

The main event is an involuntary interruption. The completion of the speaker's remarks serves as the reference point, even though the interruption prevents that point from being reached.

*<p class="jp-example">暗くなる前に、家に帰りましょう。</p>*
*Let's get home before it gets dark.*

**暗くなる** expresses the change of state used as the temporal reference point.

*<p class="jp-example">三日前に、注文していた本が届きました。</p>*
*The book I'd ordered arrived three days ago.*

The amount of time comes directly before **前に**, without **の**.

*<p class="jp-example">面接の三十分前に、会場に着きました。</p>*
*I arrived at the venue thirty minutes before the interview.*

The reference point is the interview, relative to which an interval of thirty minutes is specified.

*<p class="jp-example">前に、この店で友達と食事をしたことがあります。</p>*
*I've eaten at this restaurant with a friend before.*

**前に** is used on its own to mean “previously,” without giving a specific date.

## 前は (mae wa)

### Meaning and Usage

In its temporal uses, **前は (mae wa)** presents **an earlier period as the topic of discussion**: the speaker singles out that phase and describes what things were like, what habits they had, or what circumstances prevailed. Depending on the context, it can be translated as **“before,”** **“in the past,”** or **“before…”**.

The construction combines **前**, which identifies an earlier time, with the particle **は**, which brings it into focus. Its meaning is therefore close to “as for that earlier period…,” although this wording is intended to clarify the grammatical mechanism rather than serve as the most natural English translation.

When **前は** is used on its own, the most immediate contrast is often with the present:

*<p class="jp-example">前は毎朝走っていましたが、今は週末だけ走っています。</p>*
*I used to run every morning, but now I only run on weekends.*

The speaker describes a former habit and explicitly compares it with the current one. In this example, the frequency has changed: the activity continues, but it happens less often. **前は therefore does not automatically mean that the activity being discussed has stopped altogether.**

The comparison may also remain implicit. A sentence such as “I used to work here” normally suggests a difference from the current situation, but exactly what has changed must be inferred from the context.

**前は can also follow an explicit reference point**, which defines the period being discussed:

*<p class="jp-example">日本に来る前は、どんな仕事をしていましたか。</p>*
*What kind of work did you do before coming to Japan?*

Here, the reference point is the arrival in Japan. The question invites the listener to describe their work during the preceding period, without itself specifying whether they subsequently left that job.

This use is particularly suited to **states, circumstances, habits, and activities that characterize a period**: where someone lived, what job they held, what they could do, what opinions they had, or how they felt.

The period being described does not necessarily have to be in the past. With a recurring reference point, **前は** can express a habitual situation:

*<p class="jp-example">試験の前は、いつも緊張します。</p>*
*I always get nervous before exams.*

The speaker describes what habitually happens in the period leading up to exams. The main clause is in the non-past form because it expresses a regular occurrence. An explicit reference point can also identify a future period: what matters is that it is presented as the phase whose circumstances are being described.

Finally, **前は does not itself specify how far back that “before” lies**. It can refer to a recent situation or to a distant era. The length of the period under consideration also depends on the context.

The particle **は** is pronounced **wa** and follows **前** directly.

**After a verb, use the affirmative dictionary form**, without inserting **の**. This form remains unchanged even when the main clause describes a past situation:

*<p class="jp-example">この会社に入る前は、別の会社で働いていました。</p>*
*Before joining this company, I worked for another one.*

The person has already joined the company, but the form before **前は** is **入る**. The past tense of the situation being described is conveyed by **働いていました**.

In the basic temporal construction, we therefore use **来る前は**, **引っ越す前は**, and **働き始める前は**. Forms such as **× 来た前は**, **× 来ます前は**, and **× 来るの前は** should instead be avoided.

When the reference point is a change of state, it must be expressed using the appropriate verb: **忙しくなる前は** means “before becoming busy,” while **学生になる前は** means “before becoming a student.”

**After a noun denoting an event or activity, insert の**: **試験の前は**, **旅行の前は**, **出発の前は**. The noun identifies the reference point relative to which the earlier period is selected.

**Do not insert の after an amount of time measuring the distance from the reference point**: **三日前は**, **一年前は**, **十年前は**.

In these cases, the sentence describes the situation at a moment or during a period at that distance in time. **三年前は** thus means something like “three years ago, as things stood then…”; it does not express a situation that has lasted “for three years.”

**The main predicate can take different forms.** **～ていました** and **～ていた** are common because they are well suited to describing ongoing activities, habits, and resultant states. However, adjectives, nouns, and other verb forms can also be used: **忙しかった**, “I was busy”; **静かでした**, “it was quiet”; **学生でした**, “I was a student”; **わかりませんでした**, “I didn't understand.”

The choice depends on what is being expressed. **前は does not automatically require ～ていた**, nor does it require the past tense when the sentence describes a habitual or future situation.

As for position, the temporal reference point may be introduced at the beginning of the sentence or after another topic:

*<p class="jp-example">私は、日本に来る前は、イタリアで働いていました。</p>*
*Before coming to Japan, I worked in Italy.*

**私は** introduces the person being discussed; **日本に来る前は** selects the phase of their life to which the following information refers.

Finally, **前は** must be distinguished from **前には**. In **前には**, the particle は is added to **前に**, which retains its temporal meaning; は highlights that reference point and introduces the sense of contrast characteristic of this particle.

The two forms are not automatically interchangeable.

For example, when giving an instruction about something to do before an event, **前に** or **前には** is normally appropriate:

*<p class="jp-example">食事の前には、必ず手を洗ってください。</p>*
*Always wash your hands before meals, please.*

Notice the contrast conveyed by the particle は. The instruction is to wash one's hands not just at any time, but specifically before eating.

**前は, by contrast, is particularly suited to presenting the situation that characterizes the earlier period**. This distinction will be explored further in the comparison of the three expressions.

### Register

**前は is neutral in register** and can be used in everyday conversations, personal narratives, and descriptions of change. It can also appear in professional explanations and informative texts, especially when the temporal reference point is clearly specified.

The tone depends on the vocabulary, the predicate forms, and the other expressions used:

*<p class="jp-example">前は、よくこの公園で遊んでたんだ。</p>*
*I used to play in this park a lot.*

*<p class="jp-example">改修の前は、この建物にはエレベーターがありませんでした。</p>*
*Before the renovation, this building didn't have an elevator.*

In the first sentence, the contracted form **遊んでた** and the ending **んだ** create a colloquial, explanatory tone. In the second, the vocabulary and the form **ありませんでした** suit a polite, informative description.

The dictionary form before **前は** remains correct even when the main clause is polite. There is no need to turn it into a **ます** form to match the sentence's register.

### Examples

*<p class="jp-example">前は、毎日電車で通勤していました。</p>*
*I used to commute to work by train every day.*

A former habit is being described. Any difference from the present remains implicit.

*<p class="jp-example">前は、コーヒーが飲めませんでしたが、今は毎朝飲んでいます。</p>*
*I couldn't drink coffee before, but now I have it every morning.*

The contrast between past and present is explicitly expressed through **前は** and **今は**.

*<p class="jp-example">前は、この道には街灯がありませんでした。</p>*
*There used to be no streetlights on this road.*

**前は** introduces an earlier condition relating to a place.

*<p class="jp-example">日本に来る前は、イタリアの会社で働いていました。</p>*
*Before coming to Japan, I worked for an Italian company.*

The arrival in Japan marks the boundary of the period whose work circumstances are being described.

*<p class="jp-example">一人暮らしを始める前は、料理をほとんどしませんでした。</p>*
*Before I started living on my own, I hardly ever cooked.*

The negative main clause describes a habit from the earlier period.

*<p class="jp-example">彼と話す前は、無口な人だと思っていました。</p>*
*Before talking to him, I thought he was the quiet type.*

This presents the opinion the speaker held before speaking directly with that person.

*<p class="jp-example">試合の前は、いつも落ち着きません。</p>*
*I can never relax before matches.*

**前は** identifies a recurring phase; the non-past main clause describes a habitual situation.

*<p class="jp-example">三年前は、まだこの漢字が読めませんでした。</p>*
*Three years ago, I still couldn't read this kanji.*

**三年前は** selects the situation three years ago as the topic of discussion.

*<p class="jp-example">明日の会議の前は、事務所にいる予定です。</p>*
*I plan to be in the office before tomorrow's meeting.*

The reference point lies in the future. The main clause describes where the speaker expects to be during the period leading up to the meeting.

*<p class="jp-example">新しい制度が導入される前は、申請の手続きがもっと複雑でした。</p>*
*Before the new system was introduced, the application process was more complicated.*

The construction describes an earlier condition and, through **もっと**, compares it with the later one.

## 前から (mae kara)

### Meaning and Usage

In its temporal uses, **前から (mae kara)** means **“since before,”** **“for some time,”** or **“starting from an earlier point.”** The construction emphasizes that a situation originated before the reference point in question: something already existed, was known, was desired, or was being done at an earlier stage.

The element **前** identifies that earlier time, while the particle **から** introduces a starting point in time. The focus is therefore on the fact that the situation **goes back to an earlier point** and, in many uses, has continued up to the time being discussed.

*<p class="jp-example">前から、この作家の小説が好きです。</p>*
*I've liked this author's novels for a long time.*

The speaker expresses a preference that already existed earlier and is still present. The sentence does not specify when the preference began: **前から can indicate an earlier starting point without identifying its exact date**.

When a specific event is mentioned, **前から** indicates that the situation was already in place before that event:

*<p class="jp-example">この会社に入る前から、彼とは知り合いでした。</p>*
*He and I already knew each other before I joined this company.*

Joining the company is the reference point relative to which the relationship began earlier. The two people already knew each other before then; the sentence does not say exactly when they first met.

**Continuity can involve either a state or a habitual activity.** Knowing something, being acquainted with someone, having an interest, and wanting an object are situations that can persist over time. Studying a language or regularly visiting a particular establishment, on the other hand, may be long-standing habits: in such cases, continuity does not mean that the activity takes place without interruption.

The point up to which the situation is considered depends on the predicate and the context. With a form such as **～ています**, the connection often extends to the present; with **～ていました**, it is usually viewed from a point in the past. **前から does not itself determine when the situation ends.**

Past forms expressing desires also require care. A sentence meaning “I'd been wanting to read it for some time” might be uttered while receiving a book: it presents a desire that developed earlier, without automatically implying that the desire has disappeared. Whether it has been fulfilled depends on the situation.

**When a precise time interval is given**, the starting point can be identified more clearly. **三日前から** means “for the past three days,” with the present as the reference point; **会議の一時間前から** means “starting one hour before the meeting.”

The latter use may also refer to a future plan: for example, an activity can be scheduled to begin thirty minutes before an event that has yet to take place. The temporal reference point preceded by **前** therefore does not necessarily coincide with the present.

Finally, **前から does not always mean “for a very long time.”** Expressions such as **少し前から**, “since fairly recently,” and **ずっと前から**, “for a very long time,” allow the time interval suggested by the context to be specified or emphasized.

**After a verb, use the affirmative dictionary form**, without inserting **の**. This form remains unchanged even when the main clause describes a past situation:

*<p class="jp-example">日本に引っ越す前から、この仕事をしていました。</p>*
*I was doing this job even before moving to Japan.*

The move has already taken place, but the form before **前から** is **引っ越す**. The form **していました**, meanwhile, presents the work activity from the perspective of a past narrative.

In the basic temporal construction, forms such as **引っ越す前から**, **入学する前から**, and **有名になる前から** are therefore correct. **× 引っ越した前から**, **× 引っ越します前から**, and **× 引っ越すの前から** should be avoided.

When the reference point is a change of state, it must be expressed through the appropriate verb: **有名になる前から** means “since before becoming famous”; **忙しくなる前から** means “since before becoming busy.”

**After a noun denoting an event or activity, insert の**: **入学の前から**, **開店の前から**, **旅行の前から**. The noun establishes the reference point before which the starting point is located.

**Do not insert の after an amount of time**: **三日前から**, **二週間前から**, **半年前から**. To say “for the past three days,” the construction is therefore **三日前から**, without **の** between **三日** and **前**.

When an event is combined with a precise time interval, **の** instead links the event noun to the rest of the expression: **出発の二時間前から**, “starting two hours before departure.” With a reference point expressed by a verb, use **出発する二時間前から**.

**前から does not require a single fixed predicate form.** **～ています** and **～ていました** are common because they can present activities or states maintained over time. However, forms such as **好きです**, **苦手です**, **欲しかったです**, and **知り合いでした** can also be used, depending on the intended meaning.

For example, with **知る**, the state of knowing is normally expressed by **知っている**: **前から知っています** means “I've known it since before” or “I've known about it for some time.” The choice of predicate therefore also reflects the characteristics of the individual verb.

When the beginning of an activity is specified, predicates such as **始まります** can also occur: the temporal expression identifies the point from which the activity begins.

As for position, **前から** and expressions built around it normally precede the predicate whose temporal starting point they specify. They can appear at the beginning of the sentence or after the topic: **三年前から、私は…** and **私は三年前から…** express the same starting point with a different arrangement of the information.

It is also possible to modify a noun using **前からの + noun**: **前からの知り合い** refers to someone one has known since an earlier time; **前からの約束** refers to an arrangement or commitment made previously. In this construction, **の** is added after **から** to link the temporal expression to the following noun.

### Register

**前から is neutral in register** and can be used in both spoken and written Japanese. It is common in personal accounts, explanations of habits and preferences, references to prior knowledge, and informative communication.

In conversation, it can also introduce a question the speaker has wanted to ask for some time:

*<p class="jp-example">前から聞きたかったんだけど、この名前はどう読むの？</p>*
*I've been meaning to ask you: how do you pronounce this name?*

The wording **聞きたかったんだけど** and the final question create a colloquial tone. Referring to an earlier desire prepares the listener for the question.

The same temporal expression can also appear in a professional communication:

*<p class="jp-example">この件については、かなり前から検討しております。</p>*
*We've been considering this matter for quite some time.*

Here, the vocabulary and the polite form **検討しております** make the sentence appropriate for a formal context. **前から** retains its temporal meaning without determining the level of politeness by itself.

Even before **前から**, the dictionary form of the verb remains correct in polite sentences: the register of the main clause does not require changing it into a **ます** form.

### Examples

*<p class="jp-example">前から、この町に住んでいます。</p>*
*I've been living in this town for some time now.*

The person began living there at an earlier point and still lives there; the starting date remains unspecified.

*<p class="jp-example">前から、この映画を見たいと思っていました。</p>*
*I've been wanting to see this film for some time.*

A desire that developed earlier is being recalled. Whether it has been fulfilled depends on the context.

*<p class="jp-example">彼とは、大学に入る前からの知り合いです。</p>*
*I've known him since before I started university.*

**前からの** modifies the noun **知り合い**, presenting the relationship as predating the start of university.

*<p class="jp-example">結婚する前から、二人は同じ町に住んでいました。</p>*
*The two of them were already living in the same town before they got married.*

Living in the same town was already a reality before their marriage.

*<p class="jp-example">彼が入社する前から、私はこの部署で働いています。</p>*
*I've worked in this department since before he joined the company.*

The subject of the reference event differs from that of the main activity, which continues in the present.

*<p class="jp-example">三日前から、毎朝この公園を走っています。</p>*
*I've been running in this park every morning for the past three days.*

The starting point is precise, and the continuity concerns a habit repeated each morning.

*<p class="jp-example">少し前から、パソコンの調子がおかしいです。</p>*
*My computer hasn't been working properly for a little while.*

**少し前から** indicates a relatively recent starting point; the predicate describes a current condition.

*<p class="jp-example">開店の一時間前から、店の前に人が並んでいました。</p>*
*People were already lining up outside the shop an hour before it opened.*

The reference point is the opening, and the start of the queue is placed one hour earlier.

*<p class="jp-example">受付は、説明会の三十分前から始まります。</p>*
*Registration will begin thirty minutes before the information session.*

The sentence outlines a schedule: **前から** identifies the point from which registration will begin.

*<p class="jp-example">その店が有名になる前から、よく通っていました。</p>*
*I used to go to that place regularly, even before it became famous.*

The habit already existed before the change expressed by **有名になる**.

## Comparison and Differences

### Sequence vs. Point in Time

The difference between **前に**, **前は**, and **前から** lies in how the earlier time is presented: **locating an event**, **describing a period**, or **indicating a starting point in time**.

The reference point can be the same, but the particle changes the relationship established with the rest of the sentence.

This difference can be seen in three sentences about the work history of someone who is now married:

*<p class="jp-example">結婚する前に、この会社で働き始めました。</p>*
*I started working at this company before getting married.*

**前に** places the start of the job before the marriage. The relationship is chronological: the work began first, and the marriage took place afterward. The sentence does not specify whether the person continued working there after getting married.

*<p class="jp-example">結婚する前は、この会社で働いていました。</p>*
*Before getting married, I worked at this company.*

**前は** presents the period before marriage and describes the person's employment situation during that phase. A possible contrast with the later period is easy to infer, but whether anything actually changed must be established from the context.

*<p class="jp-example">結婚する前から、この会社で働いています。</p>*
*I've been working at this company since before I got married.*

**前から**, together with **働いています**, presents the job as an activity that began before the marriage and continues today. The marriage serves to place the beginning of the job earlier than that event, without necessarily identifying the exact starting date.

The choice therefore depends on more than the position of events on a timeline. What also matters is **which information about that time is being conveyed**: a beginning placed earlier, a situation characteristic of the preceding period, or an activity that started earlier and is still ongoing.

**Note:** Another useful comparison is with **～時 (とき)**. While **前に** explicitly indicates that one event occurs before a given point in time, **～時** identifies the time or circumstance in which something happens, without necessarily establishing the same relationship of precedence.

For example, in **家を出る前に、鍵を確認しました** (before leaving the house), the person checks their keys before leaving. In **家を出る時、鍵を確認しました** (when leaving the house, that is, just before stepping outside), by contrast, checking the keys is associated with the moment the person is about to leave.

The two constructions can therefore describe much the same situation, but from different perspectives.

In a sense, 時 acts as the pivot between 前 (before) and 後 (after).

The role of the verb form and the nuances it creates are explored in greater detail in the article [～時 (toki): Meaning and Correct Usage](/en/articles/toki-meaning-usage-japanese-grammar).



### Grammatical Constraints

The three constructions share the main patterns for connecting an element to **前**: **a verb in the dictionary form**, **an event or activity noun followed by の**, or **an amount of time without の**.

We therefore find forms such as **出発する前に／前は／前から**, **出発の前に／前は／前から**, and **三年前に／前は／前から**.

In the basic temporal construction, **the verb before 前 remains in the affirmative dictionary form even when the main clause is in the past tense**. The differences between the three particles instead emerge in their relationship with the following predicate.

**前に** normally allows an action to be placed before a reference point: checking something, making a phone call, buying something, or starting an activity. It can also refer back to an earlier experience or locate an extended activity in time. The particle does not automatically make the event brief or instantaneous.

**前は**, when introducing the period before an event, is particularly well suited to describing **states, circumstances, habits, and ongoing activities** during that period. The predicate must be consistent with this descriptive function:

*<p class="jp-example">発表の前は、とても緊張していました。</p>*
*I was very nervous before the presentation.*

Here, the speaker's state during the period leading up to the presentation is being described.

This construction must be distinguished from **前には**, in which **は** is added to the expression with **に**:

*<p class="jp-example">発表の前には、資料を全員に配りました。</p>*
*I handed out the materials to everyone before the presentation.*

**には** retains the temporal placement of the action while presenting it in a contrastive or particularly prominent frame. **前は** and **前には** are therefore not automatically interchangeable.

With **前から**, the predicate often presents a state or activity maintained over time. **～ている** and **～ていた** are common, but the construction can also accompany predicates such as **好きだ**, **欲しかった**, and **知り合いだ**. It can also indicate the point at which a scheduled activity begins.

The predicate form must therefore be chosen alongside the particle: **～ていた is not mandatory with 前は**, and **～ている is not mandatory with 前から**.

### Time and Interpretation

To interpret these expressions, we must distinguish **the moment of speaking**, **the reference point before which something is placed**, and **the point in time from which the situation is viewed**.

With **前に**, the relationship of anteriority can concern either the past or the future:

*<p class="jp-example">昨日、出発する前に、ホテルに連絡しました。</p>*
*Yesterday, I contacted the hotel before leaving.*

*<p class="jp-example">明日、出発する前に、ホテルに連絡します。</p>*
*Tomorrow, I'll contact the hotel before leaving.*

**出発する前に** remains unchanged. What changes are the time expressions and the form of the main clause; in both cases, contacting the hotel precedes departure.

**前は** can likewise describe a habitual situation rather than referring exclusively to a past period:

*<p class="jp-example">試験の前は、いつも落ち着きません。</p>*
*I'm always restless before exams.*

The sentence describes a recurring condition during the period leading up to exams.

With **前から**, the predicate form helps clarify how far forward the situation is being considered. Alongside the earlier example with **働いています**, we can consider:

*<p class="jp-example">結婚する前から、この会社で働いていました。</p>*
*I was working at this company even before I got married.*

The work is presented from the perspective of a past narrative. The sentence does not guarantee that the person still works there today, but neither does it automatically state that the employment has ended: what happened afterward must be inferred from the context.

A starting point expressed by **前から** can also be part of a future schedule:

*<p class="jp-example">受付は、会議の三十分前から始まります。</p>*
*Registration will begin thirty minutes before the meeting.*

The reference point is the meeting; registration is scheduled to start thirty minutes earlier. Here, **から** indicates a precise starting point, while **始まります** expresses the beginning of the activity.

The difference is also clear in expressions that contain an amount of time. Taking the present as the reference point:

The presence of **前** does not, by itself, determine either duration or the actual time interval. **前に** can refer to something that happened a long time ago; **前から** can also indicate a recent starting point, as in **少し前から**, “since fairly recently.”

### Register

**前に**, **前は**, and **前から** are neutral in register and can appear in both speech and writing. The choice between them depends primarily on the intended temporal and discourse function.

**前に** is common in descriptions of actions, plans, and instructions. **前は** occurs naturally in recollections, comparisons between periods, and explanations of change. **前から** is frequent when discussing habits, knowledge, desires, or activities already established earlier, but it also appears in notices specifying when something begins.

These associations do not represent a hierarchy of politeness. **前は** can describe an earlier procedure in a professional report; **前から** can appear in institutional communication; and **前に** is equally natural in a conversation between family members.

The overall tone depends on the vocabulary, sentence endings, and any honorific forms used. The dictionary form before **前** remains correct even in polite sentences: it is not replaced with a **ます** form merely to match the main clause.

### Common Mistakes

**Carrying the past tense of the main clause over to the verb before 前.** In the basic temporal construction, a reference expressed by a verb requires the dictionary form even when the narrative is in the past.

**Inserting の after a verb or an amount of time.** Use **行く前に**, without **の** after **行く**; to express “for three years,” use **三年前から**, without **の** after **三年**. The particle does appear in the basic noun construction, as in **試験の前から**.

**Confusing “three years ago” with “for three years.”** **三年前に** locates an event, while **三年前から** indicates its temporal starting point. **三年前は**, by contrast, makes the circumstances at that time the topic. The three expressions do not become equivalent merely because their translations may all involve a general idea of “before.”

**Interpreting 前は as necessarily meaning that something has stopped.** The implied contrast may concern frequency, manner, or other circumstances. An activity described with **前は** may still continue today, even if it is performed differently.

**Reading 前から as “from before until today” in every sentence.** The point from which continuity is observed can also lie in the past. Moreover, the continuity of a habit does not imply that the activity is performed without interruption.

**Changing the particle without considering the meaning of the verb.** The contrast between **知る** and **知っている** shows why the predicate form must also be chosen carefully:

*<p class="jp-example">その事実は、発表の前に知りました。</p>*
*I learned that fact before the announcement.*

*<p class="jp-example">その事実は、発表の前から知っていました。</p>*
*I already knew that fact before the announcement.*

In the first sentence, **知りました** presents the acquisition of the information, and **前に** places it before the announcement. In the second, **知っていました** describes the state of knowing, while **前から** emphasizes that this knowledge already existed earlier. The difference arises from the interaction between the particle and the predicate.

## Conclusions

Choosing between **前に**, **前は**, and **前から** becomes easier and more deliberate when we begin with the relationship we want to express: placing an event before a reference point, describing the circumstances of an earlier period, or emphasizing an earlier starting point. The contribution of each particle must be considered together with the predicate and the context, because the temporal perspective and the information being highlighted emerge from the sentence as a whole.

A useful principle when reviewing a sentence is to distinguish **whether the construction is grammatically correct** from **whether it conveys the intended meaning**. A sentence may be grammatically possible yet communicate something different from what was originally intended. Therefore, alongside checking the form used before 前, it is important to examine what the main clause actually asserts and what information is left implicit.

The expressions can also appear together in the same sentence, performing complementary functions:

*<p class="jp-example">前は、寝る前に本を読んでいました。</p>*
*I used to read a book before going to bed.*

**前は** frames a former habit; **寝る前に** places the reading in relation to bedtime. The two constructions organize different layers of the same experience: the period of life being discussed and the sequence of activities within that period.

For learners, it is therefore useful to pair the rules with **complete sentences set in recognizable situations**. 

In study notes, a brief indication of context can make the expression's function clearer: a memory contrasted with the present, preparation before an event, or an interest that already existed before a particular experience. The English translation then serves as an aid to understanding rather than becoming the only criterion for choosing an expression.

One practical exercise is to describe brief personal experiences with different communicative aims. Describing an old school habit, placing an action before a lesson, and discussing an interest that began earlier provide three distinct opportunities to use these forms. 

Moving from one intended meaning to another may also require changing the predicate: the exercise concerns constructing the meaning of the entire sentence.

When reading and listening, the sentences immediately before and after the expression also deserve attention. They often clarify the contrast suggested by 前は, identify the implicit reference point of 前に, or reveal up to which point in time a situation introduced by 前から is being considered. 

Preserving this context in revision examples helps learners recognize nuances that an isolated sentence leaves unresolved.

Mastery of these forms develops through the combination of **understanding the rules, observing actual usage, and producing meaningful statements**. The result is an ability to present an experience from the appropriate temporal perspective and to recognize that perspective in other speakers' language.
