**What does 頂戴 (ちょうだい, choudai) mean in Japanese, how do you use it, and how does it differ from ください (kudasai)?**

In modern Japanese, 頂戴 (choudai) primarily has the following meanings: 

- **“to receive” humbly**. In this sense, it functions as a respectful expression close to いただく and as the humble equivalent of もらう.
-   **a “direct but familiar request”**, with the sense of “give me / please give me” or “please do ... for me.” Dictionaries clearly distinguish these two uses: それをちょうだい means “give me that,” whereas 取ってちょうだい means “get it for me, please.” In this second use, 頂戴 no longer expresses humility in the strict sense of a verb of receiving, but rather a request colored by familiarity. 


Regarding the first use, dictionaries also note that it can refer to **receiving and consuming food or drink**, that is, *“eating/drinking”* in a humble register. It is no coincidence that, among the humble synonyms of もらう, dictionaries list 頂く, 賜る, 頂戴する, 拝領する, and 拝受する. 


As for the second use, dictionaries clearly distinguish two patterns: それをちょうだい means *“give me that,”* whereas 取ってちょうだい means *“get it/pass it to me, please.”* In this second area of usage, 頂戴 no longer expresses humility in the strict sense of a verb of receiving, but rather a **request with a nuance of familiarity**.

In the sections that follow, we will first summarize its general characteristics; we will then examine its origins before moving on to the more practical part of the article: its grammatical description, the main formation rules, and comparisons with similar forms. We will look at tone and register, and then conclude with the usual usage examples and final observations.

## 頂戴 (ちょうだい, choudai): Meaning and Usage in Japanese

頂戴 (ちょうだい) is a very useful word, but also a somewhat misleading one. On closer inspection, it is not simply *“a verb”* in the ordinary sense of the term: modern Japanese dictionaries classify it first and foremost as **a noun that can combine with する**, giving 頂戴する with the meaning *“to receive humbly”*; yet the same form is also used as a **colloquial request** (*“give me, please”*) and in the pattern 〜てちょうだい. 

For that reason, in practice it is best studied as a word with several functions. In **semantic and functional terms**, its closest modern counterpart is 頂く／戴く（いただく）, the most common humble verb corresponding to もらう. The two words have different lexical origins, however: いただく is an old native Japanese verb, whereas 頂戴 belongs to the Sino-Japanese lexicon.

The most important point to understand from the outset is this: **頂戴 brings together two very different worlds**. On the one hand, it is a humble, respectful word used to mean *“to receive”*; on the other, in forms such as それちょうだい or 取ってちょうだい, it is a *“friendly, familiar request.”* The two functions are historically related, but in modern Japanese they belong to very different registers. 

To summarize, from a functional point of view, three main modern uses can be distinguished: 頂戴する *“to receive humbly,”* ちょうだい after a noun functioning as a request, and 〜てちょうだい / 〜ないでちょうだい as a request pattern attached to a verb. This distribution is the simplest and most accurate way to understand how the word functions today.

---



## Origins and historical development 
 The word has a very concrete origin. The first element, 頂, denotes the highest part, the *“top”* or *“summit,”* but also the **crown of the head**; the second, 戴, means *“to carry on one’s head,”* *“to raise respectfully,”* and by extension *“to receive with gratitude.”* In Japanese lexicography, the compound 頂戴 is explicitly treated as **a word of Buddhist origin**. 

Its original semantic core is not *“to receive”* in the abstract, but **to raise something above one’s head as a sign of the utmost respect**. 

In Japanese, the earliest attestation recorded in major dictionaries dates to around 835, in a still-literal and solemn use meaning *“to raise respectfully”* or *“to hold above one’s head.”* 

Only later did the word acquire the meaning of **“to receive” humbly**: historical dictionaries place this development at least as far back as the late Muromachi period and the beginning of the early modern era, and later record **a specific use for food and drink in the eighteenth century**. 

A colloquial request function subsequently emerged as well: this use probably developed from shortened expressions such as 頂戴つかまつる, accompanied by the gesture of holding out one’s hand to receive something. From there arose **the modern ちょうだい *“give me / please”***. 

It is useful to compare this history with that of いただく. The native verb 頂く／戴く is much older: it is already attested in the eighth-century Man’yōshū with the meaning *“to place on one’s head,”* then develops the sense *“to receive humbly,”* later “to eat/drink” humbly, and finally also develops an **auxiliary** use in constructions such as 〜ていただく. 

In other words, modern Japanese has two closely related families: an older, native one centered on いただく, and a later Sino-Japanese one centered on 頂戴. The two families converge because both derive from the same cultural image: **receiving something by raising it respectfully**.

## Grammatical description and formation rules

The first rule to remember is this: in modern grammar, 頂戴 **is fundamentally a noun that combines with する**. The basic form is therefore 頂戴する, which then conjugates normally: 頂戴します, 頂戴した, 頂戴しない, 頂戴したい, 頂戴できますか, and so on. 

With this use, what is received is usually expressed as the object marked by を: for example, *“a gift,”* *“a word,”* *“a reprimand,”* or *“food.”* Dictionaries accordingly give examples such as 結構な品を頂戴いたしました, お叱りを頂戴する, and もう十分頂戴しました. 

The second rule concerns its use as a request after a noun. In this case, ちょうだい does not need する and follows the thing one wishes to obtain: それちょうだい, 牛肉五百グラムちょうだい. Here the word functions much like English *“give me”* or *“could I have...,”* but with a more colloquial and affectionate nuance. 

The third rule concerns its use with verbs. There are two typical attachment patterns: **the て-form + ちょうだい** and **the negative ないで / んで form + ちょうだい**. This gives patterns such as **取ってちょうだい (get it/pass it to me, please), 見てちょうだい (look!), 泣かないでちょうだい (don’t cry)**. 

This is one of the most important points, because it shows that 頂戴 as a *“request”* is a productive grammatical use, not merely a fixed formula. 

To understand how the system works as a whole, 頂戴 must then be compared with いただく. Japan’s Agency for Cultural Affairs explains that いただく is a form of **humble language** that, in addition to placing the speaker in a humble position, often conveys the idea of **receiving a benefit**. 

For this reason, sentences such as 先生に指導していただく mean not only *“to receive instruction,”* but also *“to receive it as a favor/benefit.”* In the same framework, official Japanese guidance gives constructions such as 御指導いただく and 御案内いただく, while regarding forms such as 御案内していただく as problematic when the structure directs the respect toward the wrong target. 

Another useful difference is the following: according to **NINJAL** (*National Institute for Japanese Language and Linguistics*), お／ご〜ください can be used to **directly prompt** the other person to act, whereas お／ご〜いただく cannot be directed at the listener in the same way as a direct request, because it instead describes **receiving** that action as a benefit. 

For example, ご入場ください is a direct invitation; ご入場いただく instead frames the audience’s act of entering as a benefit received by the speaker’s side, so the point of view changes.

Finally, there is a very useful practical writing rule. In official Japanese prose, when **いただく** is used as an **auxiliary**, it is written in **hiragana**, not kanji: for example, 〜ていただく rather than 〜て頂く. 

The Agency for Cultural Affairs explicitly lists this among the cases in which, even though a kanji spelling exists, the phonetic spelling is preferred for the auxiliary use. This point primarily concerns いただく, but it is essential for avoiding confusion between the independent verb *“to receive”* and the benefactive auxiliary. 

In the next two sections, we will look more closely at its use as a humble verb and its colloquial use in direct requests.

## Use as a humble verb (謙譲語) equivalent to もらう 
In **formal or business contexts**, 頂戴 is used as a humble verb (謙譲語) equivalent to もらう (*“to receive”*). It is used when the speaker wishes to show deference toward the person giving them something, typically a superior, a customer, or a respected outsider. 

In this sense, 頂戴する, as we have seen, is close to the humble verb いただく, but often has a **stiffer, more lexical, and sometimes more ceremonial feel** in formal contexts. For example, in a business meeting or a formal email, one might say:

- 「貴重なお時間を頂戴し、ありがとうございました。」 – Thank you very much for taking the time, or 「お客様からご意見を頂戴しました。」 – We received valuable feedback from our customers.


In these sentences, 頂戴しました/頂戴し… is a humble way of saying *“I/we received...”*, emphasizing respect toward the person who gave the time, opinion, and so on. 

Note that it is often combined with even more deferential forms such as 頂戴いたしました, adding the humble verb 致す (itasu) for an additional level of politeness.

For example: 「素晴らしいお品を頂戴いたしました」. This usage is appropriate in formal letters, business emails, official speeches, and similar contexts.

### Comparison with もらう

Whereas もらう simply means *“to receive”* (neutral, plain), 頂戴する is a **humble equivalent**.  

For example:

- 「友達に誕生日プレゼントをもらった。」 – I received a birthday present from a friend.
- 「社長に記念品を頂戴しました。」 – I received a commemorative gift from the company president (humble form).



In the first case, the neutral verb もらう is used, which is suitable for informal contexts; in the second, because the giver is the company president (a superior), 頂戴しました is preferred to show respect.

## Colloquial use in direct requests (〜てちょうだい) 
Colloquially, ちょうだい is used, especially in the form 「〜てちょうだい」, to **make direct requests in a familiar way**, roughly equivalent to an informal, familiar *“please do ~.”*

This use is very **informal** and common in spoken language **within the family, among close friends, or in children’s speech**, often with an affectionate or pleading tone. 

For example:

- 「ちょっと手伝ってちょうだい。」 – Help me for a moment, please.
- 「その新聞を取ってちょうだい。」 – Pass me that newspaper, please.


In these sentences, 〜てちょうだい corresponds to *“~てください”* (the standard polite form), but in an **informal register**. 

The use of ちょうだい in direct requests is **traditionally associated especially with feminine or childlike speech, as dictionaries still note**. 

This is not, however, a grammatical restriction based on gender: actual usage depends on the relationship between the speakers, the tone, and the social persona the speaker wishes to project. 

Forms such as 〜てくれ are more direct and rougher and have themselves traditionally been associated with masculine speech.

For example, a child might say 「お母さん、お菓子ちょうだい！」 – *“Mom, can I have some candy?”* An adult can also use ちょうだい in a sufficiently familiar relationship; when greater politeness toward the listener is appropriate, however, a form such as 「お菓子をください」 is more suitable.

We have seen that, in addition to ～てちょうだい, the pattern 名詞 (noun) + ちょうだい is also used colloquially with the meaning “give me...”. 

Examples:

- 「それ、ちょうだい！」 – Come on, give me that!
- 「お水ちょうだい。」 – Pass me the water, will you?

These expressions are the informal equivalents of 「それをください」 or 「お水をください」. Using them implies a high degree of familiarity: 「お水ちょうだい」 said to a waiter would sound too casual/familiar (「お水をください」 would be better in that situation). In a friendly or family setting, however, ちょうだい makes the request more direct and colloquial, often with a **familiar, sometimes coaxing tone**.

## Register and contexts of use

In its humble use, 頂戴 belongs to a **formal and deferential register**. It is natural with concrete objects, but also with non-material things such as words, reprimands, advice, and someone’s attention. Dictionaries give both material objects and examples such as お叱りを頂戴する; this is an important clue, because it shows that the word is not limited to *“things”* in the strict sense, but can extend to what is received socially or symbolically. 

In its use as a request, by contrast, the register changes completely. Modern dictionaries explicitly describe it as **mostly feminine and childlike**, and the historical dictionary adds that in contemporary language this value is used mainly **by women and children**. 

In workplace, customer-service, or institutional communication, forms such as 〜てちょうだい are generally not the best choice: the Agency for Cultural Affairs often recommends more indirect formulas that leave the listener greater room to decide, such as 〜していただけますか, お願いできますか, and お願いしてもいいですか. 

There is also a well-known caution concerning usage. The Digital Daijisen notes that お名前を頂戴できますか is often heard on the telephone and in customer service, but regards this wording as inappropriate. The dictionary gives a specific reason: 名前を頂戴する traditionally means to receive someone’s name, or part of it, for use as one’s own name or that of one’s child. 

To simply ask for a person’s name, it therefore recommends expressions such as お名前をお願いします or お名前をお聞き（お伺い）できますか.

This caution should not, however, be turned into a general rule against using 頂戴 with abstract content. **頂戴する can naturally refer to opinions, advice, reprimands, someone’s time, and other non-material things**, as in ご意見を頂戴する, ご助言を頂戴する, and お時間を頂戴する. What matters is the particular lexical combination; お名前を頂戴できますか is a specific case that calls for caution.

## Comparison with similar forms


頂戴 (ちょうだい), ください, and もらう differ in register and stylistic use. Here is a clear comparison:
- 頂戴 (in an informal request context): A **colloquial** form used to make direct, familiar requests between people who are close. It sounds **feminine or childlike** and conveys familiarity. Example: 「それ頂戴」 – Give me that. It is not used in formal situations because it is too casual. By contrast, 頂戴する (with する) belongs to the opposite register: it is a formal humble expression and means “to receive,” as explained above. 
- ください (kudasai): The standard polite form for asking for something. It is used after the て-form of a verb (〜てください) to mean “please do ~,” or after an object marked by を (〜をください) to ask to receive something. ください derives from the honorific verb 下さる, which respectfully presents someone’s act of giving something to the speaker or the speaker’s in-group and is grammatically a **polite imperative**. Example: 「これをください。」 – “Please give me this.” / 「ちょっと待ってください。」 – “Please wait a moment.”   It is suitable for most formal and informal situations (it gives the request a respectful tone without sounding excessively elaborate). 
- もらう (morau): A neutral verb meaning “to receive.” It is not in itself a politeness form (it is neither honorific nor humble). It is not used to directly ask for something (you do not say 「これをもらう」 to mean “give me this”), unless it is embedded in conditional or potential constructions (「〜てもらえますか」, discussed below). Instead, it is used to describe the act of receiving: 「田中さんに本をもらった。」 – “I received a book from Tanaka.”  In more deferential registers, もらう is often replaced by いただく or 頂戴する (humble) when the recipient places themself in a lower position relative to the giver.

In short, ください is the appropriate word for saying *“please”* in a generally polite way, 〜てちょうだい is an informal request form used mainly in familiar contexts, whereas もらう expresses receiving (not a direct request, except in special constructions) and is replaced by 頂戴/いただく in formal humble language.

## Grammatical use in requests: Vて + ちょうだい vs 〜てください vs 〜てもらう

Japanese has several constructions for obtaining something or asking someone to do something for us. Let us examine Vて + 頂戴, 〜てください, and 〜てもらう from the standpoint of grammar and nuance:

- 「Vて + 頂戴」 – As we have seen, this is a colloquial imperative equivalent to “~てください,” but more informal and familiar. 

	Grammatically, **in these cases ちょうだい follows the -te form of the main verb and is used in an auxiliary-like request function**. For example, 「手伝ってちょうだい」 combines the action 手伝う (to help) with ちょうだい to form **“Help me, please (in a familiar context)”**. 

	The verb itself does not change form (it remains in the -te form), and ちょうだい remains invariable. This construction **should not** be used in formal contexts; it is limited to colloquial language. It is also often associated with the gentle, intimate tone used by women or children, as noted above. The form is traditionally associated especially with feminine and childlike speech, but it is not grammatically restricted to those speakers; actual choice depends on context, relationship, and speaking style.

  A man might use it ironically or occasionally, but in familiar speech he will more often prefer 「〜てくれ」. 
- 「〜てください」 – This is the standard form for a **polite request**. It is built with the -te form + the verb 下さい (kudasai). Example: 「これを見てください。」 – Please look at this. 

        It is a standard polite request form used in both speech and writing. Because it remains relatively direct, however, its appropriateness depends on the relationship with the listener and on the situation.   From a grammatical standpoint, 下さい is often written in hiragana (ください) when used as a polite expression. Unlike ちょうだい, **ください is gender-neutral** and is taught to learners from the beginning as the main form for making polite requests. 
- 「〜てもらう」 – This construction is not an imperative, but describes **an action received as a favor** from someone. 

	It is formed with the -te form + もらう (to receive) and literally means **“to receive the favor of having someone do X”**. For example: 「田中さんに手伝ってもらった。」 – “I had Tanaka help me,” literally: I received the favor (or benefit) of Tanaka helping me. Here the speaker is not saying “Tanaka, help me,” but is describing after the fact that Tanaka helped, whether by request or as a favor. However, 〜てもらう also appears in interrogative sentences to ask for something politely and indirectly: 「ちょっと手伝ってもらえますか？」 – “Could you help me for a moment?” 

In this case, we have the potential form of もらう (もらえる→もらえます) in a question, implying “can I receive the favor of your doing ~?” It is more deferential than a direct 「〜てください」 and is often used when asking for help from someone who is not subordinate to the speaker (e.g. a colleague or stranger), with a more considerate tone. 
### Practical comparison

Suppose you want someone to open the door. Here are three ways to say it, with different nuances:
- 「ドアを開けてちょうだい。」 – Said, for example, to a family member in an informal tone – “Open the door for me, will you?” – Register: informal, familiar. 
- 「ドアを開けてください。」 – Said politely to someone – “Please open the door.” – Register: standard polite. 
- 「ドアを開けてもらえますか。」 – Asked politely and indirectly – “Could you open the door for me?” – Register: polite and indirect, with a more considerate tone.

The practical result (getting someone to open the door) is the same, but the choice between ちょうだい/ください/もらえますか reflects different contexts and relationships: the first is familiar; the second is generally polite; the third is even more considerate, framing the action as a favor being requested.

From a grammatical standpoint, 頂戴 as an auxiliary is not used in constructions describing a favor that has been received (that is, we do not use 「〜して頂戴した」). In such cases, 〜してもらった or its humble form 〜していただいた is used. This request use of ちょうだい is limited to the direct request itself, whereas もらう/いただく are used to describe the favor or to request it indirectly.

## Conclusions

To understand **頂戴 (ちょうだい, choudai)**, it is necessary to consider **meaning, grammatical construction, and register together**. In **頂戴する**, the word expresses receiving in a humble manner, showing respect toward the person who gives. 

After a noun or in the forms **～てちょうだい / ～ないでちょうだい**, by contrast, it functions as a direct, familiar request: asking for something, asking someone to perform an action, or asking them to refrain from doing something.

Comparing it with **もらう, いただく, and ください** helps clarify the appropriate role and context for each form. もらう expresses receiving without inherently marking deference toward the giver; いただく and 頂戴する are its humble equivalents, although they differ in construction and usage. 

In requests, ちょうだい conveys familiarity, whereas ください provides a more neutral polite formulation. Interrogative forms such as ～ていただけますか, meanwhile, allow the speaker to request a favor indirectly.

The choice therefore depends both on register and on how the listener’s action is presented.

The history of 頂戴 helps explain the connection between these uses: the gesture of respectfully raising something one received gave rise to the meaning “to receive humbly,” and the later request function emerged from the same historical development.

**The respectful origin of the word does not, however, automatically make a request with ちょうだい polite**. In contemporary Japanese, its appropriateness depends on the construction, the situation, and the relationship with the other person. This attention to context is what allows learners to move from simply knowing the meaning to using the expression appropriately.
