What is the difference between 気分 (kibun), 調子 (choushi), 体調 (taichou), and 具合 (guai), and when should you use each one?


Japanese has several words that can express **"how someone feels"** or describe the condition of a person (or a thing), and distinguishing between them can be difficult.


In particular, **気分 (kibun), 調子 (chōshi), 体調 (taichō), and 具合 (guai)** each carry different nuances. 

In short, we can say that:


- 気分 (きぶん) → how someone feels emotionally or psychologically at a given moment (mood, state of mind, subjective feeling);

- 調子 (ちょうし) → the general state of functioning of a person or thing (how something is going, form, performance);

- 体調 (たいちょう) → physical condition and bodily health (state of health, physical condition);

- 具合 (ぐあい) → the concrete condition or situation as observed from the outside (condition, state, functioning, how things are). It is the result of the way the various elements “fit together.”




Below, we will examine these words, beginning with an analysis of the kanji they contain and their typical use in everyday language (including medical and health-related contexts), with examples in Japanese and English translations. 

Finally, we will compare them to see when they are interchangeable and when they are not.

## Differences Between Kibun, Choushi, Taichou, and Guai: When to Use Each One

The difficulty in distinguishing **気分 (きぶん), 調子 (ちょうし), 体調 (たいちょう), and 具合 (ぐあい)** comes from the fact that there is no one-to-one correspondence between these words and English terms such as *mood*, *condition*, *state*, *form*, or *how someone feels*.

In many situations, two or even three of these terms can refer to the same person at the same moment without meaning exactly the same thing. What changes is **which aspect of the condition the speaker chooses to focus on**.

The basic distinction can be summarized as follows: **気分 focuses on how a condition is perceived from within; 体調 narrows the focus to physical condition; 調子 focuses primarily on how a person or thing is functioning, progressing, or performing; 具合, by contrast, describes the concrete state something is in or the way a situation presents itself**.

For this reason, the question “how do you say *I feel well* or *I don’t feel well* in Japanese?” does not have a single answer.

Saying:

<p class="jp-example">今日は気分が悪い。</p>
*I feel unwell today / I don’t feel well today.*

frames the discomfort in terms of **the person’s subjective sensation**. 気分 therefore does not refer only to mood in the psychological sense: it can also describe a subjective physical sensation, such as feeling under the weather, nauseous, or simply “not well.”

With:

<p class="jp-example">今日は体調が悪い。</p>
*I’m not feeling well physically today.*

the perspective changes. **体調 directly identifies the condition of the body**: the focus is not so much on the momentary sensation experienced by the person as on their overall physical state. Fever, fatigue, feeling unwell, recovering from an illness, or simply having a day when the body is not in its usual condition all fall naturally within the scope of 体調.

Compare this with:

<p class="jp-example">今日は調子が悪い。</p>
*I’m not in good form today / I’m off today.*

Here, the problem need not be an illness. **調子 concerns the way something is functioning or progressing compared with its normal state**. A person can be in poor 調子 because they are tired, unable to concentrate, performing poorly in sports or at work, or because something in their body is not functioning as it normally does.

It is precisely this idea of **functioning and how things are going** that explains why 調子 can be used far beyond the realm of health:

<p class="jp-example">今日はエンジンの調子が悪い。</p>
*The engine isn’t running well today.*

<p class="jp-example">最近、仕事の調子がいい。</p>
*Work has been going well lately.*

The same extension is not possible with 体調, which contains 体 “body” and therefore remains tied to a person’s physical condition.

**具合**, by contrast, overlaps in various contexts with both 調子 and 体調, but introduces yet another perspective. It indicates **the state something is in, the way it presents itself, or the concrete result of a particular set of conditions**.

For example:

<p class="jp-example">膝の具合がよくない。</p>
*My knee isn’t doing very well.*

does not necessarily describe a subjective sensation as 気分 does, nor the person’s overall physical condition as 体調 does. The focus is **on the state of the knee itself**.

Likewise, 具合 can be used for objects, mechanisms, and situations, or to indicate how something has turned out or how far it has progressed:

<p class="jp-example">機械の具合を確認する。</p>
*Check the condition / operation of the machine.*

<p class="jp-example">焼け具合を見てください。</p>
*Check how well it’s cooked.*

Here an important difference from 調子 emerges. Both can describe the condition of a person or a machine, but **調子 tends to foreground functioning, rhythm, or how something is going**, whereas **具合 focuses more on the resulting state, the way something presents itself, or the degree or extent of that condition**.

This is not, however, an absolute division. Expressions such as:

<p class="jp-example">体の調子が悪い。</p>
*I’m not in good physical condition.*

<p class="jp-example">体の具合が悪い。</p>
*I don’t feel well.*

<p class="jp-example">体調が悪い。</p>
*I’m not feeling well physically.*

can describe very similar situations. The difference is not necessarily that one sentence is correct and another is wrong, but rather **how the speaker conceptualizes the same situation**.

For example, someone can be in good overall 体調 and yet have one part of the body whose 具合 is not perfect; conversely, someone may have no real health problem and still say that their 調子 is poor because they are performing below their usual level.

気分 can also overlap with the other terms when the problem is physical. A person suffering from nausea may say 気分が悪い because they are describing **the sensation of discomfort they are experiencing**, whereas with 体調が悪い they would present the same situation as part of their **overall physical condition**.

The four words, then, are not simply four synonyms for “condition.” We can think of their differences as four different questions:

**気分** → *How do I feel?*
**体調** → *What condition is my body in?*
**調子** → *How am I functioning / how am I doing?*
**具合** → *What state is this person, this body part, this thing, or this situation actually in?*

It is this difference in perspective that determines which word sounds most natural in a given context. In the following sections, we will therefore examine **気分, 調子, 体調, and 具合** separately, starting with the kanji that make them up and looking at how their basic meanings extend into everyday usage.

## 気分 (state of mind, mood, or general feeling)

### Kanji analysis 
 
- 気 (ki) means spirit, energy, air. It represents something immaterial, such as mood or vital energy. 
 
- 分 (bun) means part, portion. 

Together, 気分 can be interpreted as *“a part of the spirit,”* that is, a person’s **inner state**. It therefore indicates **the feeling or mood experienced at a given moment**, or, more generally, **one’s overall physical feeling**.

### Meaning and usage 

気分 is used above all to **express how someone feels emotionally** (e.g. being in a good or bad mood), but it can also describe feeling physically well or unwell in a general sense (not with reference to a specific part of the body). 

For example, 気分がいい usually means being in a good mood or feeling good in general, whereas 気分が悪い can mean feeling physically unwell (often with nausea or dizziness) or being in a bad mood, depending on the context. 

Importantly, 気分 refers to **the person’s overall perceived state, not to an isolated part of the body**. It is therefore not used to say that a specific body part hurts: localized pain is normally described directly, as in 頭が痛い (*“my head hurts”*), while 具合 or 調子 may be used when referring to the condition or functioning of a particular body part.

### Examples
 
*<p class="jp-example">息子はたくさん褒められて気分がいいようです</p>*
*My son seems to be in a very good mood after receiving so much praise.*

*<p class="jp-example">朝から頭が重くて気分が悪い</p>*
*My head has felt heavy since this morning, and I feel unwell overall.* 

*<p class="jp-example">バスに酔って気分が悪くなったので、途中で降りた</p>* 
*The bus ride made me feel nauseous, so I got off before my stop.*

In the last example, 気分が悪くなった indicates a general physical discomfort, often nausea caused by motion sickness. 

In a medical context, someone might tell a doctor 昨夜から気分が悪いです to indicate nausea or a general feeling of being unwell. By contrast, of the four terms compared in this article, 気分がいい is the one used for **“being in a good mood”** in the emotional sense.

## 調子 (condition, performance, or functioning)

### Kanji analysis

- 調 (chō) means tone, harmony, adjustment. 

- 子 (shi) means child, but here it does not carry its literal meaning; in many compounds it functions as a nominal suffix without referring to its original sense. 

We can think of 調子 as indicating the “tone” or tuning of something, and the way something *“sounds”* or functions. In other words, it represents how a person or object is functioning, progressing, or performing, as well as its rhythm or general condition.

### Meaning and usage 

調子 has several uses. In everyday language, it often indicates **the general condition or functioning of people and things**. 

It can refer to a person’s physical condition (調子がいい/悪い referring to being in good form or not), to performance or how something is progressing (for example, 仕事の調子 to mean *“how work is going”*), and also to the condition of devices or parts of the body. 

For example, 調子が悪い can refer to a malfunction: 今日はパソコンの調子が悪い - *the computer isn’t working properly today*. 

Likewise, 調子がいい when referring to an object means that it is working well. With people, 調子がいい means **being in good form or performing well** (for example, in sports); *調子が悪い* indicates that someone’s condition is worse than usual or that they are not at 100%.

One distinctive feature is that 調子 is the only one of these terms also used for sound, tone, or rhythm, whether musical or spoken.

For example, 声の調子 (tone of voice), 調子を合わせる (to keep in time or match someone’s tone). 

In addition, colloquial Japanese includes expressions such as 調子に乗る (*“to get carried away / get too full of oneself,”* literally *“to get on the rhythm”*) or 調子いいね (which can mean *“you’re in good form”* but can also be flattering or ingratiating, depending on the context).

### Examples

*<p class="jp-example">昨日まで熱があったけど、今日はだいぶ調子がよくなりました</p>* 
*I had a fever until yesterday, but I’m feeling much better today (my condition has improved).* 

*<p class="jp-example">最近はお肌の調子がいいです</p>* 
*My skin has been in good condition lately.* 

 *<p class="jp-example">なんかコピー機の調子が悪いね。紙が詰まって全然動かないよ</p>*
 *Hmm, the copier isn’t working properly. The paper keeps jamming and it won’t work at all.* 
 
*<p class="jp-example">A選手、今日は調子悪いんじゃない？ゴール前のミスが多いよ</p>* 
*Player A seems off today, doesn’t he? He’s making a lot of mistakes in front of goal.*

(Note that in these examples 調子 takes on different nuances depending on the context: health, the condition of an object, or athletic performance. You can also informally ask someone 調子はどう？ to mean *How’s it going? Everything okay?* referring broadly to life or health.)

By contrast, 調子 is not used to talk about mood: for example, “to be in a good mood” is 気分がいい, not 調子がいい, unless what is meant is *“to be in good form / for everything to be going well.”*

## 体調 (physical condition, health)

### Kanji analysis 

- 体 (tai) means body. 
- 調 (chō), as we have seen, means condition, harmony. Thus 体調 literally means the condition of the body, that is, one’s state of physical health.

### Meaning and usage 

体調 is used only to indicate the state of health of living beings (typically people). When we say 体調がいい or 体調が悪い, we mean that someone feels physically well or unwell.

It is a fairly direct term: 体調が悪い means **“to be in poor health”** or **“to feel unwell.”**

This term is not used for objects or inanimate things (体調 cannot be used for a computer). Moreover, it usually refers to the general condition of the body, not to one specific area. 

For example, it is used in expressions such as 体調を崩す (*to fall ill, literally “to disrupt one’s physical condition”*) or 体調管理
*(“health management”)*.

In formal contexts (for example, at work or school), 体調不良 is commonly used to indicate **poor physical condition or ill health** in a general sense (e.g. *“absent due to 体調不良,”* meaning *“for health reasons”*). 

In everyday life, saying 体調が悪いので帰ります is a clear way of expressing *“I’m not feeling well physically, so I’m going home.”*

### Examples

*<p class="jp-example">今年の夏はとても暑いので、体調を崩す人が多いです</p>* 
*It’s extremely hot this summer, so many people are falling ill (or experiencing health problems).* 

*<p class="jp-example">旅行の前に体調を整えておきたい</p>* 
*I’d like to make sure I’m in good physical condition before the trip.*
 
*<p class="jp-example">田中さん、先週はずっと休んでいたけど今日は来ていますね。体調は良くなったのかな？</p>* 
*Mr. Tanaka was absent all last week, but he’s here today. I wonder if he’s feeling better.*

(Remember that 体調 concerns only the body. It would not be used for mood or for objects. For example, *パソコンの体調* makes no sense, whereas **パソコンの調子/具合** is perfectly natural. 体調 can also be used naturally for pets; 具合 is likewise common when referring to their health or condition.)

## 具合 (condition, state, or how something is going)

### Kanji analysis 

- 具 (gu) means tool, implement, provide. 

- 合 (ai) means to join, combine, fit. The basic idea is **“how the parts are arranged and fit together.”** In fact, 具合 was originally written 工合, where 工 meant situation or aspect and 合 meant to fit or harmonize. 

Thus, 具合 refers to how something presents itself or functions—that is, its overall state as determined by the *“way its elements fit together.”*

### Meaning and usage 

具合 is a very broad term that can refer to a person’s state of health, the functioning of an object, or how something is progressing.

#### Condition or functioning of objects and the body

For example, 機械の具合 (*the condition of a machine*) or 体の具合 (*the condition of the body*). 具合が悪い is commonly used to say that *“something is wrong”* with either a machine or one’s body. 

Example: テレビの具合がおかしい - *there’s something wrong with the television*. 

With people, 具合が悪い means feeling physically unwell (and is interchangeable with 体調が悪い in informal contexts). 

Conversely, 具合が良い can indicate that something is going well: when referring to people, however, it is less often used on its own (体の具合は良い is more common), whereas it is common in expressions such as うまい具合にいった *(“it worked out perfectly / things fell into place nicely”)*.

#### How something is progressing

For example, 仕事の進み具合 (the progress of the work), 勉強の具合 (*how one’s studies are going*), こんな具合で進めていこう (*“let’s keep going like this”*). 
In these cases, 具合 describes **“the way things are progressing.”**

#### Circumstances and convenience

具合 can refer to favorable or unfavorable circumstances. For example, それじゃ具合が悪い in conversation can mean **“that wouldn’t work well for me / that would be inconvenient / that would put me in an awkward situation.”** This use is similar to 都合 (tsugō). 

Example: 明日はちょっと具合が悪い - *“tomorrow doesn’t really work for me (I can’t make it).”* 

Here, 具合が悪い clearly has nothing to do with health; it refers instead to availability or circumstances.

#### Other less common meanings

具合 can also refer to appearance or propriety (体裁). 

For example, ここまで来て断るのは具合が悪い means *“now that we’ve come this far, refusing would look bad.”* 

These uses are less common in everyday speech, but they do exist.

具合 is very common in medical settings and in everyday conversations about health.

For example, a doctor or family member may ask 具合はどうですか? (*How are you feeling?*) when speaking to someone who is ill. Or one may say 食後の具合 (*how one feels after eating*). 

Informally, 具合 and 体調 are often equivalent when talking about health: 昨日より具合は良くなった？ – *“Are you feeling better than yesterday?”*

### Examples

 *<p class="jp-example">熱は下がったが、まだ体の具合が良くない</p>* 
 *My fever has gone down, but I still don’t feel well physically.* 

 *<p class="jp-example">洗濯機の具合がおかしいから、そろそろ買い替えないと</p>* 
 *The washing machine is acting strangely, so I’ll have to replace it soon.* 

 *<p class="jp-example">うまい具合に電車が来た</p>* 
 *The train arrived at exactly the right moment (perfect timing).* 

 *<p class="jp-example">「明日会える？」「ごめん、明日はちょっと具合が悪いんだ…」</p>* 
 *“Can we meet tomorrow?” “Sorry, tomorrow doesn’t really work for me…” (literally, “the circumstances are a little unfavorable tomorrow”).* 


In the examples above, 具合 covers several contexts: physical condition, the functioning of a machine, and favorable circumstances. 

In ordinary usage, when we simply say 具合が悪い without further context, it usually means *“I feel unwell.”* For example, *今日はちょっと具合が悪いので早退します* - *I’m not feeling very well today, so I’m leaving early.* 

If there is a risk of ambiguity, however, 体の具合 is often specified when **talking about health**, while 都合 is used when **talking about convenience or availability**.

## Comparing 気分, 調子, 体調, and 具合

The four terms described above share the general idea of **“condition/state,”** but their ranges of use and shades of meaning differ. 

Here is a comparison to clarify when they can be used in similar ways and when they are not interchangeable.

### General physical health 
 
When you want to say *“I don’t feel well”* in a general physical sense, 体調が悪い, 具合が悪い, or 調子が悪い can be used almost interchangeably. 

For example, if you call work to say that you are sick, *今日は体調が悪いです*, *今日は具合が悪いです*, and *今日は調子が悪くて* are all understandable ways of saying that you are not feeling well. 

The difference is that 体調が悪い is very clear and direct (it refers to physical health), 具合が悪い is colloquial and carries essentially the same meaning (something like *“I’m feeling a bit unwell”*), and 調子が悪い in this case means *“I’m not in my usual form.”* 

The last of these may sound slightly less serious or specific than 体調/具合, but it is perfectly natural in everyday usage.

*気分が悪い* can also mean *“to feel physically unwell,”* but it is used especially when there is a specific physical sensation of discomfort, such as nausea, dizziness, or sudden malaise.

For example, 電車に酔って気分が悪くなった - **I felt nauseous on the train**. In health-related contexts, *気分が悪い* often implies this kind of subjective physical discomfort. 

So if you have an upset stomach, dizziness, and so on, 気分が悪い is appropriate. If, on the other hand, you simply want to say that you are generally not in good form, 体調/調子/具合が悪い is more common. 

Note that 気分が悪い can also mean **“I’m in a bad mood”** in other contexts.

### Mood vs physical condition

 気分 is the only one of these terms that also clearly refers to mood or psychological state. If we say 今日は気分がいい with no further context, it is understood as **“I’m in a good mood today”** (or more generally **“I feel good today”**). 

By contrast, 今日は体調がいい means **“I feel physically well today,”** 今日は調子がいい can mean either **“I feel physically good / I’m in good form today”** or **“things are going well today / I’m on form,”** and 今日は具合がいい by itself is not very common for saying *“I feel well”* (体調/調子がいい is preferred). 

Moreover, 調子/体調/具合 are not used for *“being in a bad mood.”* In that case, one says 気分が落ち込んでいる (feeling down) or 機嫌が悪い **(being in a bad mood)**, depending on the nuance. 気分 therefore covers an emotional dimension that the other terms do not.


### A specific body part vs the whole body

When talking about a problem affecting a specific part of the body, 調子 and 具合 can be used, whereas 気分 cannot. For example, 胃の調子が悪い or 胃の具合が悪い **(“my stomach isn’t feeling right”)** are common expressions, whereas 胃の気分が悪い is incorrect. 

In that case, one could say **胃の具合** (or simply 胃がムカムカする = my stomach feels queasy). 

As noted above, when 気分 refers to the body, it describes a general condition and not a single organ or body part. By contrast, 体調 always concerns the body as a whole. 

You would never say 足の体調 (for example, if your leg hurts, 具合/調子が悪い would be used).

### Objects and devices

When talking about the operating condition of an object or device, 調子 or 具合 can be used, but not 気分 or 体調. 

For example: 

             パソコンの調子が悪い = *“the computer isn’t working properly.”* 
            

            パソコンの具合を見る = *“check how the computer is working.”* 

具合 can in fact be used for both people and things, as can 調子, whereas 体調 is restricted to living beings and 気分 concerns only moods or subjective physical sensations. 

Between 調子 and 具合, 調子 is perhaps more common in everyday speech for machinery (エンジンの調子が悪い sounds natural).  

具合 is still used, however, sometimes with a slightly different nuance: 具合がおかしい implies that *“there is something odd about the way it is functioning.”* 

In short, for objects and also for general situations, 具合 (e.g. 進み具合 = *progress*) or 調子 (e.g. 景気の調子 = *the state of the economy*) is used, not 気分/体調.

### Convenience and circumstances

As mentioned above, 具合が悪い can also describe circumstances as inconvenient or problematic, much like 都合が悪い.

None of 調子, 気分, or 体調 has this meaning. Thus, in a sentence such as それだとちょっと具合が悪いね (meaning *that would be a bit of a problem for me*), 具合 cannot be replaced by the other terms because the meaning would change completely (it would shift toward health or something else, rather than the situation).

## Formality and fixed expressions 

In formal situations (for example, when speaking to a superior or customer, or in announcements), 体調 or 体の具合 is more commonly heard. 
For example: お体の具合はいかがですか？ is a polite way to ask *“How are you feeling / how is your health?”* 

Likewise, ご気分はいかがですか？ may be used by a doctor or nurse when speaking to a patient to ask *“How are you feeling?”* (here 気分 refers to physical well-being, not mood). 

調子, by contrast, is more colloquial in this context (“体調” is more appropriate when speaking politely about health). In everyday conversation among friends or family, 具合 or 調子 are perfectly natural when asking after someone: もう具合は良くなった？, 調子どう？だいぶ良くなった.  

気分 on its own is not used to ask about another person’s state of health (it would sound more like *“how is your mood?”*), unless the other person had specifically mentioned nausea—for example, まだ気分悪い？大丈夫？ if it was already known that they had been feeling nauseous.

## Conclusion 

気分, 調子, 体調, and 具合 can overlap, especially when talking about a person’s physical condition, but they are not simply four interchangeable ways to say “feel well” or “feel unwell.” The choice depends primarily on **which aspect of the situation the speaker wants to bring into focus.**

The most useful criterion, then, is not to start from an English translation and look for its Japanese equivalent, but to ask **from what perspective the condition is being described**:

* **気分 (きぶん)** centers on what the subject **perceives or feels**. It is therefore natural for mood and state of mind, but also for subjective physical sensations of discomfort. This is why 気分が悪い can refer to feeling physically unwell, while, depending on the context, it can also convey a sense of discomfort or irritation;

* **体調 (たいちょう)** centers on **the overall physical condition of the body**. If you simply want to say that someone is physically unwell, 体調 is often the clearest choice: it does not describe performance or mood, but the state of the body itself;

* **調子 (ちょうし)** centers on **how someone or something is functioning, progressing, or performing relative to its usual state**. A person can therefore be 調子が悪い without actually being ill: they may be tired, out of form, unable to concentrate, or simply performing below their usual level. The same logic allows 調子 to be used for an engine, an activity, work, a performance, or even tone and rhythm;

* **具合 (ぐあい)** focuses more strongly on **the concrete state something is in, the way it presents itself, or the degree or extent of a resulting condition**. It can refer to health, a body part, a machine, a situation, or the result of a process, as in 焼け具合 “degree of doneness” or 出来具合 “how something turned out / degree of completion.”

A particularly important distinction, then, is that between 調子 and 具合. Both can be used, for example, when talking about the body or a machine, but 調子 more strongly suggests **functioning, progress, or performance**, whereas 具合 more readily draws attention to the **concrete state something is in**. This is not, however, an absolute rule: in areas where the two meanings overlap, both forms can be perfectly natural.

The same applies when talking about health. 体調が悪い, 具合が悪い, 調子が悪い, and, in certain circumstances, 気分が悪い can all describe someone who “doesn’t feel well,” but each expression frames the situation from a different angle: **overall physical state with 体調, concrete condition with 具合, functioning or usual form with 調子, and the sensation experienced by the subject with 気分**.

To choose the most natural word, it can therefore be useful to ask yourself four questions:

**“How do I feel?” → 気分**
**“How am I doing physically?” → 体調**
**“Am I functioning or performing as usual?” → 調子**
**“What concrete state is this thing, this body part, or this situation in?” → 具合**

These questions should be treated as guidelines, not rigid boundaries. In actual Japanese usage, there are many areas of overlap, and in many cases changing the word does not change the fact being described, but rather **the point of view from which the speaker chooses to present it**.

One final piece of advice is not to learn 気分, 調子, 体調, and 具合 only through isolated definitions. A more effective approach is to also observe and memorize the **combinations in which each term naturally occurs**: 気分転換 immediately evokes the domain of 気分, 体調を整える that of physical condition, 本調子 the idea of returning to one’s usual form, and forms such as 出来具合 show how 具合 can express the resulting state or degree of something.

Rather than trying to find one fixed English translation for each word, then, the goal is to learn to recognize **which aspect of the situation the speaker is focusing on**. It is precisely this choice of perspective that determines which of 気分, 調子, 体調, and 具合 sounds most natural.
