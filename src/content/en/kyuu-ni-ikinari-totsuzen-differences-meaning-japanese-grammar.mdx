What is the difference between 急に, いきなり, and 突然, and which of these words should be used to say *“suddenly”* in Japanese?

All three describe something that happens quickly or unexpectedly, so it is natural for bilingual dictionaries to translate them in the same way. This apparent equivalence, however, conceals a fundamental difference: **急に, いきなり, and 突然 do not frame sudden events from the same perspective**.

- 急に (kyū ni) foregrounds the speed at which a situation changes. 

	It is particularly natural when a state, condition, or process shifts from one configuration to another in a short space of time: 急に寒くなる (“to suddenly turn cold”), 急に痛くなる (“to suddenly start hurting”), 急に忙しくなる (“to suddenly become busy”). 

	The change may be instantaneous, but it may also unfold over a brief interval: what matters is that it occurs much more rapidly than expected.

- いきなり (ikinari), by contrast, highlights the absence of the stages that would normally precede an action or result. 

	Someone acts without an introduction, preparation, or warning, or moves directly to an advanced stage: いきなり質問する (“to ask a question straight away, without any preamble”), いきなり結論を言う (“to go straight to the conclusion”), いきなり優勝する (“to win on the very first attempt”). 

	The central element is not merely speed, but the fact that **the expected sequence is skipped**.

- 突然 (totsuzen), finally, presents an event as something that bursts onto the scene without warning and that, until that moment, had not been anticipated: 突然電気が消える (“the lights suddenly go out”), 突然人が現れる (“a person suddenly appears”), 突然事故が起こる (“an accident suddenly occurs”). 

	Compared with 急に, the focus shifts away from the speed of the transformation and toward the **unexpected onset of the event itself**.


The choice therefore does not depend simply on how abrupt or surprising an occurrence is. The decisive question is different: does the speaker want to describe **the speed of a change**, **the absence of intermediate stages**, or **the unexpected emergence of an event**?

The three semantic areas are not completely separate. In many sentences, more than one form is grammatically possible, but replacing one with another changes the perspective from which the event is presented.

In 急に雨が降ってきた, for example, 急に primarily evokes the rapid transition from a situation in which it was not raining to one in which it is. 

突然雨が降ってきた instead presents the beginning of the rain as an unexpected event that occurred without warning. 

いきなり雨が降ってきた is possible especially in speech and gives the onset of the rain a more abrupt, immediate, narrative feel, almost as though it had burst into the ongoing situation.

The difference becomes even clearer with human actions. In いきなりそんなことを言われても困る, the problem is not merely that the words were spoken suddenly: the speaker is reacting above all to the fact that the other person introduced the subject without preparation and without the steps the context would normally have required. 

This is why いきなり is especially common with questions, confessions, requests, attacks, departures, and other actions aimed directly at someone.

急に, いきなり, and 突然 should therefore not be regarded as three degrees of the same word, arranged from weakest to strongest. The difference lies in how the event is conceptualized:

* with 急に, the focus is mainly on **the transition from one state to another**;
* with いきなり, the focus is on **the expected sequence and the omission of its intermediate stages**;
* with 突然, the focus is on **an event that occurs without warning**.

Register and grammatical behavior also contribute to the choice. 

- 急に is extremely common in everyday conversation and is particularly flexible with verbs of change;

- いきなり has a more distinctly colloquial, direct, and expressive character; 

- 突然 is natural in speech as well, but occurs especially often in narration, news reporting, and expository writing; it can also modify a noun directly through の, as in 突然の事故 (“a sudden accident”*) or 突然の訪問 (“an unexpected visit”).

In the following sections, we will examine the origin, meaning, and behavior of each term separately. We will then compare their most frequent collocations, registers, and areas of overlap, while also identifying the cases in which replacing one with another changes the nuance or produces an unnatural sentence.

## The Difference between 急に, いきなり, and 突然

To choose between 急に, いきなり, and 突然, it is not enough to ask which of the three corresponds most closely to the English word *“suddenly.”* A more reliable criterion is often to consider **what kind of event the verb describes** and which phase of that event the speaker intends to foreground.

急に combines particularly naturally with verbs expressing change, such as なる (*“to become”*), 変わる (*“to change”*), 増える (*“to increase”*), 減る (*“to decrease”*), or なくなる (*“to cease to exist, to no longer be present”*). In these cases, there is an earlier state and a later one, and 急に signals that the transition between them took place in an unusually short time.

いきなり, by contrast, frequently occurs with concrete actions, especially when someone speaks, moves, or acts without carrying out the steps the recipient would have expected: 言う (*“to say”*), 話しかける (*“to speak to someone”*), 立ち上がる (*“to stand up”*), 殴る (*“to hit”*), or 告白する (*“to confess, to declare one’s feelings”*). 

The action is perceived from the outside as lacking any introduction or preparation.

突然 is less closely tied to any single event structure. It can accompany intentional actions, natural phenomena, and events independent of human agency, and is particularly common with verbs such as 現れる (*“to appear”*), 消える (*“to disappear”*), 起こる (*“to occur”*), 死ぬ (*“to die”*), and 訪れる (*“to arrive, to visit”*). 

The event is presented as something that bursts onto the scene without warning.

These associations are not rigid rules. The same verb can occur with all three adverbs, but the point of view changes:

*<p class="jp-example">彼は急に立ち上がった。</p>*
*“He stood up abruptly.”*  
*急に highlights the speed of the transition from a seated to a standing position*.

*<p class="jp-example">彼はいきなり立ち上がった。</p>*
*“All of a sudden, he stood up.”*  
*いきなり suggests that he stood up without any preceding gesture, signal, or preparation*.

*<p class="jp-example">彼は突然立ち上がった。</p>*
*“He suddenly stood up.”*  
*突然 presents the action as an unexpected event interrupting the situation already under way*.

The difference therefore does not necessarily lie in the event being described, but in how the speaker frames it: as a **rapid change**, as an **action lacking preparation**, or as an **event that unexpectedly enters the scene**.

In the following sections, we will examine 急に, いきなり, and 突然 separately, exploring their etymology, meaning, main contexts of use, and linguistic register.
This will clarify when the three adverbs can overlap and when replacing one with another changes the nuance of the sentence.

## 急に (kyūni)

### Etymology, Definition, and Usage
*急に* derives from the adjectival noun (形容動詞) 急なり (*kyū-nari, “sudden, abrupt”*), built on the kanji 急, which is of Chinese origin and carries the meaning **“urgent.”**

In Classical Japanese, 形容動詞 were inflected with the copula なり, whose adverbial or continuative form (連用形) is に.
This means that 急なり developed into **急に**: に is therefore not an added particle, but the *“fossilized trace”* of the inflection itself. 

急に indicates that a change or action occurs **without warning, within a very short time**. 

As an adverb, it describes an extremely rapid change as observed from the outside, often with an implicit sense of surprise or urgency. Some Japanese dictionaries define it as «物事が前触れなく突然に起こるさま», that is, **“the manner in which something occurs suddenly, without any warning.”** 

It overlaps with 突然, ふいに, and other expressions, but focuses on a change of state or a rapid action rather than on an emotional reaction. 
In other words, 急に primarily emphasizes the **speed** or **abruptness** of the change. It often implies that something happens **“quickly”** or **“suddenly”** relative to the speaker’s expectations. 

At times, it carries a slight sense of inevitability or urgency in context (e.g. 急に忙しくなった — *suddenly became busy*). Unlike 突然, it does not emphasize the element of *“emotional shock”* so much as the aspect of **“acceleration / rapid transition.”** 
### Register

**急に is fairly neutral**, though slightly more conversational than 突然. It is used both in everyday conversation and in informal writing. According to studies of stylistic nuance, 急に tends to sound *softer* and more characteristic of speech than 突然. In formal contexts, 突然 is often preferred, whereas 急に is frequent in informal dialogue and colloquial narration. 
### Examples 
*<p class="jp-example">外は、急に雨が降り始めました。</p>* *“It suddenly started raining outside.” — A sudden change in the weather. This shows 急に used for a rapid shift—the onset of rain—without warning.*

*<p class="jp-example">授業中に、急に眠くなりました。</p>* *“I suddenly became sleepy during class.” — A sudden internal change: drowsiness sets in all at once.*


*<p class="jp-example">急にお風呂のお湯が出なくなりましたから、困っています。</p>*
*“The hot water for the bath suddenly stopped running, so I’m in a bind.” — The abrupt interruption of an ongoing process—the water supply—in a domestic context.*

*<p class="jp-example">急にお金が必要になったので、親に借りました。</p>* *“I suddenly needed some money, so I borrowed it from my parents.” — A situation in which money is unexpectedly required. This illustrates the use of 急に for an unforeseen need.*


In each example, 急に presents an extremely rapid change. 

**Note**: **急に does not modify an object or participant, but the event as a whole**. Rather, it describes how the event unfolds in time: suddenly. This is why it combines naturally with verbs of change or inception, such as 雨が降り始める, 眠くなる, and 必要になる.

## いきなり (ikinari) 


### Etymology, Definition, and Usage

いきなり probably derives from **行きなり** (**いきなり**), in which 行き refers to the course or manner in which an action proceeds, while なり is the Classical Japanese copula.

Originally, the expression meant something along the lines of **“as events unfold,”** from which the idea developed of an action proceeding without intermediate stages.

The modern meaning *“suddenly”* arose precisely from this absence of transition: something happens without preparation or introduction, proceeding directly to the result.

Thus, いきなり indicates that something happens **suddenly**, **without any lead-up**. Dictionaries such as the *Daijisen* define いきなり as *«何の前触れもなく急に事が起きるさま、突然»*, that is, **“the manner in which something happens suddenly, without any warning.”**


In practice, it corresponds to **“suddenly”** or **“all of a sudden.”** 

It often suggests that all intermediate stages have been skipped—that the action goes straight to the point.

In its general meaning, it is similar to 突然, which we will examine in the next section. Compared with 急に, いきなり can carry a **nuance of surprise** associated with something being done **“without prior reflection,”** reflecting its older sense of acting “on impulse, without thinking too much.”

For example, in *いきなり会長職とは荷が重い* (*being appointed president all of a sudden is a heavy responsibility*), the tone is more colloquial and ironic. いきなり can also imply a more abrupt shift in the situation, often with colloquial emphasis.

In other words, both 急に and いきなり can imply surprise, but for different reasons: **急に signals a sudden change of state**, generating surprise through the speed of the transition, whereas **いきなり indicates the absence of intermediate stages**, producing surprise through the lack of preparation.

**Note**: Some modern interpretations analyze なり as a form of the verb なる (*“to become”*), but this reading is misleading.
Although なり formally coincides with the continuative form (連用形) of なる, in いきなり it does not perform a verbal function; instead, it represents a fossilized form of copular origin (に + あり).

The expression 行きなり should therefore not be interpreted as *“to go and become,”* but as a construction describing how a process unfolds, from which the modern meanings **“without intermediate stages”** and **“suddenly”** developed. 


### Register
いきなり belongs to a **predominantly colloquial register**. It is frequent in everyday speech and also occurs in narratives with an informal or dynamic tone.

Compared with 突然, it is less formal and closer in register to 急に, although it remains semantically distinct from it. 

It can be compared with ふいに, which tends to occur in more literary contexts, especially those involving perception.
In English, it is often rendered as *“suddenly,”* but its use remains typical of informal contexts: it is not an elevated lexical choice, but a natural and immediate expression. 
### Examples
*<p class="jp-example">授業中に、いきなり先生に質問をされたので、答えられなかった。</p>* *“During class, the teacher suddenly asked me a question, so I could not answer.” — A question that came “out of nowhere,” without any transition. Here, いきなり indicates that the event—the question—occurred without warning.*

*<p class="jp-example">道路では、子供がいきなり飛び出してくることがあるから、気をつけましょう。</p>* *“A child may suddenly dart out into the road, so be careful.” — いきなり highlights the child’s abrupt movement into the street.*

*<p class="jp-example">ここ2、3日でいきなり寒くなってきた。</p>* *“Over the past two or three days, it has suddenly turned cold.” — An abrupt change in the weather. いきなり emphasizes that the cooling occurred all at once.*

*<p class="jp-example">部屋で着替えていたら、いきなり母がドアを開けて入ってきたのでびっくりした。</p>* *“I was changing in my room when my mother suddenly opened the door and walked in, which startled me.” — A colloquial use of いきなり to describe someone entering unexpectedly without knocking.*

In these examples, いきなり always indicates an event that occurs without any lead-up or preparation. Note that, as an adverb, it can appear before the verb (飛び出す, 寒くなる, 開ける) or at the beginning of the sentence.

## 突然 (totsuzen) 


### Etymology, Definition, and Usage

突然 is a Sino-Japanese compound formed from the kanji 突 (*“to thrust suddenly,”* *“sudden”*) + 然 (*“so,” “state”*). 

It derives from Classical Chinese with the meaning **“suddenly.”** In modern Japanese, it is used adverbially and can also modify nouns with の, as in 突然の事故 (*“a sudden accident”*). Beyond its Chinese origin, the word has no specific native Japanese etymology.

It means **“suddenly”** or **“unexpectedly.”** The *Daijisen* defines it as *«予期しないことが急に起こるさま。だしぬけであるさま»*, that is, **“the manner in which something unexpected occurs suddenly.”** 

It is very similar to 急に, but places greater emphasis on **unpredictability and the sense of a sudden turn of events**. It is generally used when an event surprises the people involved and has an emotional impact. 

突然 focuses on the **complete absence of warning** and the resulting sense of astonishment. In English, 突然 is often translated as **“suddenly”** or **“all of a sudden,”** though expressions such as **“in the blink of an eye”** can sometimes convey its force even more effectively. 

It differs from 急に in that it can also imply stronger emotional involvement: one is surprised, astonished, or shaken by what happens. Unlike いきなり, 突然 is more **neutral or formal and less colloquial**. 
### Register

As already noted, 突然 tends to be more formal or more characteristic of written language than 急に or いきなり. 
It is frequently used in the media and in written texts such as news articles and reports, and appears less often in ordinary everyday conversation. 

突然 can sound **somewhat stiffer and more characteristic of writing**, which is why it is often preferred in official letters or formal communication. 突然 is also used in conversation, but speakers often simply choose 急に instead. 
### Examples

*<p class="jp-example">授業中に野崎さんが突然泣き出した。</p>* *“During class, Nozaki-san suddenly burst into tears.” — An unexpected and surprising event: the sudden onset of crying.*

*<p class="jp-example">さっきまで晴れていたのに、突然大雨になった。</p>* *“It had been clear until just a moment ago, and then it suddenly began pouring.” — Emphasis on an unexpected and abrupt change in the weather.*

*<p class="jp-example">突然、犬が飛び出してきた。</p>* *“A dog suddenly darted out.” — A short sentence that emphasizes the shock of the dog’s appearance.*

*<p class="jp-example">突然の事故で、歌手のAさんが亡くなりました。</p>* *“Singer A died unexpectedly in an accident.” — Use in a formal context, such as a news report. 突然 presents the tragic event as entirely unexpected.*


These examples show 突然 used in everyday situations as well as in a formal announcement. It often precedes the verb directly (飛び出す, 死ぬ) or combines with の to form phrases such as 突然の事故.

## Comparing 急に, いきなり, and 突然


### General Usage and Emotional Impact

All three are used for events that are **“rapid and unexpected,”** but 突然 places greater emphasis on surprise and emotional impact, whereas 急に focuses on the speed of the change. いきなり is similar to 突然 in colloquial usage, but is more informal and can sometimes imply **acting without reflection**, an aspect connected with its morphological origin. 

For example, in the pair «突然、犬が飛び出してきた» and «いきなり犬が飛び出してきた», the latter sounds more colloquial, while the former sounds more neutral and slightly more literary.

### Interchangeability

In many everyday contexts, 急に and 突然 are interchangeable when the only emphasis is on rapidity—for example, *急に雨が降ってきた* and *突然雨が降ってきた*. 突然, however, can give the sentence a more dramatic or formal tone. In informal conversation, いきなり often replaces 突然: for example, in *家に帰ったらいきなり母が…*, using 突然 would sound less natural because it would create a more detached tone. 

Conversely, a news report would use 突然. 急に is more neutral and generally acceptable in most contexts except extremely formal ones, where 突然 is preferred.

### Typical Contexts:

- 急に frequently occurs with verbs of change of state or movement (雨が降り出す, 機嫌が変わる, 目が覚める, 痛くなる, 進むなど);
- いきなり often appears in narrative or colloquial sentences to introduce a direct action (e.g. 飛び出す, 切りつける, 質問する), or to indicate that a preparatory stage has been interrupted or skipped;
- 突然 is common in headlines, news reports, or narratives that seek to emphasize the unexpected nature of an event (e.g. 大声を出す, 訪問する, 事故が起こる).

### Notes on Register

As we have seen, 突然 is more formal, 急に more conversational or informal, and いきなり the most informal of the three. For example, one would not normally say “急に事故があった” in a journalistic context, whereas 突然 would be more appropriate. 

Conversely, 突然に can sound somewhat stiff in casual conversation. Although いきなり is informal, it gives a narrative a vivid, abrupt feel, much like *“out of the blue”* in English.

### Contrasting Examples
    - 突然犬が飛び出してきた！ (news / emotional impact) vs 急に犬が飛び出してきた。 — The first emphasizes surprise, the second the speed of the event.
    - いきなりそんなことを言われても困るよ。 (colloquial) vs 突然そんなことを言われても困る — The first has a more conversational and informal tone, while the second sounds more formal.
    - The same applies to changes of state: 急に寒くなった = 突然寒くなった (similar), while with verbs such as 始める the contrast is minimal. With いきなり, the emphasis would fall on skipping the expected sequence (e.g. 話の順番を飛ばして、いきなり結論を述べた vs ...突然結論を述べた). 

Regardless of register, the first has a more dynamic, narrative feel, while the second is more neutral and less vivid.

## Conclusions

急に, いきなり, and 突然 share a common semantic core: all three can describe something that happens rapidly, unexpectedly, or without the speaker having clearly sensed it coming. Translating them as *“suddenly,”* *“all of a sudden,”* or *“out of the blue”* is therefore not incorrect, but an English translation alone cannot reveal the perspective adopted in the Japanese sentence.

The fundamental distinction can be summarized as follows:

* **急に foregrounds the speed of the change**. The focus falls on the transition from an earlier state to a new one, occurring in less time than would normally be expected. This is why it is particularly natural with verbs indicating transformation, variation, the beginning of a state, or its cessation;

* **いきなり foregrounds the absence of preparatory stages**. An action is performed directly, without an introduction or warning and without following the order that the recipient would normally have expected. It is therefore very common with questions, statements, movements, and other concrete actions directed at someone;

* **突然 foregrounds the unexpected onset of the event as a whole**. The event is presented as something that appears, occurs, or takes place without warning. It can refer to intentional actions, natural phenomena, appearances, disappearances, accidents, and other events, regardless of whether they produce a strong emotional reaction.

These differences should not, however, be turned into automatic rules. In many contexts, two or even all three forms can accompany the same verb. Replacing one with another does not necessarily make the sentence incorrect; more often, it shifts its perspectival center. 

The same gesture can be represented as a rapid transition with 急に, as an action lacking preparation with いきなり, or as an unexpected event bursting onto the scene with 突然.

A practical criterion is therefore to begin by examining the **predicate** and the structure of the event:

* when something changes rapidly relative to its previous condition, 急に is often the most natural choice;
* when an introduction, preparation, or normally expected sequence of steps is skipped, いきなり captures the situation more effectively;
* when the event itself is to be presented as unpredictable and occurring without warning, 突然 is particularly suitable.

Only after this should register be considered. 

急に is extremely frequent in everyday conversation and generally maintains a neutral tone. 

いきなり is more distinctly colloquial, direct, and narratively vivid. 

突然 is fully usable in speech as well, but is especially natural in narration, expository writing, news reports, and more controlled forms of communication. These are, however, usage preferences: none of the three terms belongs exclusively to a single register.

To learn to distinguish them, it is more useful to memorize **collocations and complete sentences** than three isolated translations. Associations such as 急に寒くなる, いきなり質問する, and 突然現れる help reveal the most common tendencies. They should not, however, become rigid formulas: the context may allow another adverb, giving the sentence a different nuance.

The English translation should therefore be chosen only after first determining the adverb’s function. *“Suddenly”* can fit all three, while expressions such as *“rapidly,”* *“all of a sudden,”* *“out of the blue,”* *“without any preamble,”* or *“straight away”* may better convey, depending on the context, the rapidity expressed by 急に, the skipped stages encoded by いきなり, or the unpredictability of 突然.


A final distinction concerns whether the event is actually realized. 急に, いきなり, and 突然 normally describe the way in which something **actually occurs**. 

When something is merely on the verge of happening, or is narrowly avoided, Japanese instead uses different constructions, such as [～そうになる and ～ところだった](/en/articles/sou-ni-naru-and-tokoro-datta-difference-japanese-grammar/), which respectively express the imminence of an event and an outcome that almost materialized but ultimately did not.



The real difference between the terms examined in this article therefore does not concern three different degrees of surprise, but **three ways of structuring the same kind of experience through language**: 急に focuses primarily on the transition, いきなり on the expected sequence that was skipped, and 突然 on the event’s unexpected appearance. 

Understanding this distinction makes it possible not only to choose a more natural adverb, but also to interpret more precisely how a Japanese speaker structures and presents what happens.
