What does mattaku (全く, まったく) mean in Japanese, and when would you translate it as “completely,” “not at all,” “really,” or “good grief!”?


**全く** (written まったく in hiragana) is a Japanese adverb that, depending on the construction and context, can mean **“completely,” “entirely,” “absolutely,” “not at all,” “not in the slightest,” “really,” or “indeed”**.

This range of translations reflects the different functions the word can serve:
- Expressing the completeness of a state;
- Reinforcing a negation;
- Strongly emphasizing a fact, a judgment, or a personal reaction.

In speech, it can also become an exclamation of exasperation or displeasure, rendered in English as “Oh, come on!”, “Seriously?”, or “For goodness' sake!”, depending on the situation and tone of voice.

To understand what connects these uses, it helps to start with the kanji used to write the word: **全**. Its meaning encompasses two closely related ideas: **totality**, something considered as a whole, and **wholeness or integrity**, the state of having nothing missing.

We find it, for example, in 全部（ぜんぶ, “all,” “everything”）, 全体（ぜんたい, “the whole,” “the entirety”） and 完全（かんぜん, “completeness,” “perfection”）. What they share is the notion of a complete whole, with no missing parts or excluded aspects.

In 全く, this idea is expressed through a word of native Japanese origin. The adverb derives from **the continuative form, or 連用形（れんようけい）, of the adjective 全い（まったい）**, meaning “complete,” “whole,” or “perfect,” which is related to the classical form 全し（まったし） and, further back, to またし.

The ending ～く is therefore the adverbial ending of the adjective: the semantic shift is from “complete” to “completely.” In contemporary usage, 全く has become established as an independent adverb, with a range of functions extending beyond simply describing completeness.

This origin provides a useful starting point: with 全く, a condition can be presented as fully realized, or a possibility can be ruled out altogether. The same idea of totality can therefore accompany both an affirmative and a negative statement:

*<p class="jp-example">この二つの文章は全く同じだ。</p>*
*These two passages are exactly the same.*

*<p class="jp-example">彼の説明は全く分からない。</p>*
*I don't understand his explanation at all.*

In the first example, 全く emphasizes the complete correspondence between the two passages; in the second, it reinforces the absence of understanding. **The negative meaning comes from the construction with 分からない**, while 全く makes that negation absolute: “I don't understand it one bit.”

This is the relationship behind translations such as “not ... at all” or “not ... in the slightest.” With an affirmative expression, however, the same adverb can indicate that something is completely new, exactly the same, or entirely different.

Alongside this sense of completeness, 全く can take on a more distinctly **emphatic** function. Speakers then use it to lend force to what they say, presenting it as something they fully agree with, something that genuinely happened, or something they feel strongly about. In such cases, “really” or “indeed” may sound more natural than “completely.”

For example, 全くその通りです means “That's exactly right” or “You're absolutely right”: the adverb expresses complete agreement with what has been said. A reply such as 全くだ or 全くです can likewise convey emphatic agreement.

The speaker's involvement becomes especially clear in **exclamatory use**. An exasperated 全く！ uttered after yet another setback can convey irritation, frustration, disappointment, or reproach. To translate it, we need to capture the reaction being expressed: “Oh, come on!”, “You've got to be kidding me!”, or something similar.

The construction, intonation, and situation are therefore essential for distinguishing a firm statement from an outburst of impatience.

To make sense of these uses, the following sections will first explore the **meaning and nuances** of 全く, distinguishing completeness, total negation, and emphasis. A brief overview will then show **how 全く is used in a sentence**, bringing together its main constructions in a handy reference table. We will also examine patterns such as 全くの and replies with 全くだ・全くです.

Next, we will look at **register and contexts of use**, exploring how its effect changes across description, conversation, and emotional reactions. A comparison with **similar expressions**, including 全然, 完全に, and 本当に, followed by a series of **usage examples**, will help clarify when to choose 全く and how to interpret and translate it in context.

## 全く (mattaku, まったく): Meaning and Usage in Japanese

The core meaning of **全く（まったく, mattaku）** revolves around **completeness**: a condition is presented as fully realized, with no parts or aspects left out. This is the basis for translations such as “completely,” “entirely,” and “wholly,” but also for strengthening a negation, expressed in English as “not ... at all,” “not ... in the slightest,” or “absolutely not.”

In actual usage, 全く can also lend force to a statement or evaluation, approaching the meanings “really,” “indeed,” or “truly.” To understand how these possibilities relate to one another, we need to establish what is being presented as complete: the state being described or the speaker's commitment to what they are saying.

The starting point is the kanji **全**, whose meanings include **“all,” “whole,” “complete,” and “intact”**. Japanese dictionaries explain its meaning through two complementary ideas: something that encompasses every part of a given whole, and something that lacks nothing, expressed by the definition 欠けたところがない.

The first highlights the inclusion of every element; the second, the presence of all the parts or characteristics needed to constitute a complete whole.

This relationship can be seen in words where 全 has the Sino-Japanese reading ゼン. In 全員（ぜんいん）, “all the members” or “everyone,” every member of the group is included; in 全文（ぜんぶん）, “the full text,” the entire text is included; in 完全（かんぜん）, “completeness” or “perfection,” the word expresses a state in which nothing is lacking.

Totality can thus refer both to the full extent of a group and to the integrity of what makes something whole. This second perspective, together with the general idea of wholeness, makes the meaning of 全く particularly clear.

The connection between the character and the adverb is semantic: **全く is a word of native Japanese origin and is written with 全 because the character represents its semantic field**. The word is based on the adjective 全い（まったい）, “complete,” “intact,” or “perfect,” corresponding to the classical form 全し（まったし）. The latter is a variant, with a doubled consonant, of the older またし.

In older vocabulary, the idea of integrity could also refer to life and the body: being “intact” meant remaining unharmed or unscathed. This use helps convey the concrete sense of the original meaning: something remains whole, without being damaged or deprived of any part. The same semantic field later encompassed the completeness of objects and situations, understood as lacking nothing or having no defects.

**まったく is the ～く form of this adjective**, that is, its 連用形（れんようけい, continuative form）, which became established as an adverb. The relationship can be illustrated by the shift from “complete” to “completely,” or from “intact” to “wholly.” In contemporary Japanese, however, 全く has become lexically independent and developed a variety of uses that also require us to consider its emphatic and exclamatory extensions.

When used to describe a state or relationship, 全く can emphasize that the description applies fully. If two things are said to be the same, their correspondence is presented as complete; if knowledge of something is denied, even the slightest familiarity with it is ruled out:

*<p class="jp-example">二人の意見は全く同じだ。</p>*
*Their opinions are exactly the same.*

*<p class="jp-example">その件については全く知らない。</p>*
*I know absolutely nothing about that matter.*

In the first example, 全く emphasizes that the two opinions are exactly the same; in the second, it makes the negation expressed by 知らない absolute. The meaning of the adverb thus works in two directions: **the full realization of a given condition** or, in a negative construction, **the complete absence of what is being negated**. It is the negation that gives rise to the reading “not at all”: 全く specifies its scope, ruling out even the partial existence of what is denied.

This relationship explains why the idea of totality can lead to such different English translations. “Completely” presents a condition as entirely true; “not ... at all” rules that condition out altogether.

An expression such as 全く違う, “to be completely different,” also follows from the completeness of the difference, even though it contains no negative ～ない form. To interpret 全く, we therefore need to consider the meaning of the expression it accompanies as well as its grammatical form.

Completeness also provides a way to distinguish 全く from a general “very.” In its descriptive uses, the adverb highlights the full or total nature of a condition. An opinion described as 全く同じ is said to be exactly the same; knowledge denied through 全く知らない is ruled out entirely. This distinction matters because **a high degree and a complete condition are different concepts**, even if they sometimes overlap in discourse.

Alongside this descriptive dimension, however, there is an **evaluative and emphatic** one: speakers may use 全く to express their firm conviction that something is just as they say, that their judgment is fully justified, or that they agree with what someone else has said.

In these cases, the translation shifts toward “really,” “indeed,” or “truly.” In 全くその通りだ, “That's exactly right,” the emphasis falls on complete agreement with a statement. In other contexts, the adverb may accompany an impression, an admiring assessment, or a critical judgment.

One useful way to connect these meanings is to consider **where the emphasis falls**. Sometimes the focus is on the completeness of the situation being described; at other times, on the conviction or personal involvement with which the speaker presents it. This latter function allows 全く to accompany evaluations for which literal completeness would be difficult to measure: its communicative effect is to strengthen what is said.

Personal involvement can ultimately become the very focus of the expression. When 全く is uttered as an exclamation, even on its own, it can express annoyance, exasperation, disappointment, or displeasure. Here its primary contribution is the emotional response: translating it means capturing the tone, with English expressions such as “Oh, come on!” or “Seriously?”, depending on the context.

The underlying idea of completeness thus offers a key to understanding the word's various uses, while the precise meaning emerges from the interplay between the expression it modifies, the construction, the context, and intonation. With this in mind, we can turn to the next section and examine the different nuances of 全く one by one, identifying what it reinforces in each case and which English translation best conveys the effect.

**Note**: The relationship between intensity and the speaker's evaluation is also explored in our comparison of [とても, かなり, and ずいぶん](/en/articles/totemo-kanari-zuibun-difference-japanese-grammar/). The distinction between high degree, a reference threshold, and subjective reaction provides a useful point of comparison for understanding why 全く calls for different translations depending on construction and context.

## The Different Nuances of 全く

As we have seen, the underlying idea of completeness in **全く** takes on different nuances according to the construction and context. This adverb can present a state as fully realized, strengthen a negation to express total absence, or, in the **全くの** construction, qualify what a noun denotes in unequivocal terms.

The emphasis may also fall on the firmness of the speaker's judgment, their complete agreement with the other person's words, or a reaction of irritation or disappointment.

To interpret and translate it correctly, we therefore need to look at **what is being emphasized and what function it serves in the utterance**: “completely,” “not at all,” “really,” “exactly,” and “Oh, come on!” capture different effects of the same word.

We will examine these meanings separately through examples and commentary, bearing in mind that they can sometimes overlap: an evaluation strengthened by 全く may, for example, also convey the speaker's annoyance.

### Completeness and Totality: “Completely,” “Entirely,” “Exactly Alike”

The basic nuance of 全く is to present a state, property, or relationship as **fully realized**. The most immediate English translations are “completely,” “entirely,” “wholly,” or “in every respect,” depending on the expression it accompanies.

The adverb's contribution is particularly clear with words expressing identity, difference, or novelty. 全く同じ means “exactly the same”; 全く違う means “completely different”; 全く新しい means “completely new.” In each case, the description is presented as fully applicable: the identity is complete, the difference is radical, and the novelty is substantial.

*<p class="jp-example">この二つの文章は、最後の一文を除けば全く同じだ。</p>*
*These two passages are identical in every respect except for the last sentence.*

Here, 全く emphasizes the full correspondence between the passages **within the scope defined by the sentence**: the final sentence is explicitly excluded from the comparison. The totality expressed by the adverb must therefore be interpreted in relation to what is actually being considered in context.

*<p class="jp-example">この発見によって、全く新しい研究分野が開かれた。</p>*
*This discovery opened up an entirely new field of research.*

全く新しい presents the field of research as something genuinely new, opening up possibilities that did not previously exist. The adverb reinforces the novelty of what is being described.

*<p class="jp-example">兄と私は、物事の考え方が全く違う。</p>*
*My older brother and I have completely different ways of looking at things.*

The difference is presented as sharp and profound. The comparison concerns how the two people think: the specific aspect identified in the sentence.

This nuance can accompany favorable, unfavorable, or neutral descriptions. **Completeness does not, in itself, imply either approval or disapproval**: those attitudes come from the content of the sentence and its context.

### Total Negation: “Not ... at All,” “Not ... in the Slightest,” “Absolutely Not”

When 全く directly accompanies a negative predicate, it can strengthen the negation to express **total negation**. English commonly uses “not ... at all,” “not ... in the slightest,” “not ... one bit,” or “absolutely not,” depending on the construction.

The key point is that even the slightest trace of what is being denied is ruled out. 全く分からない presents an absence of understanding; 全く知らない, an absence of knowledge; and 全くない, the absence of whatever is under discussion.

*<p class="jp-example">何度説明を聞いても、その仕組みが全く分からない。</p>*
*No matter how many times I hear the explanation, I still don't understand how that mechanism works at all.*

The speaker presents their lack of understanding as total. This is stronger than simply saying they have difficulty understanding: even after repeated explanations, they cannot work out how the mechanism operates.

*<p class="jp-example">その発言に、相手を傷つける意図は全くなかった。</p>*
*I had absolutely no intention of hurting the other person with those words.*

Here, 全く strengthens the negation of intent. The speaker rules out having wanted to hurt the other person, without necessarily commenting on the actual effect of their words: someone may have felt hurt even when no such intention existed.

This example shows how important it is to identify **exactly what is being negated**. Totality applies to the element targeted by the negation, in this case the intention to cause harm.

A negative evaluation may also be built into the meaning of a word, even when the sentence does not end in ～ない:

*<p class="jp-example">こんな曖昧な説明では全くだめだ。</p>*
*Such a vague explanation is no good at all.*

だめだ is grammatically affirmative in form but expresses a judgment of inadequacy. 全く intensifies that judgment: the explanation is rejected as wholly inadequate.

**Total negation and displeasure are nevertheless distinct aspects.** 全く can reinforce a negation in a neutral observation, a clarification, or a denial. Whether irritation is also conveyed depends on the content and the way the sentence is spoken.

### Emphasis on Facts and Judgments: “Really,” “Indeed,” “Truly”

全く can be used to **lend force to a fact, an impression, or the speaker's evaluation**. In this reading, the emphasis concerns how firmly the speaker stands behind what they say.

The most natural English translations are often “really,” “indeed,” or “truly.” The focus shifts from the completeness of a condition to the force with which the speaker presents a judgment or reaction.

*<p class="jp-example">この短期間でこれほど上達するとは、全く大したものだ。</p>*
*Making such progress in so little time is truly impressive.*

Here, 全く intensifies the praise. The combination with ～とは also highlights the speaker's reaction to an achievement that inspires admiration.

*<p class="jp-example">彼が一人で全部準備していたとは、全く驚いた。</p>*
*I was genuinely surprised to learn that he had prepared everything by himself.*

The adverb highlights the surprise felt. In English, “genuinely” or “really” naturally conveys the speaker's personal reaction, whereas “completely” would sound awkward in this context.

*<p class="jp-example">確認もせずに人を犯人扱いするなんて、全くひどい話だ。</p>*
*It's truly outrageous to treat someone like a criminal without even checking the facts.*

In this case, a critical judgment is strengthened. The disapproval comes from the content of the evaluation, while 全く makes it more emphatic.

**The emphatic use can therefore accompany both praise and criticism, admiration and surprise.** The whole expression must be interpreted to identify the attitude being reinforced.

The phrase **全くのところ** belongs to the same broad semantic area of emphasizing what is true or actually the case and can be translated, depending on context, as “to tell the truth,” “in truth,” or “in fact.” It introduces what follows as a frank statement of the actual situation:

*<p class="jp-example">全くのところ、私にも理由は分からない。</p>*
*To tell the truth, I don't know the reason either.*

Here, what matters is how the entire statement is introduced: the speaker frankly sets out what they know—or do not know.

### Full Agreement with a Statement: “Exactly Right,” “I Completely Agree”

In conversation, 全く can express **complete agreement with what another person has said**. The speaker regards the other person's judgment as correct or appropriate, or simply expresses agreement with it.

This sense is especially clear in expressions such as 全くその通りです, “That's exactly right,” and 全く同感です, “I completely agree.” It also appears in the brief replies 全くだ, 全くです, and 全くだね.

*<p class="jp-example">A：辞書の訳だけでは、この二つの言葉の違いは分かりにくいですね。<br />B：全くその通りです。実際の文脈を見る必要があります。</p>*

*A: “It's hard to tell the difference between these two words from dictionary translations alone.”*  
*B: “Exactly right. We need to look at how they're used in real contexts.”*

全く reinforces the recognition that the observation is valid. B fully agrees with A's assessment of the difficulty and adds a related point.

*<p class="jp-example">A：この仕事を一人で全部やるのは大変だね。<br />B：全くだね。誰かに手伝ってもらったほうがいいよ。</p>*

*A: “Doing all this work on your own is tough.”*  
*B: “It really is. You'd be better off getting someone to help.”*

The reply 全くだね can be understood as a confirmation equivalent to “You're absolutely right.” What the speakers agree on can be positive or negative: one can agree with praise just as readily as with a complaint.

Brief replies, however, must be interpreted in context. A negation that can be inferred from the question may be left unspoken:

*<p class="jp-example">A：説明を聞いて、少しは分かった？<br />B：いや、全く。</p>*

*A: “Did you understand at least some of it after hearing the explanation?”*  
*B: “No, not at all.”*

In this last case, 分からなかった, “I didn't understand,” is understood rather than spoken. **The meaning of the reply depends on the preceding utterance and on what has been omitted.** The agreement conveyed by 全くだ・全くです must therefore be distinguished from the elliptical use in which a negation is supplied from context.

### Emphatic Noun Modification with 全くの: “Pure,” “Complete,” “Outright”

In the construction **全くの + noun**, the meaning expressed by the noun is presented in unequivocal, absolute, or unqualified terms. Depending on the noun, English might use expressions such as “pure coincidence,” “a complete beginner,” “a complete fabrication,” or “an outright lie.”

This construction retains its connection with completeness, but the communicative effect varies with the noun: it may stress the absence of intention behind a coincidence, a lack of expertise in a field, or the entirely invented nature of an account.

*<p class="jp-example">あの店で彼に会ったのは、全くの偶然だった。</p>*
*Running into him at that shop was pure coincidence.*

全くの偶然 presents the encounter as entirely coincidental. The speaker rules out its having been arranged or deliberately sought.

*<p class="jp-example">茶道については全くの素人なので、基本から教えていただけると助かります。</p>*
*I'm a complete beginner when it comes to the tea ceremony, so I'd appreciate it if you could start with the basics.*

全くの素人 strengthens the admission of inexperience in the specified field. The speaker presents themselves as lacking specialist knowledge, thereby explaining the request to start with the fundamentals.

*<p class="jp-example">彼の説明は、全くの作り話だった。</p>*
*His explanation was a complete fabrication.*

The explanation is described as entirely made up. 全くの makes this categorization emphatic: the account is presented as entirely fabricated.

Translations such as “pure,” “complete,” or “outright” are therefore **context-dependent ways of conveying the same intensifying function**. “Pure” works well with “coincidence” and “fabrication”; for an inexperienced person, “a complete beginner” or “entirely unfamiliar with the subject” sounds more natural.

Here too, the evaluation depends on the noun and the situation. A coincidence may be fortunate, inexperience may be acknowledged neutrally, and a lie may be condemned with disapproval.

### Irritation, Exasperation, and Disappointment: “Oh, Come On!”, “Seriously?”, “Ugh!”

Finally, 全く can take on an **exclamatory function**, through which the speaker expresses annoyance, exasperation, disappointment, or displeasure.

In this reading, the emotional reaction to the situation is the heart of the expression. English can convey it with “Oh, come on!”, “Seriously?”, “Ugh!”, or another exclamation suited to the context and intensity of the tone.

*<p class="jp-example">まったく、何度同じことを言わせるの！</p>*
*Oh, come on! How many times do I have to tell you the same thing?*

全く introduces an impatient outburst. The speaker conveys exasperation at having to repeat something already said; the adverb thus contributes to the reproachful tone of the entire utterance.

*<p class="jp-example">また鍵を忘れたの？ まったくもう！</p>*
*You forgot your keys again? Seriously?!*

In the combination **まったくもう**, the emotional reaction becomes even stronger. The repetition of the incident is expressed by また, while the exclamation conveys the resulting exasperation.

The exclamation can also concern an event over which the speaker has little control. Whether there is another person to reproach depends on the situation.

*<p class="jp-example">ったく、また電車が遅れてる。</p>*
*Ugh, the train's late again.*

**ったく** is a shortened, colloquial form of まったく, used to express this kind of reaction. Here the annoyance concerns the train's delay and may be voiced even as a comment to oneself.

Its intensity varies with intonation and the relationship between those involved, ranging from a disgruntled mutter to a firm rebuke. Placing it at the beginning or end of an utterance can also make its role as an emotional comment more apparent.

**Emphasis on a judgment and an exclamatory outburst can also overlap.** In a sentence expressing disapproval, 全く may simultaneously intensify what is being said and convey the speaker's irritation. Context, intonation, and the degree to which 全く is uttered independently are therefore crucial when choosing between a translation such as “really” and an exclamation such as “Oh, come on!”

**In its adverbial use, 全く is invariable**: tense, negation, and politeness are expressed by the predicate it accompanies. For example, in 全く分からなかった, “I didn't understand at all,” and 全く分かりません, “I don't understand at all,” the verb form changes, while 全く stays the same.

## Register and Contexts of Use

**全く belongs to standard Japanese and is used across a wide range of contexts**: it appears in expository writing, educational texts, professional communications, and everyday conversation. Its effect, however, varies considerably with the construction, the content of the sentence, and the relationship between the speakers.

To describe its register, we need to distinguish **the type of text**, **the degree of grammatical politeness**, and **the force of the statement**. A formulation may be suitable for a written report and still be highly categorical; an exclamation can convey impatience even within a sentence using です・ます. Choosing 全く therefore requires consideration both of the communicative setting and of the attitude one wishes to express.

### Expository, Academic, and Professional Writing

In descriptive and negative constructions, 全く fits naturally into **explanations, analyses, reports, and arguments**. It can highlight a radical difference or an exact correspondence, or show that a phenomenon is entirely absent within a defined scope.

Teaching materials comparing similar expressions point to a particular association between 全く～ない and more formal situations or written language, whereas 全然～ない is more often associated with everyday conversation. This is a usage tendency, not an absolute rule: both adverbs occur in a variety of contexts.

*<p class="jp-example">今回の調査結果は、当初の予想とは全く異なる。</p>*
*The results of this survey are completely different from the initial predictions.*

The sentence could appear in a report or a discussion of research findings. 全く intensifies the difference, while the vocabulary and structure of the utterance contribute to its expository register.

*<p class="jp-example">調査対象となった資料には、その用例は全く見られなかった。</p>*
*No examples of that usage were found in the materials examined.*

Here, the total negation is restricted to the materials actually analyzed. The wording specifies the scope within which the stated absence holds true.

This is especially useful in argumentative writing: **the force of 全く must match the scope of the claim one intends to make**. Saying that a usage does not appear in the documents examined limits the observation to those documents. Precision thus comes from the combination of adverb, predicate, and specification of the scope under consideration.

In narrative texts, 全く can also appear in the narrator's voice or in a character's thoughts. In literary contexts, the same word can appear in descriptions, personal judgments, and emotional reactions.

### Everyday Conversation and Polite Forms

The use of 全く in formal writing coexists with its natural use in speech. In a casual conversation, it may accompany a categorical observation, a declaration of ignorance, or an expression of agreement.

*<p class="jp-example">この話は全く知らなかったよ。誰から聞いたの？</p>*
*I didn't know anything at all about this. Who told you?*

The conversational tone emerges from the sentence as a whole, particularly the particle よ and the question in the plain form. 全く retains its function of strengthening the negation.

The same adverb can also appear in a more respectful formulation:

*<p class="jp-example">申し訳ありませんが、その件については全く存じません。</p>*
*I'm sorry, but I know absolutely nothing about that matter.*

Politeness is conveyed by the introductory expression and by 存じません, while 全く makes the stated lack of knowledge unequivocal. The utterance may be appropriate when someone wishes to make it unmistakably clear that they have no information; the way it is spoken and how the conversation continues also help determine its effect.

Expressions of agreement likewise adapt to different levels of familiarity. 全くだね can be used in an informal exchange; 全く同感です and 全くその通りです allow the same agreement to be expressed politely.

*<p class="jp-example">その点については、全く同感です。具体例を増やしたほうがよいと思います。</p>*
*I completely agree on that point. I think it would be better to include more concrete examples.*

Here, 全く reinforces a reasoned agreement that would also be appropriate in a work or academic discussion. The additional comment makes the speaker's contribution to the exchange explicit.

**The choice between plain forms, polite forms, and respectful expressions therefore concerns the utterance as a whole.** 全く can accompany all of them while retaining its contribution of completeness or emphasis.

### Strength of a Statement and the Relationship Between Speakers

One important distinction is between **speaking politely** and **softening a judgment**. The endings です・ます add politeness, but the content can still be very forceful.

In its [guidance on keigo usage, the Agency for Cultural Affairs (Bunkachō)](https://www.bunka.go.jp/seisaku/kokugo_nihongo/kokugo_shisaku/keigo/chapter1/detail.html) notes that the respectful effect of an expression also depends on its content, the speaker's attitude, and the situation. This principle helps us evaluate statements reinforced by 全く.

*<p class="jp-example">その説明は全く間違っています。</p>*
*That explanation is completely wrong.*

The sentence has a polite ending but expresses categorical disagreement. Such directness may be intentional in a correction; when addressing someone who has just given the explanation, however, it may come across as a very strong stance.

The practical question, then, is **how broad a judgment one wants to make**. If the criticism concerns only one part of an explanation, specifying that part helps limit the disagreement. If the intention is to reject the entire explanation, 全く makes that force explicit.

The same firmness can have a reassuring effect when the content is favorable to the listener:

*<p class="jp-example">その点については全く問題ありませんので、ご安心ください。</p>*
*There's absolutely no problem with that point, so please don't worry.*

Here, total negation rules out a potential problem and offers reassurance. The emphatic wording reinforces that reassurance.

**The effect of 全く therefore changes according to what is being affirmed, denied, or evaluated.** To interpret it, we need to ask whether the sentence describes a fact, confirms an opinion, rejects a position, or passes judgment on someone's behavior.

### Exclamations, Familiarity, and Shortened Forms

When used as an exclamation, 全く brings the speaker's reaction to the foreground. This use is characteristic of dialogue and the representation of inner thoughts: it can convey exasperation, disappointment, annoyed disbelief, or reproach.

Its intensity varies according to intonation, the situation, and the relationship between the people involved. Between people who know each other well, a sentence may sound like good-natured scolding; when the speakers are less familiar with each other, the same opening may make the annoyance quite apparent.

Exclamatory uses can also occur alongside grammatically polite forms:

*<p class="jp-example">まったく、もう少し静かにしてもらえませんか。</p>*
*Oh, come on! Could you please keep it down a little?*

The request uses ～てもらえませんか, but the opening exclamation makes the impatience clear. The politeness of the construction and the speaker's displeasure coexist in the same utterance.

Research by [Jun (2015)](https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/51853/27594_Dissertation.pdf) documents this possibility and distinguishes exclamatory use from ordinary adverbial intensification. In the examples analyzed, 全く can also appear in a character's inner monologue: the emotional reaction may therefore be directed at the situation or expressed inwardly.

The shortened form **ったく** makes the colloquial character particularly clear:

*<p class="jp-example">ったく、また傘を忘れた。</p>*
*Ugh, I forgot my umbrella again.*

The speaker expresses irritation at their own forgetfulness. The exclamation forms part of a spontaneous comment, even without another person to reproach.

まったくもう and ったくもう can also heighten exasperation. In professional communication, these forms would draw attention to a strong personal reaction; in dialogue, informal messages, or narrative writing, they can instead help convey a speaker's voice and attitude.

In the combination まったくもう, もう also contributes to the expression of annoyance or exasperation, taking on an exclamatory meaning close to “Ugh!” or “Enough already!” This use exists alongside its temporal meanings and is discussed in our article on the [differences between もう, すでに, and 今更](/en/articles/sudeni-mou-imasara-difference-usage-japanese-grammar/), which provides a fuller picture of its range of uses.




### What Corpus Studies Reveal

Differences in register are also supported by corpus-based research. The [BCCWJ, Balanced Corpus of Contemporary Written Japanese](https://clrd.ninjal.ac.jp/bccwj/) covers different genres, including books, magazines, newspapers, and web texts, making it possible to study words in their written contexts.

In [Liu's (2019) study](https://repository.ninjal.ac.jp/record/2589/files/LRW2019_25_O-3-4.pdf), the register of まったく, ぜんぜん, すこしも, and ちっとも is examined using eight grammatical parameters (or morphosyntactic indicators). In the analysis of negative constructions, まったく falls toward the more formal end of the spectrum. The factors considered include predicate type, sentence endings, and the presence of colloquial forms.

The finding describes **a relative tendency in the data analyzed**. To apply it when reading, we must distinguish the ordinary use of 全く with a predicate from agreement replies and exclamatory outbursts. An adverb's overall behavior across texts can coexist with conversational uses that have a very different tone.

The nature of the materials must also be considered: dialogue within a novel is a written representation of conversation. Observations drawn from such texts should be understood in relation to the type of data they come from.

### Spelling: 全く, まったく, and ったく

**全く and まったく are two ways of writing the same word**: the former uses kanji, while the latter is written entirely in hiragana. Editorial conventions and document type also influence the choice.

The spelling 全く is included in the [Jōyō Kanji Table](https://www.bunka.go.jp/kokugo_nihongo/sisaku/joho/joho/kijun/naikaku/kanji/joyokanjisakuin/). The [official guidelines for kanji use in government documents](https://www.bunka.go.jp/kokugo_nihongo/sisaku/joho/joho/kijun/sanko/koyobun/pdf/kunrei.pdf) also list 全く among the adverbs that should, in principle, be written in kanji.

This is a writing convention associated with a particular context. In general prose, the choice between 全く and まったく may depend on the publication's style. The descriptive, emphatic, or exclamatory meaning must be identified from the construction and context: the presence of kanji or hiragana alone is not enough to determine it.

**ったく, by contrast, represents a shortened pronunciation**, associated with colloquial language. Its use can therefore help characterize speech as represented in writing, a spontaneous comment, or a personal reaction. The full form is normally used in expository explanations; in dialogue, retaining the abbreviation can help make a character's voice more distinctive.

## Usage Examples

### Completeness and Totality

*<p class="jp-example">別々に作業したのに、二人が描いた図は細部まで全く同じだった。</p>*
*Although they worked separately, the drawings the two of them produced were identical down to the smallest detail.*

**Commentary:** 全く同じ expresses complete correspondence. 細部まで, “down to the smallest detail,” specifies the extent of the correspondence between the two drawings.

*<p class="jp-example">見た目はよく似ているが、この二つの漢字は意味が全く異なる。</p>*
*These two kanji look very similar, but their meanings are completely different.*

**Commentary:** 全く applies to the difference in meaning, while the visual similarity remains. Totality concerns the particular aspect identified in the sentence.

### Total Negation

*<p class="jp-example">会議の内容は覚えているが、誰がその案を出したのかは全く思い出せない。</p>*
*I remember what was discussed in the meeting, but I can't for the life of me remember who put forward that proposal.*

**Commentary:** The total negation is limited to the identity of the person who made the proposal. The speaker remembers other things about the meeting but cannot retrieve that particular piece of information.

*<p class="jp-example">一週間待っても先方から返事が全くなかったので、こちらからもう一度連絡した。</p>*
*After a week without any reply from them, we contacted them again ourselves.*

**Commentary:** 全くなかった emphasizes the complete absence of replies during the period in question. This observation explains the decision to make contact again.

### Emphasis on Facts and Judgments

*<p class="jp-example">半年足らずでこれほど難しい曲を弾きこなせるようになるとは、全く見事なものだ。</p>*
*To be able to play such a difficult piece so skillfully in less than six months is truly impressive.*

**Commentary:** 全く reinforces an admiring judgment. Surprise at the rapid progress is also conveyed by ～とは; “truly” captures the speaker's emphasis here.

*<p class="jp-example">資料を読みもせずに結論を出すなんて、全くあきれた話だ。</p>*
*Drawing conclusions without even reading the materials is truly appalling.*

**Commentary:** The adverb makes the negative judgment expressed by あきれた話だ more forceful. The speaker strongly disapproves of behavior they regard as careless.

### Full Agreement with a Statement

*<p class="jp-example">「便利さだけでなく、安全性も考えるべきではないでしょうか。」「全くその通りです。判断の基準を見直しましょう。」</p>*
*“Shouldn't we consider safety as well as convenience?” “You're absolutely right. Let's reconsider our evaluation criteria.”*

**Commentary:** 全くその通りです expresses complete agreement with the other person's observation. The following proposal shows that this agreement leads to a concrete decision.

*<p class="jp-example">「締め切りの直前になって予定を変えられると困るよね。」「全くだね。もっと早く知らせてほしかったよ。」</p>*
*“It's a real problem when they change the plans right before the deadline, isn't it?” “You're absolutely right. I wish they'd told me sooner.”*

**Commentary:** 全くだね confirms the judgment just expressed. What the speakers share is a complaint, but the reply still serves to express agreement.

### Categorical Qualification with 全くの

*<p class="jp-example">同じ日に同じ本を買っていたのは全くの偶然で、事前に相談したわけではない。</p>*
*It was pure coincidence that we'd bought the same book on the same day; we hadn't arranged it beforehand.*

**Commentary:** 全くの偶然 presents the coincidence as entirely fortuitous. The following clarification rules out the possibility that the purchases had been coordinated.

*<p class="jp-example">陶芸については全くの素人ですが、以前から一度やってみたいと思っていました。</p>*
*I'm a complete beginner when it comes to pottery, but I've wanted to give it a try for a long time.*

**Commentary:** 全くの reinforces the condition expressed by 素人, presenting the speaker as someone with no training in the field. The judgment is limited to pottery.

### Irritation, Exasperation, and Disappointment

*<p class="jp-example">まったく、何度言ったら分かるの？使った物は元の場所に戻してよ。</p>*
*Oh, come on! How many times do I have to tell you before you understand? Put things back where they belong after you've used them.*

**Commentary:** まったく introduces an irritated rebuke. “Oh, come on!” conveys the impatience of someone who feels they are having to repeat a request they have already made.

*<p class="jp-example">ったく、急いでいるときに限ってプリンターが動かない。</p>*
*For goodness' sake, the printer never seems to work when I'm in a hurry.*

**Commentary:** The shortened form ったく signals a colloquial outburst. The annoyance concerns an inconvenience and may be expressed even when speaking to oneself.

## Conclusions

Studying 全く is above all about recognizing **the force and scope of what is being said**. Using it appropriately means first identifying what is being emphasized and then considering the effect of the utterance in context. The completeness of a description, the categorical force of a denial, and the speaker's emotional involvement can all be expressed by the same word, through different constructions and contexts.

To make its use second nature, it is worth starting by memorizing **short, complete expressions** such as 全く分からない, 全く同じ, 全くの偶然, and 全くその通りです, associating each with a sentence and a recognizable situation. This helps build a repertoire of real-world usage patterns that can gradually be expanded through reading and listening. For each example collected, it is useful to note what is being reinforced and what attitude comes across.

One practical exercise is to compare the same expression **with and without 全く**. For example, 分からない and 全く分からない both express a lack of understanding, but the second presents that lack of understanding as total. Comparing them helps isolate the adverb's contribution and assess when that added force is genuinely needed. In writing, too, it can be helpful to reread a sentence after temporarily removing 全く to see what difference it makes to the meaning or emphasis.

In spoken Japanese, it is also important to consider what was said before, along with pauses and intonation. Responses expressing agreement and exclamatory outbursts are easier to interpret in the context of a full exchange. It is therefore useful to practice the same expression in short dialogues, working out who is speaking, whom they are addressing, and what reaction they want to convey.

When comparing similar expressions, it is important to check **the entire combination**, because **similar English translations do not guarantee that the expressions can be used in the same ways in Japanese**.

When in doubt, consult a usage dictionary and compare several authentic examples, paying attention to the genres in which they appear. Corpora can be particularly helpful in identifying these associations and contextual differences.

Finally, it is useful to distinguish recognition from active use: forms such as ったく can be understood even at an early stage, but using them appropriately requires greater familiarity with tone and the relationships between speakers. The aim is to learn to choose 全く when the sense of completeness or emphasis it conveys truly matches what you intend to express.
