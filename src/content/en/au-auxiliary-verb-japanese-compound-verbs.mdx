**What does ～合う (あう, au) mean in Japanese compound verbs, how is it used, and how does it change the meaning of the base verb?**


The auxiliary verb **合う (あう, au)** is used to form **compound verbs expressing reciprocity, participation in a shared action, or the coming together of multiple elements**. When attached to another verb, it can indicate that **two or more people act toward one another**, as in *“helping each other,”* or that several elements meet, mix, or come together. 



Its meaning must therefore be interpreted in relation to the verb it combines with: *“each other,”* *“mutually,”* and *“together”* capture some of its central meanings, but individual compounds may call for different translations.

These uses become easier to understand if we begin with the meaning of **合う as an independent verb**, which includes the ideas of “joining,” “fitting together,” “matching,” and “being in agreement.” 

The common thread is the relationship between two or more elements, which may come together physically, prove compatible, or be in agreement. In compound verbs, this relationship is expressed through the action or process denoted by the first verb: it may take the form of an exchange between people, participation in a shared activity, or the mixing or overlapping of things.

In terms of formation, **合う attaches to the 連用形 (れんようけい) of a verb**, usually recognizable as the stem used before ます: 話す becomes 話し, giving **話し合う (はなしあう)**; 助ける becomes 助け, giving **助け合う (たすけあう)**. 合う thus forms the second element of a compound verb, helping specify the relationship between the participants in the action.

The difference from the base verb becomes clear when we compare **助ける**, “to help,” with **助け合う**, “to help each other”: the former can describe one person helping another, whereas the latter presents the help as mutual. 

Similarly, **話す** means “to speak” or “to tell,” while **話し合う** foregrounds the exchange between speakers and can mean “to discuss,” “to exchange views,” or “to consult one another” about a matter, sometimes with the aim of finding a solution. This does not mean that 話す necessarily describes one-way communication: the compound makes the interaction explicit and has established uses of its own.

Other common compounds include **愛し合う (あいしあう)**, “to love each other,” **見つめ合う (みつめあう)**, “to gaze at each other,” and **殴り合う (なぐりあう)**, “to exchange punches.” These examples show that reciprocity can involve feelings, looks, or physical actions, and can characterize an affectionate relationship just as readily as a confrontation. 

The relationship expressed by 合う does not, therefore, necessarily imply cooperation, harmony, or agreement between the people involved.

Its use also extends beyond deliberate human actions. In **混ざり合う (まざりあう)**, “to mix together,” and **重なり合う (かさなりあう)**, “to overlap,” the compound describes a relationship that develops between several elements, such as colors, substances, or layers. Here the connection to the basic meaning of 合う is particularly clear: the elements mix or overlap with one another. 

The translation “together” must therefore be interpreted in light of the process being described; the mere fact that several actions take place simultaneously is not, by itself, enough to explain this construction.

**合う provides a productive pattern of verb formation**, provided the combination makes semantic sense. At the same time, knowing its general meaning is not enough to understand every compound: some have developed particular meanings that must be learned as part of their established usage. **知り合う (しりあう)**, for example, commonly means “to meet someone” or “to become acquainted.” 

Studying these verbs therefore means recognizing both the contribution of 合う and the meaning of the compound as a whole.

In this article, we will explore the **meaning and usage of 合う in Japanese grammar**, then examine its **formation and grammatical structure**. We will go on to analyze **how the meaning of verbs ending in 合う differs from that of their base verbs**, with particular attention to **register and contexts of use**. A section devoted to **usage examples** will show how these meanings work in actual sentences, followed by a **conclusion** that brings together the essential points for understanding and using the construction.

## 合う (あう, au): Meaning and Usage as a Standalone Verb and an Auxiliary

To understand how **合う** is used, it helps to begin with its basic meaning **as an independent verb**, so that we can better appreciate what it contributes to the formation of **compound verbs**. 

The ideas of joining, matching, and agreement found in its independent uses provide a starting point for interpreting the reciprocal relationships or shared participation expressed by many compounds ending in ～合う.

We will therefore begin with the main meanings and uses of **合う (あう)**, alongside its transitive counterpart **合わせる (あわせる)**, which expresses the act of bringing elements together, making them match, or bringing them into agreement. From there, we will turn to **合う as an auxiliary verb**, examining how it interacts with the meaning of the verb it attaches to and what relationships it can express between the participants in the action or process being described.

### The Verb 合う (and 合わせる): Meaning and Function

As an independent verb, **合う (あう)** is an **intransitive godan verb** with several meanings, including “to join,” “to fit together,” “to match,” “to be compatible,” “to be suitable,” and “to meet.” 

These meanings can be understood through a shared idea: **two or more elements are related through union, correspondence, or agreement**. This relationship may be physical, as when two parts fit together, or abstract, as when two opinions coincide or something meets a particular criterion.

In its most concrete sense, 合う can describe elements that **come together and join**. One example is two rivers converging at the same point:

*<p class="jp-example">二つの川がこの地点で合う。</p>*

*The two rivers meet at this point*.

Here the union is physical: two previously separate elements come together.

In other cases, the verb expresses a **match in shape, size, or characteristics**. A key may fit a lock, a shoe may fit someone's foot, and two components may fit together. The meaning therefore depends on the elements being related:

*<p class="jp-example">この靴は私の足に合っている。</p>*

*These shoes fit my feet well*.

Here 合う describes the compatibility between the shoes and the wearer's feet. The same idea of suitability appears when something meets certain conditions or is appropriate to a particular situation.

The match may also involve **tastes, sensibilities, and ways of thinking**. **気が合う (きがあう)** means to get along or be on the same wavelength because two people share similar feelings or outlooks. **口に合う (くちにあう)**, by contrast, refers to food or drink that suits someone's tastes. In both cases, 合う expresses compatibility, but the nature of that compatibility changes with the context.

Another use concerns **agreement or coordination**: **意見が合う (いけんがあう)** means that opinions agree, while **息が合う (いきがあう)** describes people who act in sync, with well-coordinated timing or rhythm. The idea of agreement can therefore concern both what people think and how they carry out an activity.

When the point of reference is a standard of correctness, 合う can also mean **“to be right” or “to be correct.”** **答えが合っている (こたえがあっている)** means that an answer is correct; when used of a clock, **合っている** means that it shows the right time. The standard of comparison may remain implicit: an answer is correct in relation to the solution, and a clock in relation to the actual time.

The relationship between two elements can also be assessed in terms of whether something is worthwhile. In **割に合う (わりにあう)**, the result or benefit justifies the cost, time, or effort involved. The negative form **割に合わない** often corresponds to “it's not worth it”: what is gained does not make up for what must be invested.

The intransitive **合う** has a corresponding **ichidan transitive verb, 合わせる (あわせる)**. The connection in meaning is especially clear in senses such as **“to join,” “to make fit,” “to match,” “to adjust,” and “to bring into agreement”**: with 合わせる, the subject performs an action that causes one or more elements to come together or correspond. 

The *Daijisen* describes its original meaning in precisely these terms: making something fit or coincide.

This verb, too, applies to both concrete and abstract situations. **手を合わせる (てをあわせる)** means to put one's hands together, palm to palm; **力を合わせる (ちからをあわせる)** means to join forces. 

In **時計を合わせる (とけいをあわせる)**, on the other hand, a clock is adjusted so that it shows the correct time. 合わせる can also mean bringing quantities or people together, hence the use of **合わせて** to mean “altogether” or “in total.”

The difference between the two verbs becomes clear when we look at how the same situation is presented:

*<p class="jp-example">この時計は合っている。</p>*

*This clock shows the correct time*.

*<p class="jp-example">時計を正しい時刻に合わせる。</p>*

*I set the clock to the correct time*.

In the first sentence, **合う describes the correctness of the time shown**; in the second, **合わせる expresses the action of setting the clock**. The clock is presented as the topic with は in the first sentence and as the object being acted upon with を in the second. 

The distinction concerns how the sentence is constructed and the situation portrayed: the fact that 合う is intransitive does not imply that the match must arise spontaneously. A clock may show the correct time precisely because someone has set it.

With 合わせる, moreover, **one element may serve as the reference point for adjusting another**. In **自分の予定を相手の予定に合わせる**, “to adjust one's plans to suit the other person's schedule,” the schedule marked by に serves as the reference point, while the plans marked by を are changed. The verb can therefore also express adapting to conditions that have already been established.

The relationship between 合う and 合わせる must nevertheless be interpreted in light of individual expressions. For example, while **答えが合っている** means that an answer is correct, **答えを合わせる** can mean comparing answers to check whether they match. In this usage, 合わせる describes a process of comparison and checking, without inherently implying that the answers are being corrected.


### 合う as an Auxiliary Verb: Meaning and Usage

When 合う **is used as an auxiliary verb**, the relational meaning already present in its independent use is extended to the action or state expressed by the other verb.

The idea of joining or correspondence thus helps explain meanings such as **“doing something to each other,”** **“taking part in an action together,”** or **“entering into a reciprocal relationship.”** 

The contribution of 合う centers on the connection between the participants: the action is presented as an exchange, a shared experience, or a process involving multiple elements in relation to one another.

Its most characteristic meaning is **reciprocity**. 

In **助け合う (たすけあう)**, “to help each other,” the people involved both give and receive help; in **愛し合う (あいしあう)**, “to love each other,” the feeling is mutual. The meaning of the compound therefore encompasses both directions of the relationship: one person acts toward the other, who in turn performs an action of the same kind toward the first person. 

This relationship can also involve a group: 助け合う can describe mutual support among members of a family or community, rather than being limited to two individuals.

Reciprocity can also involve **the exchange of something**. **見せ合う (みせあう)**, for example, means *“to show one another something”*: several people might show each other their photographs, each presenting their own to the others. The compound emphasizes the reciprocal nature of showing, even though what each participant shows may be different.

This relationship does not necessarily involve cooperation or harmony. **憎み合う (にくみあう)** means “to hate each other,” while **殴り合う (なぐりあう)** means “to exchange punches”: hostile feelings and confrontational actions can also be reciprocal.

The connection to the independent meaning of 合う must therefore be understood without assuming that every compound implies positive agreement. What these uses have in common is the relationship between the participants, which can be expressed through either mutual assistance or conflict.

Alongside reciprocity, some compounds primarily express **participation in a shared experience**. **喜び合う (よろこびあう)** means “to rejoice together,” for instance by sharing the joy of a victory or good news. 

Here the meaning is not that each person directs the same action toward another, as happens with helping or hitting: it is the joy that is shared. The idea of coming together is expressed through emotional participation in the same event.

It is therefore useful to distinguish **reciprocal exchange from sharing an experience**. The translations “each other” and “together” capture different aspects of the meaning of 合う and should be chosen according to the compound. 

“Helping each other” emphasizes mutual support; “rejoicing together” expresses shared joy. Simply having several people present, or having them perform an activity at the same time, does not fully account for the meaning of the construction.

In other compounds, the connection to the concrete idea of joining remains particularly clear. **混ざり合う (まざりあう)** means “to mix together”: it can describe substances, colors, or smells blending with one another, for example. Here the relationship concerns the elements involved in the process and does not require people acting deliberately. 

Compared with uses that express an exchange between people, 合う retains a meaning closer to the convergence or mixing of multiple elements.

The resulting verb must also be understood **as a whole**. **話し合う (はなしあう)** can refer to speaking to each other, but it also has the established meaning of “discussing a matter,” “exchanging views,” or “consulting one another,” with participants putting forward their positions in an effort to find a solution. Reciprocity thus takes the form of an exchange of ideas. 

The verb may describe a discussion aimed at reaching an agreement, without guaranteeing that an agreement is actually reached.

Similarly, **知り合う (しりあう)** is commonly used to mean **“to become acquainted” or “to meet someone.”** The compound expresses the beginning of a personal relationship between people. 

Recognizing the contribution of 合う helps explain its meaning, but the most natural English translation depends on the usage of the entire verb. “Mutually” and “together” are therefore useful guides, not translations to apply mechanically to every compound.

This range of meanings does not, however, mean that **合う can be freely attached to any verb**. The combination must allow an interpretation consistent with the relationship expressed by 合う; how natural it sounds depends on the verb's meaning and the context. The mere fact that an activity can be carried out by several people does not automatically make the resulting compound natural. The principles governing these possibilities will be examined in greater depth in the section on formation and grammatical structure.

## 合う: Grammatical Description and Formation Rules

Grammatically, **合う forms the second element of a compound verb (複合動詞, ふくごうどうし)**. It attaches to a specific form of the preceding verb and helps create a single verbal unit. To use the construction correctly, we need to distinguish **how the compound is formed**, **how it is conjugated**, and **which combinations sound natural in Japanese**.

### The Connecting Form: 連用形 + 合う

The basic construction is:

**Verb in the 連用形 + 合う**

Like the auxiliary verb [～込む (komu)](/en/articles/auxiliary-verb-komu-in-japanese-meaning-usage/), which we discuss in a separate article, ～合う attaches to a verb's 連用形, or *masu* stem. Although the two follow the same formation pattern, they change the meaning of the base verb in very different ways.

A practical approach is therefore to start with the ます-form, remove ます, and add 合う: **話します → 話し → 話し合う**.

How this stem is formed depends on the verb class:

- With **godan verbs**, the final mora changes from the -u vowel series to the corresponding -i series: 話す becomes 話し, and 送る becomes 送り;
- With **ichidan verbs**, the final る is removed: 助ける becomes 助け, and 見つめる becomes 見つめ;
- With **verbs ending in する**, する becomes し: 理解する becomes 理解し.

These changes concern the connecting form; whether they yield a natural compound also depends on meaning and usage, as we will see below.

### Conjugating Compound Verbs Ending in 合う

Once the compound has been formed, **conjugation takes place in the final element, 合う**, a godan verb ending in う. The compound therefore follows this pattern even when the original verb belongs to another class: **助ける is ichidan, but 助け合う conjugates as a godan verb**. The stem 助け remains unchanged, while the ending changes.

Tense, negation, and politeness are therefore expressed through the final element of the compound: for example, **話し合った** is the past form of 話し合う, while **助け合わない** is the negative of 助け合う. The て-form also allows the verb to be followed by other constructions, as in **助け合っている**, “to be helping each other,” or **話し合ってください**, “please talk it over.”

### Which Verbs Can Combine with 合う?

**Forming a compound correctly does not, by itself, guarantee that it will sound natural.** The pattern is productive, but the meaning of the verb and the context must support an interpretation consistent with the function of 合う. Simply being able to do an activity with other people does not automatically make a particular combination appropriate.

One particularly clear possibility involves verbs describing **actions or attitudes directed toward another person**: helping, encouraging, teaching, showing something, or understanding someone can all be presented as reciprocal relationships. 

Examples include **助け合う**, **励まし合う**, **教え合う**, **見せ合う**, and **理解し合う**. Feelings such as love and hatred can also be mutual, as shown by **愛し合う** and **憎み合う**: the construction thus encompasses emotional and psychological relationships as well.

**The preceding verb can be either transitive or intransitive.** Intransitive examples include **喜び合う**, which expresses shared joy, and compounds such as **混ざり合う**, in which several elements take part in a process of mixing. 

There is therefore no general requirement for the verb to take a direct object or describe a voluntary action: what matters is the relationship expressed by the combination as a whole.

The choice also depends on **combinations established in the language**. For example, with 待つ, “to wait,” the verb commonly used for meeting at an agreed place and time is **待ち合わせる (まちあわせる)**, which follows a different pattern of formation. The reciprocal nature of the situation does not automatically make compounds ending in 合う interchangeable with those ending in 合わせる.

### Participants and Particle Use

Reciprocal uses involve **at least two participants**, but they do not necessarily have to appear together as the subject. They may be introduced jointly through a structure such as **AとBが…**, or one participant may be marked as the subject while the other is introduced with **と**, following the pattern **AがBと…**. The subject can also be presented as the topic with は.

*<p class="jp-example">私は兄と助け合っている。</p>*

*My brother and I help each other*.

In this sentence, 私 is introduced as the topic with は, while 兄と identifies the other participant. **Having a singular topic does not, therefore, eliminate reciprocity**. In other contexts, the other participant may be left unstated because they are already known, or the subject may directly refer to a group, such as 学生たち, “the students.”

Expressions such as **互いに (たがいに)** and **お互いに (おたがいに)** can make the reciprocal meaning explicit or emphasize it. They are common with these compounds, but **they are not required parts of the construction**: 助け合う can already express mutual assistance without お互いに.

The relationship between participants does not prevent the sentence from also specifying **what is being exchanged**:

*<p class="jp-example">友達と写真を見せ合った。</p>*

*A friend and I showed each other some photographs*.

Here **友達と** identifies the other participant, while **写真を** indicates what is being shown. Reciprocity concerns the act of showing something to the other person; the photographs remain the object marked by を.

**話し合う** can likewise take an element marked by を to indicate the topic under discussion, as in **将来のことを話し合う**, “to discuss the future.” 

The fact that it is intransitive as an independent verb does not, therefore, automatically determine the behavior of all its compounds: **particles must be chosen according to the compound verb as a whole and the role of each noun in the sentence**.

## Base Verbs and Compounds with 合う: Differences in Meaning

As we have already seen, the fundamental difference between a base verb and its corresponding 合う compound is that **the auxiliary adds or highlights a relationship between the people or elements involved in the action or process expressed by the base verb**.

In reciprocal uses, this means that participants act toward one another: **助ける** means “to help,” while **助け合う** means “to help each other.” The simple verb describes helping someone; the compound expresses a relationship in which the participants both give and receive help.

This relationship may also involve **shared participation**, without a direct exchange: **喜ぶ**, “to rejoice,” becomes **喜び合う**, “to rejoice together” or “to share in the joy.” 

With inanimate elements, the emphasis may instead fall on their coming together or interacting, as in **混ざり合う**, “to mix together.” 合う is therefore not simply a way of saying that several subjects do the same thing: it highlights the connection between their actions or the processes involving them.

**The base verb may already express interaction**, however. In such cases, 合う may primarily serve to emphasize that interaction. In other compounds, the meaning has become specialized, as in **知り合う**, “to meet someone” or “to become acquainted.” We must therefore assess the change verb by verb, as the comparisons below demonstrate.

Adding **合う** can thus make a reciprocal relationship explicit, foreground participation in a shared experience, or contribute to a particular established meaning of the compound. The difference from the base verb must therefore be considered case by case: **the two forms may describe different situations, but they may also present the same situation while drawing attention to different aspects of it**.

### 助ける and 助け合う: Making Reciprocity Explicit

**助ける** describes helping someone; **助け合う**, by contrast, presents the help as a reciprocal relationship between the participants.

*<p class="jp-example">私は友達を助けた。</p>*

*I helped a friend*.

*<p class="jp-example">私は友達と助け合った。</p>*

*A friend and I helped each other*.

The first sentence states that I helped a friend, without specifying whether that friend also helped me. The second directly expresses mutual support: each person helped the other.

**The base verb does not rule out reciprocity in the actual situation; it simply does not express reciprocity on its own.** A broader context could describe an exchange of help using 助ける, but with 助け合う, that relationship is already part of the compound's meaning.

### 話す and 話し合う: From Conversation to Discussion

The comparison between **話す** and **話し合う** requires more care because **話す can already describe a conversation between several people**. The difference is therefore not a rigid distinction between one-way communication and dialogue.

*<p class="jp-example">友達と将来について話した。</p>*

*I talked about the future with a friend*.

*<p class="jp-example">友達と将来について話し合った。</p>*

*I discussed the future with a friend and exchanged views*.

The first sentence reports a conversation about the future. That conversation, too, may involve questions, answers, and exchanges of opinions.

In the second, **話し合う brings the exchange between the speakers more clearly into focus**, as they put forward ideas and points of view on the subject. Depending on the context, that discussion may be intended to clarify an issue, make a decision, or find a solution. 

The compound can also describe a relaxed exchange: its use does not always require a problem to solve or a final decision to reach.

### 喜ぶ and 喜び合う: From Joy to Shared Joy

With **喜ぶ**, the focus is on the joy felt by the subjects; with **喜び合う**, the emphasis is on their participation in a shared moment of happiness.

*<p class="jp-example">選手たちは優勝を喜んだ。</p>*

*The players were delighted to win the championship*.

*<p class="jp-example">選手たちは優勝を喜び合った。</p>*

*The players shared the joy of winning the championship*.

The presence of several people in the first sentence is not enough, in itself, to express that they shared the feeling: we know the players were happy, but the verb does not specifically emphasize the relationship between them.

**喜び合う, on the other hand, presents that joy as a shared experience.** The contribution of 合う therefore concerns how the participants experience the feeling together. The compound does not simply indicate that each person feels a greater degree of joy.

### 競う and 競い合う: Emphasizing an Existing Relationship

In some cases, the base verb already contains the idea of a competitive relationship between participants. **競う**, “to compete,” is one such example: competition already presupposes the presence of other competitors. **競い合う** can place further emphasis on this reciprocal rivalry while retaining a meaning close to that of the original verb.

*<p class="jp-example">二人は速さを競った。</p>*

*The two competed to see who was faster*.

*<p class="jp-example">二人は速さを競い合った。</p>*

*The two competed to see who was faster, each trying to outdo the other*.

Both sentences describe a competition between two people. In the second formulation, 合う places greater emphasis on their rivalry with one another.

This example shows why **the presence of 合う does not always introduce an entirely new meaning**. Sometimes the compound makes a relationship already implied by the base verb more prominent, without automatically suggesting a more intense, longer, or more difficult competition.

### 混ざる and 混ざり合う: Highlighting the Relationship Between Elements

A similar semantic overlap can be seen between **混ざる** and **混ざり合う**. Both can describe mixing; the compound places particular emphasis on the way the different elements mix with one another.

*<p class="jp-example">赤と青の絵の具が混ざった。</p>*

*The red and blue paints mixed together*.

*<p class="jp-example">赤と青の絵の具が混ざり合った。</p>*

*The red and blue paints blended together*.

Both sentences involve multiple elements: **混ざる does not need 合う to describe different substances mixing together**. The compound makes the relationship between those elements within the process more explicit.

The distinction can therefore be subtle, and a natural English translation might be identical for both sentences. The presence of 合う does not require us to attribute greater intensity or completeness to the result.

### 知る and 知り合う: When the Meaning of the Entire Verb Changes

The contribution of 合う may also be part of an established lexical meaning. This is the case with **知り合う**, commonly used to mean “to meet someone” or “to become acquainted,” in contrast with **知る**, which encompasses knowing, learning, or finding out about something.

*<p class="jp-example">その俳優のことは以前から知っていた。</p>*

*I had known who that actor was for some time*.

*<p class="jp-example">その俳優とは去年知り合った。</p>*

*I met that actor last year*.

The first sentence is compatible with indirect knowledge: I might know who that actor is from their films or from things I have read, without ever having met them personally. **知る can, of course, also refer to knowing someone personally**, but it does not require that interpretation in every use.

The second sentence, with 知り合う, refers to the point at which a personal acquaintance began. In English, we can express the distinction by contrasting **“knowing who someone is”** with **“meeting someone” or “becoming acquainted with them”**: the compound has a meaning of its own that goes beyond mechanically adding “each other” to the translation of the base verb.

## Common Verbs Ending in 合う and What They Mean

Verbs ending in **合う** express different kinds of relationships: reciprocal exchange, support, sharing, competition, or contact between multiple elements. The following selection also includes compounds with established meanings that are worth learning as individual words.

### Communication, Understanding, and Sharing Knowledge

- **話し合う (はなしあう)**: to discuss, exchange views, or consult one another about an issue. It emphasizes interaction between speakers, often with the aim of making a decision or finding a solution.

- **語り合う (かたりあう)**: to talk with one another, exchanging stories, thoughts, or reflections. It can describe, for example, a conversation in which people share memories, experiences, or opinions.

- **言い合う (いいあう)**: to say things to each other or exchange comments and impressions. It can also mean “to argue” or “to bicker” when the exchange becomes confrontational.

- **教え合う (おしえあう)**: to teach or explain things to one another, sharing what each participant knows with the others.

- **学び合う (まなびあう)**: to learn from one another through discussion and the exchange of knowledge, experiences, and perspectives.

- **見せ合う (みせあう)**: to show one another something, such as photographs, drawings, or work one has done. Each person shows something to the others and looks at what they show in return.

- **理解し合う (りかいしあう)**: to understand each other, making an effort to grasp the other person's thoughts, reasons, or feelings.

### Help, Support, and Sharing

- **助け合う (たすけあう)**: to help each other. The people involved both give and receive help, supporting one another in times of need or difficulty.

- **支え合う (ささえあう)**: to support each other materially, practically, or emotionally. It can describe the mutual support that enables people to face a difficult situation together.

- **励まし合う (はげましあう)**: to encourage one another, giving each other confidence, courage, or motivation to face difficulties and keep going.

- **認め合う (みとめあう)**: to recognize one another's worth, abilities, or standing. It can also involve acknowledging and accepting differences.

- **譲り合う (ゆずりあう)**: to give way to one another, yielding a seat, a turn, or the right of way. It can also mean meeting each other halfway through mutual concessions.

- **分け合う (わけあう)**: to divide something among several people so that everyone receives a share, as when sharing out food.

- **分かち合う (わかちあう)**: to share something with others. It can be used for goods and resources, but also for experiences, joys, sorrows, and feelings.

- **出し合う (だしあう)**: to pool contributions from several people, such as money, ideas, or suggestions. Each person offers something that becomes part of a shared undertaking.

### Feelings, Gazes, and Reciprocal Gestures

- **愛し合う (あいしあう)**: to love each other. It describes mutual love between the people involved.

- **見つめ合う (みつめあう)**: to gaze at each other or look steadily at one another.

- **抱き合う (だきあう)**: to hug or embrace each other.

- **喜び合う (よろこびあう)**: to rejoice together, sharing the joy of an event, an achievement, or good news.

### Conflict and Competition

- **憎み合う (にくみあう)**: to hate each other. The hostile feeling exists on both sides of the relationship.

- **殴り合う (なぐりあう)**: to hit each other or exchange punches. It describes a reciprocal exchange of blows.

- **競い合う (きそいあう)**: to compete or vie with one another, each trying to outperform the others or achieve better results.

- **取り合う (とりあう)**: to fight or compete over something, with each person trying to claim it. In **手を取り合う**, it means “to hold hands”; **取り合わない**, by contrast, can mean “to pay no attention” or “to disregard.”

### Contact, Mixing, and Overlapping

- **触れ合う (ふれあう)**: to touch or come into contact. It can refer to physical contact, but also to human encounters and exchanges through which a connection develops.

- **混ざり合う (まざりあう)**: to mix together. It describes multiple elements, such as substances, colors, or smells, becoming mixed.

- **重なり合う (かさなりあう)**: to overlap. It may refer to physical objects, images, sounds, or other phenomena that overlap.

- **向き合う (むきあう)**: to face one another, with each person turned toward the other. Figuratively, it also means to confront a reality or issue and give it one's attention.

### Compounds with Established Meanings

With these verbs, the meaning of the whole word deserves particular attention: the presence of 合う cannot simply be translated as “each other.”

- **知り合う (しりあう)**: to meet or become acquainted with someone; to get to know each other. It refers to the beginning of a personal acquaintance between the people involved.

- **付き合う (つきあう)**: to spend time with someone or maintain a relationship with them. It can refer to a romantic relationship, but also to keeping someone company or joining them in an activity.

- **落ち合う (おちあう)**: to meet up at a particular place, often by prior arrangement. It can also describe rivers joining together.

- **似合う (にあう)**: to suit someone or look good on them, matching their appearance or characteristics. It is commonly used with clothes, colors, and accessories, but also applies in other situations.

- **釣り合う (つりあう)**: to be balanced, proportionate, or well matched. It can refer to weights and quantities, but also to harmony between elements or the correspondence between levels and characteristics.

## 合う: Contexts of Use and Register

**Compounds ending in 合う are used in both conversation and writing, from everyday interactions to professional and institutional contexts.** The presence of 合う does not, in itself, make an expression more formal, colloquial, or polite: its main contribution concerns the relationship between the participants in the action or process. 

To assess register, we therefore need to consider **the meaning of the individual compound, the wording of the sentence, and the relationship between the speakers**. The range of uses, from instructional dialogues to public documents, shows that the construction is not restricted to any single communicative setting.

### In Everyday Conversation and at Work

Among family and friends, and in other personal relationships, these verbs can describe exchanges, mutual support, and shared experiences. 

**見せ合う** can refer to two friends showing each other photographs; **励まし合う** to mutual encouragement before an exam; and **話し合う** to a conversation in which people work through an issue together. The subject matter may be ordinary and concrete, without requiring an especially solemn tone.

The same compounds can also occur in the workplace: **話し合う** can be used to discuss a problem with colleagues, while **意見を出し合う** can be used to gather everyone's input.

In these cases, the choice highlights discussion and participation among the people involved. **話し合う**, in particular, covers both relaxed conversation and the exchange of opinions aimed at addressing or resolving an issue; the context makes clear which meaning is relevant.

### In Education and Public Communication

In schools and other educational settings, compounds such as **教え合う**, **学び合う**, and **認め合う** allow learning and living together to be presented as experiences to which everyone contributes. 

The Japan Foundation, for example, uses 教え合う and 学び合う to describe activities in which participants exchange knowledge and learn from one another. School projects also use expressions that connect learning together with acknowledging one another's differences.

In public communication, **助け合う** can serve as a call for solidarity. Japan's Fire and Disaster Management Agency, for example, uses it to explain mutual assistance within a community in the event of a disaster. 

In an appeal such as **助け合おう**, “let's help each other,” the exhortative tone comes from the sentence as a whole and its communicative purpose.

### Politeness and the Relationship with the Listener

**合う is not an honorific or humble form.** The appropriate level of politeness is expressed through the normal resources of Japanese, including ます-forms. The reciprocity of the action remains separate from how the speaker addresses the listener.

Compare these two formulations:

*<p class="jp-example">この件について、もう一度話し合おう。</p>*

*Let's discuss this matter again*.

*<p class="jp-example">この件について、もう一度話し合いましょう。</p>*

*Let's discuss this matter again*.

The first is a direct suggestion that may sound natural between people who know each other well; the second expresses the invitation politely. **The meaning of 話し合う remains the same**: what changes is how the listener is invited to take part. The choice depends on the situation and the relationship between the people, following the usual distinctions between plain and polite styles.

The plain style also appears in writing: its presence in a text is not, by itself, enough to identify the text as colloquial. The school report cited above, for example, uses plain forms in a formal institutional text.

### The Expressive Nuances of Individual Compounds

Some compounds can acquire particular expressive force depending on their subject matter. In a narrative, **語り合う** may describe an exchange of memories or personal reflections; **分かち合う**, when used of joys and sorrows, emphasizes emotional sharing. 

These uses do not, however, make either verb exclusively literary: the nuance also depends on what is being discussed and how it is presented.

Likewise, compounds such as **憎み合う** and **殴り合う** describe conflict, without 合う introducing an affectionate or conciliatory tone. **Whether the situation is positive or negative, the degree of emotional involvement, and the level of politeness are separate considerations** that must be assessed in the expression as a whole.

## Usage Examples

The following sentences illustrate reciprocity between people, the sharing of an experience, and interaction between inanimate elements.

*<p class="jp-example">困ったときは、近所の人たちと助け合っています。</p>*
*"Whenever things get difficult, my neighbors and I help each other."*

*<p class="jp-example">明日の予定について、みんなで話し合いましょう。</p>*
*"Let's all discuss tomorrow's plans."*

*<p class="jp-example">休み時間に、友達とスマホの写真を見せ合った。</p>*
*"During the break, a friend and I showed each other photos on our phones."*

*<p class="jp-example">授業のあと、学生たちは分からなかったところを教え合っていた。</p>*
*"After class, the students helped one another with the parts they hadn't understood."*

*<p class="jp-example">意見が違っても、落ち着いて話せば理解し合えると思います。</p>*
*"I think we can understand each other if we talk calmly, even when we disagree."*

*<p class="jp-example">優勝が決まった瞬間、選手たちは抱き合って喜び合った。</p>*
*"The moment they clinched the championship, the players hugged each other and celebrated together."*

*<p class="jp-example">二人は子どものころから、何をするにも競い合ってきた。</p>*
*"The two have competed over everything since childhood."*

*<p class="jp-example">あの二人は、昔から憎み合っている。</p>*
*"Those two have hated each other for a long time."*

*<p class="jp-example">台所では、コーヒーと焼きたてのパンの香りが混ざり合っていた。</p>*
*"In the kitchen, the smell of coffee mingled with the aroma of freshly baked bread."*

*<p class="jp-example">私たちは、十年前に友人の結婚式で知り合いました。</p>*
*"We met ten years ago at a friend's wedding."*

## Conclusion

Studying 合う reveals how closely **grammar, meaning, and actual usage must be considered together**. The formation follows a recognizable pattern, but interpreting a compound requires understanding the relationship it expresses: a reciprocal action, shared participation, or a process in which several elements come together or interact. 

Some verbs, moreover, have developed a specific established meaning that must be learned with the word itself.

Comparing the compound with its base verb helps us recognize this contribution. Sometimes 合う explicitly introduces reciprocity; at other times it highlights an interaction already possible without the auxiliary. 

**The difference may therefore lie in how a situation is presented**, without necessarily changing all the facts being described. To understand it, we need to look at what the sentence draws attention to, as well as how it is translated into English.

For learners, a useful approach is to **learn each compound together with a short sentence**, rather than memorizing only its translation. Within that sentence, identify who is taking part, what relationship exists between the participants, and whether an object of exchange is also expressed. This helps you learn the meaning and the particles together, without automatically transferring the base verb's grammatical pattern to the compound.

One effective exercise is to choose a pair of verbs already encountered in the article and write two sentences describing a concrete situation: one with the simple verb and one with the compound, adjusting the particles where necessary. 

The task is then to explain **what information becomes explicit or which aspect is given greater emphasis**. If the comparison reveals a difference in meaning, that difference is worth noting too.

It is also useful to distinguish between **recognizing a compound while reading and choosing one when writing or speaking**. When reading, knowing the function of 合う provides an initial clue to interpretation; when speaking or writing, it is better to start with combinations you have already learned and observe how they are used in similar contexts. 

The productivity of the construction also helps learners understand and explore further combinations, provided the overall meaning sounds natural.

Translation, too, can become a way to check your understanding: “each other,” “together,” “with one another,” or a specific English verb will be appropriate in different situations. **Understanding 合う means being able to identify the relationship expressed by the sentence**, even when English conveys it in different words or leaves it implicit.
