**What does 時 (とき, toki) mean in Japanese, and how does it link two clauses? Why, for example, can 食べる時 appear in a sentence about the past, while 食べた時 can be used to talk about the future? And when does 時 suggest "before" (前) or "after" (後)?**



時 (とき, toki) is a noun that can function much like a temporal conjunction in Japanese: it connects two clauses, each describing a situation, and identifies the moment, period, or occasion when something happens.


In the cases just mentioned, とき (toki) is generally translated as “when,” but **this translation can obscure distinctions that Japanese conveys through its choice of verb forms**.


Indeed, translating it invariably as “when” in temporal clauses can be inaccurate and misleading.


For example, changing 食べる時 to 食べた時 can alter the relationship between events: what is expressed in the main clause may take place before the meal, during it, or after it has ended.

With many **action verbs**, when a sentence describes two activities occurring in sequence, the nonpast form before 時 tends to place the main action before the event used as a reference point, giving the sentence a reading close to **前 (まえ, mae)**. The plain past form (た), on the other hand, can present that event as already having taken place, shifting the relationship toward **後 (あと, ato)**. 

Thus, ご飯を食べる時、手を洗う suggests washing one's hands before eating, whereas ご飯を食べた時、手を洗う favors the reading “after eating.” The perspective from which the meal is viewed changes, and with it the point at which we place the handwashing.

This distinction does not necessarily correspond to the difference between past and future relative to the moment of speaking. We can recount a past incident while retaining 食べる before 時, or talk about a future occasion using 食べた. 

**The form of the main clause and the form preceding 時 can serve different temporal functions**: 


- One helps locate the episode in time;
- The other may specify the relationship between the situations being described.

As we will see in detail later, with **verbs of motion** such as 行く, 来る, and 帰る, we also need to consider the different stages of a journey. 行く時 can refer either to the time before departure or to the journey itself: an action can occur before a journey is complete without necessarily occurring before it begins.

By contrast, 行った時 can place the main action after the journey has been completed, once the destination has been reached.

The picture changes with **predicates expressing a state**, such as いる (“to be”), as well as with adjectives and nominal expressions. 日本にいる時 and 日本にいた時 can both refer to a past period during which another event takes place. Here, the past form does not require us to place the main action after the state has ended: meeting someone can happen while we are still in Japan, even when we say 日本にいた時.

Even with actions, moreover, the relationship can be one of simultaneity. We might watch television while eating breakfast, or describe the means of transport used for the very same journey mentioned before 時. The forms ～ている時 and ～ていた時 also allow us to bring an ongoing activity or a continuing state into focus. 

**The tendencies toward 前 and 後 must therefore be interpreted in light of both the predicate's meaning and the content of the sentence as a whole**, rather than treated as automatic equivalences.

The study of 時 is not generally considered an advanced grammar topic, nor should it be treated as a niche area of Japanese grammar. I have nevertheless decided to cover it because grammar books, and textbooks for foreign learners in particular, tend to deal with it only superficially: they focus almost exclusively on cases where translating 時 as “when” sounds natural. 

And yet, even at JLPT N4 level, it is not uncommon to encounter sentences in exercises or reading-comprehension passages where the different tenses and verb forms in clauses linked by 時 introduce temporal nuances that go well beyond a simple translation as “when.”

Without the tools to recognize these distinctions, we risk misinterpreting the sentences. 

At best (if we can call it that), we may miss an important nuance in the temporal relationship between events; at worst, especially when reading a passage, we may misunderstand the sequence of actions, making it difficult to follow the thread of the discussion and continue making sense of the text. In other words, our reading and comprehension can come to a halt.


To understand these mechanisms fully, we will begin with the nominal nature of 時 and how it combines with verbs, adjectives, and nouns. We will then examine combinations of tenses across the two clauses, explaining when they suggest “before,” “after,” or “during,” and why the pattern changes with actions, movement, and states.
 
For convenience, the following sections will repeatedly use the same two or three verbs, making it easier to compare situations according to the tenses chosen.


Our analysis of the examples will also lead us to the role of particles, negative forms, and the distinction between the temporal reference established by 時 and the relationships expressed by ～たら and ～と.

## 時 (とき, toki): Meaning and Usage — From “Time” to “When”


The noun 時（とき, toki） primarily means “time,” “moment,” or “occasion.” 

However, when it is used to connect two clauses, it performs a grammatical function equivalent to that of a subordinating temporal conjunction, generally corresponding to “when” or “at the time when” in English, while always remaining a noun in its own right. This is an important point to keep in mind.

Many texts group 時 and other words such as 後, 前, or けど under labels such as “subordinating conjunctions” (note that the first two are nouns, whereas けど is a conjunction in the strict sense). 

I am not especially fond of applying this label indiscriminately here, because 時, 後, and 前 are not themselves conjunctions as lexical categories, even though the constructions they form can establish a relationship of subordination between two clauses. I therefore prefer the broader term “subordinator.”


In these constructions, 時 thus establishes a subordinate relationship between two clauses, each of which has a specific function:

The subordinate temporal clause, which comes before 時, describes an action, event, or state that provides the temporal reference point against which what is expressed in the main clause is interpreted.

The main clause, by contrast, contains the central information of the sentence: it describes what happens, happened, or will happen in relation to the temporal reference established by the subordinate clause.

In other words, the subordinate clause establishes the temporal reference, while the main clause expresses what happens in relation to that reference.

Consider an example:

*<p class="jp-example">漢字がわからない時、辞書で調べます。</p>*
*When I don't know a kanji, I look it up in the dictionary.*

The sentence can be divided into two parts:

漢字がわからない時: this is the subordinate temporal clause, identifying the situation in which the action described in the following main clause takes place.

辞書で調べます: this is the main clause, expressing the action performed in that situation, namely consulting the dictionary.

It is important to note, however, that 時 does not lose its status as a noun. More precisely, it is the entire construction consisting of the clause preceding 時 and 時 itself that takes on a subordinating function with a temporal meaning.

This distinction becomes particularly important when examining the verb forms that precede 時: depending on their configuration and the context, the relationship between the events described by the two clauses may take on different temporal nuances that cannot always be reduced to simple simultaneity.



Thus, in the constructions we are concerned with, 時 functions as a **formal noun (形式名詞)**.

But why is this so important to remember? 

It is essential not to lose sight of this point, because once we understand that 時 retains its nominal nature, it also becomes clear that **the clause preceding it follows the same structure as Japanese relative clauses**: it appears before the noun and specifies its meaning. 

More precisely, this is a **noun-modifying clause (連体修飾節)**, which in our case identifies the moment or circumstance being referred to.

In short, 時 remains a noun, but its temporal reference is specified by the element or clause that comes before it.


The expression 日本にいた時, for example, literally means “the period when I was in Japan.” Within a sentence, it can simply be rendered as “when I was in Japan.”

*<p class="jp-example">私は日本にいた時、お茶を習いました。</p>*
*When I was in Japan, I studied the tea ceremony.*

日本にいた identifies the period; お茶を習いました tells us what happened during that period. Here お茶を習う refers to studying the tea ceremony.

時 does not necessarily indicate a single instant. It can also refer to a fairly long period, such as one's university years, a stay abroad, or an examination period.

*<p class="jp-example">ひろしは試験の時に風邪をひいた。</p>*
*Hiroshi caught a cold during the exam period.*

With a noun such as 試験, “exam,” the expression 試験の時 identifies the time or period associated with that event. The context clarifies how broad this temporal reference is.

*<p class="jp-example">これは出発の時に渡します。</p>*
*I'll give you this when we leave.*

Here 出発の時 identifies the occasion of departure. On its own, it does not establish a strict contrast between “before” and “after.”

The reading とき should be distinguished from じ, used, for example, in 三時, “three o'clock.” The kanji is the same, but the grammatical construction is different.

## How to form a clause with 時

As we have seen, the principle is **noun modification**: whatever comes before 時 specifies which moment or period we are talking about.

With verbs and い-adjectives, the plain form is normally used. With な-adjectives and nouns, we must instead use the appropriate form for modifying a noun.

<div class="hn-table">

| Element before 時 | Non-past form | Past form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verb | 話す時 | 話した時 |
| い-adjective | 高い時 | 高かった時 |
| な-adjective | 静かな時 | 静かだった時 |
| Noun | 先生の時 | 先生だった時 |

</div>

Depending on context, the expressions in the table mean “when I speak / spoke,” “when it is / was expensive,” “when it is / was quiet,” and “when I am / was a teacher.” These translations are only approximate: especially with verbs, the temporal relationship must be reconstructed from the sentence as a whole.

### Why are な and の used with adjectives and nouns?

時 remains grammatically a noun. That is why 静かだ, which can end a sentence, becomes 静かな before 時, just as it does before other nouns.

Likewise, in the nonpast, nouns connect to 時 with の: 学生の時, “when one is a student,” or 先生の時, “when one is a teacher.” The past form だった can be used: 学生だった時, 先生だった時.

**We do not simply add 時 to a complete sentence and leave the preceding form unchanged in every case:** instead, we build an expression that modifies the noun 時.

This also explains why the plain form before 時 is normal within a polite sentence. In 日本にいた時、お茶を習いました, politeness is conveyed by 習いました; there is no need to change いた to いました.

Negative forms follow the same principle: 話さない時, 話さなかった時, 高くない時, 静かではない時, 学生ではなかった時. Here, too, their temporal meaning depends on the kind of situation being described.

### The subject of a clause with 時: why is が used?

Because the clause before 時 modifies a noun, its explicitly stated subject is normally marked by **が**. This is especially clear when the two clauses have different subjects.

*<p class="jp-example">メアリーは私が日本にいた時、フランスにいた。</p>*
*Mary was in France when I was in Japan.*

メアリーは introduces the topic of the entire sentence: we are talking about Mary. 私が, by contrast, identifies the subject of the situation that specifies 時: I was the one who was in Japan.

The reason is not simply that “two different people require が.” Rather, **the subject’s role within the clause modifying 時** makes が the neutral choice.

When the subject is shared or easily inferred, it is often omitted. In 私は日本にいた時、お茶を習いました, for example, there is no need to repeat 私が before 日本にいた.

## 時 and 時に: what difference does に make?

In the ordinary temporal construction, 時 can be followed by **に**, but the particle can often be omitted.

*<p class="jp-example">私は学生の時、よく勉強した。</p>*
*When I was a student, I studied a lot.*

*<p class="jp-example">私は学生の時によく勉強した。</p>*
*I studied a lot during my student years.*

The two sentences can describe the same situation. に makes the temporal function of the expression explicit: it identifies the period in which what follows takes place. In a suitable context, this can give greater prominence to the time in question, potentially suggesting “it was precisely when I was a student that…”

**The presence of に does not, however, automatically create such strong emphasis.** Often the difference is slight and does not call for two sharply distinct English translations.

Moreover, に does not turn 時 into a mathematically precise point in time: even an extended period can serve as the temporal reference for the main predicate.

### 時は, 時には, and 時しか

Particles following 時 help organize the discourse without, on their own, changing the order of events.

**時は** presents the time or situation as the topic: “when this happens, as far as that situation is concerned…” **時には** combines the temporal reference with the topical or contrastive function of は. **Not every sequence ～時には is an instance of the independent expression 時には, which can mean “sometimes”**.

With **時しか**, followed by a negative predicate, the action is instead restricted to those circumstances alone.

*<p class="jp-example">僕は静かな時しか本を読まない。</p>*
*I only read when it's quiet.*

The meaning “only” comes from the sequence しか～ない. 時 identifies the circumstances in which the reading takes place.

## Tenses with 時: distinguishing when an episode happened from the order of events

Consider a simple structure: **A 時、B**. A specifies the temporal reference point; B is the main clause.

In ordinary narrative usage, the main predicate helps locate the episode relative to the time of speaking. A past predicate normally recounts a past event; a nonpast predicate can express a habit, a general situation, or a future event.

The form before 時, on the other hand, can be chosen in relation to the time expressed by the main clause. In such cases, we speak of **relative tense**: the reference point is not necessarily the speaker's “now.”

When the reference point is the time of speaking, we speak instead of **absolute tense**. Clauses with 時 can be interpreted from either perspective: not every past form that appears in them therefore marks anteriority relative to the main clause.

That is why both **a nonpast form before 時 with a past-tense main clause** and **a た-form before 時 with a main clause referring to the future** are possible.

With many action verbs, the contrast can be explained by asking whether, at the time of the main action, the event preceding 時 has yet to take place, is underway, or has already reached the relevant point.

We must not, however, turn this explanation into the equation “～る = always unfinished; ～た = always completed.” States and retrospective descriptions of an episode allow for different possibilities, which we will examine in the following sections.

When we say that a sentence **leans toward 前 or 後**, the comparison is always the same: **it is B, the main action, that takes place before or after A, the event preceding 時**.

## The four tense combinations: 食べる時 and 食べた時

Comparing eating with washing one's hands allows us to examine the four basic combinations. Here, the actions are distinct, and the context favors a sequential reading.

### 食べる時、洗う: the main action tends toward 前 (before)

*<p class="jp-example">私はご飯を食べる時、手を洗う。</p>*
*When I'm about to eat, I wash my hands first.*

The sentence can express a habit. In a context referring to an upcoming occasion, it can also mean that I will wash my hands before eating.

When I wash my hands, the meal is viewed as an event still to come. The natural reading is therefore close to **ご飯を食べる前に**, “before eating.”

The sense of “before” emerges from the relationship between the two actions: here, washing one's hands is preparation for the meal. 時 does not mean “before” in itself.

### 食べた時、洗う: the main action tends toward 後 (after)

*<p class="jp-example">私はご飯を食べた時、手を洗う。</p>*
*After eating, I wash my hands.*

Now the washing is placed at a point when the meal has already been eaten. The most natural reading is close to **ご飯を食べた後で**, “after eating.”

This sentence, too, can describe a habit or, in the appropriate context, future behavior. In the latter case, the translation would be “I'll wash my hands when I've finished eating.”

**食べた does not, therefore, require us to imagine a meal that has already taken place at the moment of speaking.** The meal may be in the future but already completed relative to the handwashing that follows.

### 食べる時、洗った: before eating, in a past episode

*<p class="jp-example">私はご飯を食べる時、手を洗った。</p>*
*I washed my hands before eating.*

洗った places the handwashing in the past. 食べる, however, presents the meal as something still to come at the moment when I washed my hands.

The entire episode belongs to the past, but the relationship between the actions remains the same as in the first sentence: **first the handwashing, then the planned meal**. That is why 時 is preceded by 食べる, even though the entire account is translated into English using the past tense.

On its own, the sentence does not even guarantee that the meal actually took place afterward: it identifies the handwashing as an action performed on an occasion when I was about to eat.

### 食べた時、洗った: after eating, in a past episode

*<p class="jp-example">私はご飯を食べた時、手を洗った。</p>*
*I washed my hands after eating.*

The main clause recounts handwashing that has already taken place; 食べた presents the meal as already eaten relative to that handwashing.

In the context of this pair of actions, the favored sequence is therefore **first the meal, then the handwashing**.

### Summary of the four combinations

<div class="hn-table">

| Sentence | Time of the episode | Most natural temporal relationship in these examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 食べる時、手を洗う | Habit or future | Handwashing before the meal: toward 前 |
| 食べた時、手を洗う | Habit or future | Handwashing after the meal: toward 後 |
| 食べる時、手を洗った | Past | Handwashing before the meal: toward 前 |
| 食べた時、手を洗った | Past | Handwashing after the meal: toward 後 |

</div>

The table describes these particular sentences, in which the context favors two successive actions. **It is not a table of substitutions that work with every verb in every situation.**




### Why 時 can suggest 前 and 後 without meaning either “before” or “after”

At this point, let us clarify why 時 seems to take on a meaning so close to 前 or 後 in the examples we have just examined, even though it does not directly express either one.

The reason lies in its function: **時 does not, in itself, indicate a sequence of events; rather, it identifies a moment or circumstance in relation to which the main action is situated**. The verb form preceding 時, together with the meaning of the actions and the context, determines how their temporal relationship is interpreted. Its meaning remains “time” or “moment” throughout.

In the examples so far, 食べる時 presents the meal as something still to come at the moment of washing one's hands; 食べた時, by contrast, presents it as already completed. In both cases, the meal provides the temporal reference around which the handwashing is situated, but the perspective changes according to the verb form used.

This also explains another important nuance. **Unlike 前 and 後, which explicitly express a relationship of anteriority or posteriority, 時 evokes the temporal circumstance in which the main action takes place.** 

When the two actions are closely related, as in washing one's hands and eating, the construction therefore tends to suggest a degree of temporal proximity: we imagine the handwashing shortly before the meal or shortly after it has ended.

This is not, however, a rule requiring the interval to be short. 時 does not necessarily mean “immediately before” or “immediately after,” nor does it specify how much time must separate the two actions. That sense of proximity comes from the context. 

Constructions with 前 and 後, by contrast, can simply establish which event precedes or follows the other, even when there is a considerable interval between them.

**The fundamental difference, then, is this: 前 and 後 foreground temporal sequence, whereas 時 foregrounds the moment or circumstance serving as a reference point.** That is why, in certain constructions, 時 can suggest “before” or “after” without either meaning belonging to it directly.

## Why ～る時 does not always mean “before” and ～た時 does not always mean “after”

As we saw in the preceding section, 時 identifies the moment or circumstance in relation to which the main action is situated. **Unlike 前に and 後で, which directly express a relationship of anteriority or posteriority, 時 does not itself establish which of the two actions comes before or after the other.** 

The verb form preceding it, together with the meaning of the actions and the context, guides the temporal interpretation.

When the two actions are closely related, as with washing one's hands and eating, the construction can suggest events occurring close together, making 時 seem similar to 前 or 後. 

However, this interpretation is not obligatory: the actions may also take place at the same time or belong to the same episode, without either one necessarily preceding the other.

It is precisely this flexibility that explains why **～る時 does not always mean “before” and ～た時 does not always mean “after”**.

### The nonpast form can indicate a simultaneous activity

*<p class="jp-example">松本さんは朝ご飯を食べる時、いつもテレビを見る。</p>*
*Matsumoto always watches television while eating breakfast.*

The natural reading is that Matsumoto watches television during breakfast. We do not have to imagine Matsumoto watching television before he starts eating.

Why is 食べる時 interpreted differently from the handwashing example? Because **watching television and eating can take place within the same interval**, whereas in the other example, washing one's hands is normally understood as preparation.

The meanings of the actions and the context thus contribute to the temporal reading. The form 食べる alone does not establish a universal “before.”

### The past form (た) can refer back to an entire past episode

*<p class="jp-example">昨日、料理をした時に、指を切ってしまいました。</p>*
*Yesterday, I cut my finger while I was cooking.*

料理をした時 can evoke the past episode in which I was cooking; cutting my finger was part of that episode. There is no need to imagine that I was injured only after I had finished cooking.

Here, the た-form allows us to look back on the occasion. To explicitly foreground the activity as it was unfolding, we can use 料理をしている時 or 料理をしていた時.

This is an essential qualification: **presenting an event as past does not always mean placing the entire main action after that event has ended**.

### Temporal proximity does not always mean “immediately”

In the handwashing comparisons, 時 can suggest a moment close to the beginning or end of the meal. However, it does not, by itself, encode the strict meaning “immediately before” or “immediately after.”

To express these meanings explicitly, we can use, for example, **～る直前に**, “immediately before,” and **～た直後に**, “immediately after.”

## Verbs of motion: before departure, during the journey, or after arrival?

With verbs such as 行く, 来る, and 帰る, this distinction requires particular care. A trip involves departure, the journey itself, and, in the situations we are considering here, arrival at a destination.

Consequently, an action performed **before the journey is completed** does not necessarily occur before departure: it may also occur during the journey.

### 行く時 with a main clause referring to the future

*<p class="jp-example">私はシカゴへ行く時、すしを食べるつもりだ。</p>*
*I intend to eat sushi when I go to Chicago.*

In this comparison, 行く時 allows two natural readings: eating sushi **before leaving** or **during the journey to Chicago**.

The English translation “when I go” does not make this distinction explicit. To clarify it, we might translate the sentence as “I intend to eat sushi before leaving for Chicago” or “during my trip to Chicago,” depending on the context.

The orientation is toward **前 relative to the completion of the journey**, but not necessarily toward “before setting off.”

### 行った時 with a main clause referring to the future

*<p class="jp-example">私はシカゴへ行った時、すしを食べるつもりだ。</p>*
*I intend to eat sushi once I've arrived in Chicago.*

行った時 presents the journey as already completed relative to the planned time of eating. The favored reading, then, is that the sushi is eaten after reaching the destination, during the visit or stay.

In this comparison, the sentence leans toward **後 relative to arrival**. The form 行った does not necessarily describe a journey already completed as of today: both the journey and the meal may still lie in the future.



**Note**: In the examples referring to the future, A Dictionary of Basic Japanese Grammar (by Seiichi Makino and Michio Tsutsui, The Japan Times, 1986) adds ～つもり (intention) at the end of the sentence because it serves an important function here. This construction expresses an intention already formed and presents the meal as something the speaker intends to do in the future, regardless of the verb form preceding 時.

**つもりだ does not, therefore, determine whether the meal will take place before or after arrival in Chicago**; rather, it helps frame the situation as future-oriented—or, more precisely, makes it clear that both sentences describe a future intention. 

The different temporal interpretations depend instead on the contrast between 行く時 and 行った時: in the first construction, the journey is regarded as not yet completed, whereas in the second, it is presented as already completed relative to the planned meal.

In this way, **行った時 can refer to a future journey** despite using the past form, because that form expresses the completion of the journey relative to the main action, not necessarily relative to the moment of speaking.


### 行く時 with a past-tense main clause

*<p class="jp-example">私はシカゴへ行く時、すしを食べた。</p>*
*I ate sushi when I went to Chicago.*

The episode is in the past because the main clause ends in 食べた. Yet 行く時 presents the journey as still incomplete at the time of the meal.

Compared with 行った時, the favored readings are “I ate sushi before leaving for Chicago” and “I ate sushi during the journey to Chicago.”

Here, too, the nonpast form does not refer to the present: it describes the relationship between the meal and that past journey.

### 行った時 with a past-tense main clause

*<p class="jp-example">私はシカゴへ行った時、すしを食べた。</p>*
*When I went to Chicago, I ate sushi after arriving.*

In the comparison between these four sentences, the meal is understood as taking place after the journey has reached its endpoint: **after arrival**, at the destination.

We must not, however, extend this interpretation to every sentence containing 行った時. The expression can also refer more broadly to “that time I went…” The content of the main clause remains decisive, as the next comparison shows.

## When the two clauses describe the same journey

So far, we have compared two distinct actions: going to Chicago and eating sushi. Now let us see what happens when the main clause specifies **how the journey already mentioned is made**.

### 行く時、車で行く

*<p class="jp-example">私はシカゴへ行く時、車で行く。</p>*
*When I go to Chicago, I go by car.*

The sentence can express a habit or, in the appropriate context, a choice made for a future trip.

There are not two journeys to place in sequence. 車で行く specifies the means of transport used for the journey identified by シカゴへ行く時. For this reason, 行く時 does not mean “before going to Chicago” here.

### 行く時、車で行った

*<p class="jp-example">私はシカゴへ行く時、車で行った。</p>*
*When I went to Chicago, I went by car.*

The main clause places the journey in the past. The form 行く before 時 remains compatible with that temporal setting: the main clause describes how the journey took place.

There is no need to translate this as “Before going to Chicago, I went by car.” Such a translation would introduce a sequence of events that the sentence does not require.

### 行った時、車で行った

*<p class="jp-example">私はシカゴへ行った時、車で行った。</p>*
*That time I went to Chicago, I went by car.*

This sentence, too, can refer to a single journey. 行った時 recalls a past occasion; the main clause adds the means of transport.

**The two past forms do not, therefore, force the reading “After arriving in Chicago, I went there by car.”** The relationship is between an episode and one of its characteristics, not between two successive journeys.

### Why is 行った時、車で行く problematic in this comparison?

*<p class="jp-example">× 私はシカゴへ行った時、車で行く。</p>*

The sentence is unnatural under the intended reading of **one and the same future journey**: “When I go to Chicago, I'll go by car.”

If we interpret 行った relative to the main clause, the journey is already complete, which conflicts with 車で行く describing how that same journey will be made. If, instead, we interpret it as referring back to a past trip, the main clause does not provide the normal retrospective description of it.

This judgment applies to the specific reading just defined. **It does not mean that ～た時 followed by a nonpast predicate is always incorrect:** 行った時、すしを食べるつもりだ is possible precisely because eating and traveling are two distinct actions that can be placed in sequence.

## With states, the past form before 時 does not mean “after the state has ended”

The patterns observed with two successive actions do not automatically apply to predicates such as いる, to adjectives, or to nominal expressions.

A state can extend over time and provide the background for another situation. In such cases, with a past-tense main clause, both nonpast and past forms are often possible before 時.

### 日本にいる時 and 日本にいた時

*<p class="jp-example">私は日本にいる時、田中先生に会った。</p>*
*I met Professor Tanaka when I was in Japan.*

*<p class="jp-example">私は日本にいた時、田中先生に会った。</p>*
*I met Professor Tanaka when I was in Japan.*

The two sentences can describe the same temporal relationship: the meeting takes place during the stay in Japan.

With **いる**, the state is presented as true at the time of the meeting: I was in Japan at that moment. With **いた**, the speaker can recall that state as belonging to a past period.

There is no need to assign the two sentences a sharp difference in every context. Above all, **いた時 does not mean that the meeting happened after I had left Japan**. The past form can refer to the period being recalled without implying that the state had already ended when the meeting took place.

### 高い時 and 高かった時

*<p class="jp-example">肉が高い時は、魚も高かった。</p>*
*When meat was expensive, fish was expensive too.*

*<p class="jp-example">肉が高かった時は、魚も高かった。</p>*
*During the period when meat was expensive, fish was expensive too.*

Both sentences can place the high prices within the same period. 高い does not require the interpretation “now,” and 高かった does not mean “after meat had stopped being expensive.”

The past form can place greater emphasis on the distance from the present, especially when an earlier situation is contrasted with the current one. This contrast, however, is a contextual nuance, not an obligatory consequence of every past form.

The same principle helps us understand an example like this:

*<p class="jp-example">テリーはジュースが安い時にたくさん買っておいた。</p>*
*Terry stocked up on juice when it was cheap.*

安い describes the price at the time of purchase. It does not claim that the juice is still cheap now. 買っておいた adds the idea of buying it in advance in anticipation of a later need.

### 学生の時 and 学生だった時

A similar possibility arises with nouns. **学生の時** identifies the period when someone is a student; **学生だった時** explicitly presents that period as past.

*<p class="jp-example">私が大学生だった時、妹はまだ三つだった。</p>*
*When I was at university, my younger sister was only three.*

The two states belong to the same period: I was a university student, and my sister was three years old. The first だった form does not place the second state after the end of my studies.

学生の時 can also be used to talk about the past, as we saw in 私は学生の時、よく勉強した. **の does not mark present tense in the way an English present-tense form does, and it does not have to be replaced by だった whenever the narrative is set in the past.**

## ～ている時 and ～ていた時: foregrounding what was in progress

When we want to clearly indicate an activity in progress, we can use **～ている時** or, in a past narrative, **～ていた時**.

*<p class="jp-example">ご飯を食べている時、友達から電話がかかってきた。</p>*
*While I was eating, a friend called me.*

*<p class="jp-example">ご飯を食べていた時、友達から電話がかかってきた。</p>*
*While I was eating, a friend called me.*

In both sentences, the meal was in progress when the call came. 食べている presents the activity as ongoing at the time of that past event; 食べていた allows it to be presented as an activity that was underway during the period being recalled.

We should not interpret 食べていた時 as “when I had already finished eating.” **The past form here refers to an ongoing activity, not to the completion of the meal.**

This shows why we need to consider the whole predicate. 食べた and 食べていた both end in a past form, but they do not describe the situation in the same way.

### ～ている does not always indicate an action in progress

The construction ～ている can also express a state resulting from a change. Its meaning depends on the verb.

*<p class="jp-example">眼鏡をかけている時は、小さい字もよく見えます。</p>*
*When I'm wearing glasses, I can see even small print clearly.*

Here 眼鏡をかけている describes the state of having glasses on. It does not necessarily indicate the ongoing act of putting them on.

To interpret ～ている時, we must therefore ask **what situation ～ている expresses with that particular verb** before establishing its relationship with the main clause.

## Negative forms: ～ない時 and ～なかった時

With negative forms, too, we must distinguish a situation viewed as it unfolds from an occasion viewed retrospectively.

*<p class="jp-example">朝ご飯を食べない時は、コーヒーだけ飲みます。</p>*
*When I don't eat breakfast, I just drink coffee.*

食べない時 identifies occasions on which I do not eat breakfast. It does not automatically mean “before eating”: negation does not, by itself, establish a relationship of anteriority.

*<p class="jp-example">朝ご飯を食べなかった時は、昼前におなかがすきます。</p>*
*On days when I've skipped breakfast, I get hungry before noon.*

Here 食べなかった presents the fact of not eating breakfast as already established in the situation being described: by the time hunger sets in, breakfast has been skipped.

The nonpast main clause can describe a habitual consequence. There is no contradiction between 食べなかった and すきます: **the first form concerns what has or has not already happened relative to the subsequent situation; the second describes a recurring pattern**.

Again, though, the type of predicate matters. 分からなかった時 can mean “when I didn't understand,” describing a past state without requiring that the state had already ended when the main action began.

## 時, ～たら, and ～と: temporal reference and conditional relationships

In English, “when” can introduce either the time of an event or the circumstance that brings about a result. In Japanese, these functions may call for different constructions.

At its core, 時 identifies a time, occasion, or situation. **～たら** can present the occurrence of a circumstance as the condition for what follows; **～と** can express a regular relationship or an expected result when that circumstance occurs.

### “When you see it, you'll be surprised”

*<p class="jp-example">それを見たら、驚きますよ。</p>*
*When you see it, you'll be surprised.*

*<p class="jp-example">それを見ると、驚きますよ。</p>*
*Once you see it, you'll be surprised.*

Here, we are not simply identifying the time or occasion when someone will feel surprised. We are predicting a reaction caused by seeing something: “if you see it / once you see it, you'll be surprised.”

To express this relationship, ～たら and ～と are the natural choices. Substituting **それを見る時、驚きますよ** or **それを見た時、驚きますよ** does not convey this stand-alone prediction as naturally.

The reason is not that 時 is incompatible with the verb 驚く. In a past narrative, we can say:

*<p class="jp-example">初めてその絵を見た時、本当に驚きました。</p>*
*The first time I saw that painting, I was genuinely surprised.*

Here, the speaker recalls a past occasion and describes what they felt at that time. The temporal relationship is entirely appropriate to the function of 時.

### Why we should not say that 時 is always incompatible with conditions

The distinction we have just seen is useful, but we must not turn it into an absolute prohibition such as “時 can never refer to a hypothetical situation.”

*<p class="jp-example">道が分からない時は、近くの人に聞いてください。</p>*
*If you don't know the way, ask someone nearby.*

The sentence is natural: it identifies the situation in which the advice applies. Translating it as “if” is perfectly natural in English, even though Japanese frames the circumstance with 時は.

We should therefore not choose a construction solely on the basis of whether “when” or “if” appears in the translation. The useful question is: **am I identifying a situation in which a statement holds, or am I foregrounding the occurrence of a condition and the result that follows from it?**

The two perspectives can also overlap. Compare:

*<p class="jp-example">駅に着いた時に、電話してください。</p>*
*Please call me when you arrive at the station.*

*<p class="jp-example">駅に着いたら、電話してください。</p>*
*Once you've arrived at the station, please call me.*

Both can be used for a request involving a future action. The first specifies when the call should take place; the second presents arrival as the circumstance that triggers the call.

This is not a difference that always requires separate English translations. It is nonetheless useful for understanding why, in other contexts, replacing 時 with ～たら changes how natural the sentence sounds or what it means.

## How to choose the form before 時

To use 時 confidently, it helps to start with what the sentence means, rather than making everything depend on the English translation of the verb.

- **Identify what the main clause says.** Does it recount a past episode, describe a habit, or express a future plan or request?
- **Examine the predicate before 時.** Does it express an action, movement, a state, or an activity presented with ～ている?
- **Determine the relationship between the situations.** Does the main action happen before, during, or after the other situation? Or does it add a detail about the same episode?
- **Decide how explicitly to express the relationship.** If the main point is to say “before” or “after,” 前に and 後で express that relationship directly. If the aim is to identify the occasion when something happens, 時 may be the appropriate choice.

With **two distinct actions occurring in sequence**, ～る時 often tends toward **前**, while ～た時 tends toward **後**. With **movement**, ～る時 can also include the journey itself; with **states**, past and nonpast forms can describe simultaneity; when **the same episode is described from two perspectives**, the た-form does not require a sequence of events.

The core function of 時 is to identify **the time or occasion associated with the situation that precedes it**. The form of the predicate helps us choose the perspective from which to view that situation, while the content of the entire sentence clarifies how it relates to what is expressed in the main clause.
