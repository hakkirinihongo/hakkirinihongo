**What does ありがとうございます (arigatou gozaimasu) really mean, and where does it come from?**

ありがとうございます (arigatou gozaimasu) ultimately derives from the Classical Japanese adjective **ありがたし**, the historical source of modern **ありがたい**, originally used to describe something rare, precious, or difficult to obtain.

**ありがとうございます** is one of the very first expressions people learn in Japanese. At first glance, it seems to be a simple equivalent of *"thank you."*

In reality, however, this familiar phrase has a much richer history. **ありがとう** did not originally arise as a straightforward expression of gratitude, but from the ancient adjective **ありがたし** (later **ありがたい**, *arigatai*), which conveyed the idea of **something rare, precious, or difficult to come by or receive**.

Understanding this origin also helps us use the expression more appropriately.

Saying ありがとう is not simply a matter of acknowledging a favor. Embedded in the history of the word is the idea that what someone has done for us is not something to be taken for granted.

It is precisely from this nuance—something *"difficult to obtain,"* and therefore precious—that the expression came to convey gratitude.

In this article, we will explore the true, underlying meaning of **arigatou gozaimasu**, its historical origins, its relationship with **ありがたい**, the difference between **ありがとう** and **ありがとうございます**, and the main contexts in which this expression is used in modern Japanese.

## ありがとうございます (arigatou gozaimasu) – Meaning

ありがとうございます can correspond to **“thank you,”** **“many thanks,”** or the more formal **“I appreciate it,”** depending on the context and the relationship between the speaker and the listener.

Some expressions become so familiar that we no longer notice their weight, history, or depth. ありがとうございます is one of them.

It is universally recognized, to the point that it is often uttered almost automatically—even by people who are only beginning to study Japanese or who do not know the language at all. Yet behind this seemingly familiar expression lies a history spanning centuries, shaped by linguistic evolution, cultural change, and subtle shifts in meaning.

It is one of the fundamental expressions of gratitude in modern Japanese, but its meaning goes far beyond the simple English translation *"thank you."*

In Japanese, ありがとうございます does more than acknowledge that we have received something useful or pleasant. It also conveys that we recognize the other person's action as something worthy of attention, respect, and acknowledgment.

The expression is made up of **ありがとう**, which conveys the actual thanks, and **ございます**, which makes the expression more polite.

For this reason, ありがとうございます is more respectful than simply saying ありがとう. It does not necessarily mean *"thank you very much,"* although that translation may fit certain situations. The key distinction is not the intensity of the gratitude but the level of politeness.

ありがとう is natural among friends, family members, and people with whom one has a close relationship, whereas ありがとうございます is more appropriate when speaking to people one is not close to, superiors, teachers, customers, colleagues, or strangers. We will return to this point in greater detail later in the article.

The deeper meaning of ありがとうございます is therefore twofold.

On the one hand, it expresses gratitude: **the speaker acknowledges having received a favor**, help, information, a gift, a service, or even a simple act of kindness.

On the other hand, it also expresses **an awareness of the relationship between the speaker and the other person**. The speaker does not take the other person's action for granted. In this sense, ありがとうございます is not simply a "polite" expression, but also a way of positioning oneself appropriately within that relationship.

In modern Japanese, **ありがとうございます** is an extremely common and versatile expression. It can be used when someone helps us, offers us something, pays us a compliment, gives us information, waits for us, devotes time to us, or simply does something kind for us.

It is equally at home in everyday conversation and in more formal settings, including emails, business messages, shops, restaurants, schools, and professional environments.

It is one of those expressions whose core function remains constant across different registers: acknowledging and expressing gratitude.

It is also important to understand that ありがとうございます is not simply a "longer" version of **ありがとう**.

In Japanese, longer expressions often signal greater social distance, consideration, or politeness toward the other person.

Adding **ございます** makes the expression more appropriate in situations where spontaneity alone is not enough and respect must also be conveyed.

For this reason, whenever you are unsure how close your relationship with the other person is, ありがとうございます is generally the safest choice.

As noted above, possible English equivalents include *“thank you,”* *“thanks,”* *“many thanks,”* and “I appreciate it,” but none of them covers every context perfectly.

**“Thank you”** conveys the general meaning; **“I appreciate it”** places greater emphasis on appreciation rather than necessarily indicating a higher level of politeness; and **“thank you very much”** works when the context calls for a more emphatic expression of gratitude.

The key point, however, is that ありがとうございます combines gratitude with politeness. It conveys not only *"I have received something,"* but also *"I recognize the value of what you have done for me."*

For this reason, we cannot regard it as nothing more than a simple expression of thanks.

In the following sections, we will first examine how the expression is formed before tracing its historical origins, allowing us to understand what it originally meant and how, step by step, it became one of the expressions most representative of Japanese sensibilities.

## The Origin and Meaning of the Adjective ありがたい (有り難い)

The adjective 「ありがたい」 belongs to the category of -i adjectives (形容詞) and in modern Japanese can mean *“grateful, appreciated, or welcome.”*

Even today, it is used when expressing gratitude, for example toward heaven or toward deities.

From a morphological perspective, it is a compound formed from the element あり (the ren’yōkei form of ある in Classical Japanese) combined with the classical adjective 難し（かたし）. In other words, 「ありがたい」 literally derives from the idea of **“something that exists only with difficulty,”** that is, something rare and precious.

Its kanji spelling, 有り難い, clearly reflects this composition: 有り (“to exist,” “to have”) + 難い (“difficult”).

Originally, in fact, 「ありがたい」 referred to **something rare**, difficult to find or obtain. The term appears with this meaning as early as classical Japanese texts from the Heian period. For example, in the *Makura no Sōshi* (枕草子, c. 1000), the expression 「ありがたきもの」 describes *“things that are arigataki,”* that is, **“things that are difficult to find in this world.”**

Likewise, even older texts such as the *Man’yōshū* (8th century) use 「ありがたし」 to refer to something exceptionally rare. In certain archaic contexts, when 「ある」 was used in the sense of *“to live/to exist in the world"*, 「ありがたい」 could even mean *“life is difficult,”* that is, *“painful”* or *“full of hardship.”*

Over time, the meaning of 「ありがたい」 expanded from *“rare and precious”* to describe something extraordinarily positive or worthy of admiration.

For example, it could refer to an exceptional person or to something admirable *“so extraordinary as to be out of the ordinary.”* 

From this, the meaning of *“being grateful for something”* gradually emerged. If someone does something so rare and precious that it seems invaluable to us, we naturally feel grateful.

By medieval Japan, this adjective had begun to express joyful appreciation and gratitude for an exceptional act or favor. In other words, 「ありがたい」 came to embody the idea that *“what you have done for me is so rare and precious that I am deeply grateful to you for it.”*

This emotional nuance of gratitude, already embedded within the adjective itself, laid the groundwork for the later use of 「ありがとう」 as an independent expression of thanks.

From a historical-linguistic perspective, 「ありがたい」 is the modern form (*kōgo*) of the Classical Japanese adjective 「ありがたし」, which belonged to the ク活用 (*ku-conjugation*) class of adjectives.

In Classical Japanese, 「ありがたし」 ended in -shi in its conclusive form (終止形), took -ki in its attributive form (連体形), producing 「ありがたき」, and had a continuative form (連用形, *ren'yōkei*) ending in -ku, namely 「ありがたく」.

These different grammatical forms underwent distinct phonological developments. The attributive ending -ki changed to -i through a process known as イ音便 (*i-onbin*), giving rise to the modern adjective 「ありがたい」. The continuative form 「ありがたく」, on the other hand, underwent ウ音便 (*u-onbin*), producing 「ありがたう」, from which the modern expression 「ありがとう」 developed.

In other words, 「ありがたい」 and 「ありがとう」 share the same historical origin, but they developed through different grammatical forms and sound changes. Understanding this distinction is essential to tracing the formation of 「ありがとうございます」, which we will examine in the following section.

In summary, 「ありがたい」 has ancient origins and underwent a semantic development that led from the literal meaning of **“difficult to obtain or encounter (and therefore rare)”** to the idea of **“something precious or worthy of appreciation,”** and ultimately to the expression of deep gratitude.

## From ありがたし to ありがとうございます: How the Expression Developed

The modern expression 「ありがとうございます」 (which can be translated as *“Thank you very much”* or *“I sincerely thank you”* in formal contexts) ultimately derives from the Classical Japanese adjective 「ありがたし」, through its continuative form 「ありがたく」 and the phonological change known as ウ音便 (*u-onbin*). Below, we will examine the grammatical, phonological, and morphological developments that gave rise to this polite expression, step by step.


### The Adjectival Base

Historically, the starting point is the Classical Japanese adjective 「ありがたし」, whose continuative form (連用形, *ren’yōkei*) was 「ありがたく」. Modern Japanese preserves the corresponding -ku form: 「ありがたい」 becomes 「ありがたく」 when it modifies a following predicate.

This grammatical correspondence should not be confused with a direct historical sequence in which the modern adjective 「ありがたい」 developed first and subsequently became 「ありがとう」. Rather, both modern forms share the same classical origin, with 「ありがとう」 deriving specifically from the continuative form 「ありがたく」.

As a continuative form, 「ありがたく」 can function adverbially and, depending on the construction, convey a sense of gratitude or appreciation. It does not, however, necessarily correspond to the English adverb *“gratefully”* in every context.

### The Phonological Change (ウ音便)

The sound change that connects 「ありがたく」 with 「ありがとう」 is known as **ウ音便 (*u-onbin*)**. In this type of adjectival inflection, the consonant /k/ in the continuative ending -ku was lost, leaving -u. As a result, 「ありがたく」 became 「ありがたう」.

The resulting vowel sequence /au/ subsequently merged into a long /oː/ sound. Thus, 「ありがたう」 came to be pronounced *arigatō*, which is conventionally written 「ありがとう」 in modern Japanese. This was a phonological development, distinct from the semantic changes through which the expression gradually acquired its modern meaning of gratitude.

In practical terms, 「有り難く」 (*arigataku*) developed into 「有り難う」 (*arigatō*) through this sound change.

A similar development can be seen in 「おめでとう」, which derives from 「おめでたく」 through the intermediate form 「おめでたう」, and in 「おはようございます」, historically connected to 「お早く」 through its u-onbin form 「お早う」. The same phenomenon also appears in polite constructions such as 「高く」 → 「高うございます」 (*takō gozaimasu*).

Historically, adjectival ウ音便 was particularly characteristic of western varieties of Japanese, including the language spoken in Kyoto and the wider Kansai region. Unlike in modern standard Japanese, where these forms are most familiar in conventional greetings and before polite verbs such as 「ございます」 or 「存じます」, corresponding u-onbin forms remain part of ordinary adjective inflection in some western Japanese dialects.

The phenomenon is therefore neither exclusive to Kyoto nor limited to fossilized expressions. Rather, it reflects a broader historical sound change whose distribution and subsequent development varied across Japanese dialects.

### The Addition of the Polite Verb

The polite verb 「ございます」 (*gozaimasu*) ultimately derives from 「ござる」, a verb historically related to 「ある」 (*aru*, “to exist”). More specifically, 「ございます」 developed from the older polite form 「ござります」 (*gozarimasu*), which combined the continuative form of 「ござる」 with the polite auxiliary 「ます」. The change from 「ござります」 to 「ございます」 became established during the later Edo period.

This development must be distinguished from the ウ音便 (*u-onbin*) that produced 「ありがとう」 from 「ありがたく」. The two elements followed separate historical paths before becoming established together in the modern expression 「ありがとうございます」.

From a grammatical perspective, 「ありがとうございます」 combines 「ありがとう」, the u-onbin form historically derived from the continuative 「ありがたく」 of the Classical Japanese adjective 「ありがたし」, with the polite verb 「ございます」. The resulting expression conveys gratitude while also marking politeness toward the listener.

From an etymological standpoint, the expression can be understood in relation to the original meaning of 「ありがたし」: something rare, precious, or difficult to come by. This allows us to paraphrase its underlying sense as *“what you have done is something precious to me.”* Such a paraphrase, however, illustrates the historical meaning of the adjective rather than the literal grammatical meaning of the modern expression, which functions as an established polite way of saying *“thank you”* or *“I appreciate it.”*

Historical evidence also shows that the u-onbin form 「ありがたう」 was used with several different polite expressions long before 「ございます」 became standard. For example, the *Nihon Kokugo Daijiten* (日本国語大辞典) cites 「御志ありがたう候」 from the Noh play *Matsukaze* (松風, c. 1423), as well as 「ありがたふ存ます」 from the eighteenth-century work *Shōdan Isha Katagi* (笑談医者気質, 1774).

These examples demonstrate that expressions of gratitude based on 「ありがたう」 could be combined with different polite predicates, including 「候」 and forms of 「存じます」. They should not, however, be interpreted as direct grammatical predecessors that necessarily evolved into 「ございます」.

The modern 「ありがとうございます」 is therefore best understood as the combination of two historically distinct elements: the adjective-derived expression 「ありがとう」, which came to express gratitude, and 「ございます」, which provides the polite register.



### In Summary

The historical formation of the modern Japanese expression for *“thank you”* can be summarized as follows:

「ありがたし」 (the Classical Japanese adjective meaning *“rare; precious,”* which later also came to express gratitude) → the continuative form 「ありがたく」 → 「ありがたう」 (through ウ音便) → 「ありがとう」 (modern pronunciation and spelling) → 「ありがとうございます」 (with the polite verb 「ございます」).

This sequence illustrates the grammatical and phonological relationship between the forms, rather than establishing a precise chronology of their earliest attestations. The modern adjective 「ありがたい」 developed from the same classical source, but it is not an intermediate stage in the formation of 「ありがとう」.

**The past-tense version 「ありがとうございました」** follows exactly the same logic, except that it uses the past form 「ございました」.

It is also worth noting that in modern writing, 「ありがとう」 is usually written in hiragana, whereas the kanji form 「有難う」 tends to have a more literary or formal flavor.

In any case, 「ありがとうございます」 is the expression that combines the etymological notion of “something rare and precious” with the linguistic mechanism of formal politeness in Japanese.

## The Historical Development of ありがとう / ありがとうございます

The history of 「ありがとう」 as an expression of gratitude is closely connected to changes in the meaning of 「ありがたし」 and the development of Japanese conventions of politeness. As we have seen, the adjective originally conveyed **the idea of rarity and preciousness**, describing something unusual, difficult to obtain, or exceptionally valuable.

In its earliest uses, 「ありがたし」 was not simply a formula for thanking another person. It was a descriptive adjective whose range of meanings gradually expanded to include feelings of appreciation and gratitude.

During the Muromachi period (1336–1573), expressions such as 「かたじけない」 (忝い, *katajikenai*) were more commonly used to convey gratitude, particularly in situations involving deference, obligation, or a sense of indebtedness toward another person.

「かたじけない」 expressed the feeling that someone's kindness or favor was more than one deserved, combining gratitude with humility and respect. According to the *Nihon Kokugo Daijiten* (日本国語大辞典), this expression was more prevalent during the Muromachi period, while 「ありがたい」 became increasingly dominant from the Genroku era (1688–1704) onward.

However, this does not mean that 「ありがたい」 lacked associations with gratitude before that period. Its semantic development had already begun much earlier, particularly in religious and literary contexts.

Buddhist thought played an important role in this development. In medieval religious language, the idea of something being 「ありがたい」 was closely associated with the rarity and preciousness of receiving the Buddha's teachings, experiencing compassion, or encountering circumstances considered exceptionally fortunate.

One particularly relevant Buddhist teaching is the parable of the blind turtle and the floating log (盲亀浮木, *mōki fuboku*). The story describes a blind turtle that surfaces from the depths of the ocean only once every hundred years and the extraordinary difficulty of its encountering the single hole in a piece of wood floating on the water.

The parable illustrates how extraordinarily rare it is to be born human and encounter the Buddhist teachings. Its emphasis on rarity and the preciousness of such an opportunity closely reflects the original meaning of 「ありがたし」.

This connection helps explain why the adjective could express not only something rare or difficult to obtain, but also something so precious that it inspired profound gratitude. The parable illustrates this religious understanding of rarity, although it should not be treated as the direct linguistic origin of 「ありがとう」.

Importantly, the use of 「ありがたう」 in expressions of gratitude is documented before the Edo period. The *Nihon Kokugo Daijiten*, for example, cites 「御志ありがたう候」 from the Noh play *Matsukaze* (松風), in a textual source dated to approximately 1423.

This early example is particularly significant because it shows 「ありがたう」 being used to express gratitude in combination with the polite predicate 「候」 (*sōrō*). It therefore demonstrates that the expression had already acquired a recognizable function of thanking someone during the medieval period.

During the Edo period (1603–1868), 「ありがとう」 became increasingly widespread as an expression of everyday gratitude. Its use gradually extended beyond religious and literary contexts, becoming more familiar in ordinary social interactions.

An important source for understanding the language of this transitional period is the *Nippo Jisho* (日葡辞書), the Japanese-Portuguese dictionary compiled by Jesuit missionaries and published in 1603–1604.

The dictionary records 「ありがたい」 (*arigatai*) with meanings associated with reverence and something worthy of respect or veneration. This provides valuable evidence of the adjective's semantic range at the beginning of the seventeenth century, when its associations with preciousness, respect, and appreciation were already well established.

However, the dictionary's treatment of the adjective should not be confused with evidence that the modern expression 「ありがとうございます」 had already acquired precisely the same social distribution and degree of conventionalization that it has today.

Over the following generations, expressions based on 「ありがたう」 became more common in everyday language. Historical sources document its use both with polite predicates and, eventually, as an independent expression of thanks.

For example, the *Nihon Kokugo Daijiten* cites an independent use of 「ありがたふ」 in *Hokka Tsūjō* (北華通情), published in 1794. Unlike the earlier example from *Matsukaze*, this expression appears without a following polite verb, illustrating its established use as a standalone formula of gratitude.

The emergence and spread of these forms should be understood as part of a gradual linguistic development rather than a single, clearly identifiable moment when 「ありがたし」 suddenly became 「ありがとう」 in its modern sense.

By the later Edo period and into the Meiji era, expressions based on 「ありがとう」 had become firmly established in Japanese usage. The combination with 「ございます」 eventually became the familiar polite formula 「ありがとうございます」, while 「ありがとう」 alone came to serve as its less formal counterpart.

It is also important to distinguish the historical rise of 「ありがたい」 as an expression of gratitude from the spread of 「ありがとう」 as a conventional formula of thanks. The two developments are closely related, but they are not identical: one concerns the expanding meanings of an adjective, while the other concerns the establishment of a particular expression in everyday communication.

An interesting point in the history of 「ありがとう」 concerns a popular etymological misconception involving the Portuguese word *“obrigado,”* which also means *“thank you.”*

According to this theory, the Japanese expression supposedly originated from the Portuguese word after the arrival of Portuguese traders and missionaries in Japan during the sixteenth century. Some versions of the story even reverse the direction of borrowing, suggesting that Portuguese *“obrigado”* came from Japanese 「ありがとう」.

Neither explanation is supported by the historical or linguistic evidence.

The Portuguese first arrived in Japan in 1543, whereas the Japanese expression 「ありがたう」 is documented in a formula of gratitude dating to approximately 1423, more than a century earlier. Its underlying adjective 「ありがたし」 is considerably older still, with attestations reaching back to the eighth-century *Man'yōshū* (万葉集).

Furthermore, the development from 「ありがたく」 to 「ありがたう」 and subsequently 「ありがとう」 can be explained through established historical sound changes in Japanese, particularly ウ音便 (*u-onbin*), without any need to invoke borrowing from Portuguese.

The resemblance between 「ありがとう」 and *“obrigado”* is therefore coincidental. Despite their similar meanings and superficially comparable sounds, the two expressions have entirely different linguistic histories.

The evolution of 「ありがとう」 thus reflects a long process of semantic expansion, phonological change, and changing conventions of politeness within Japanese itself, transforming an adjective associated with rarity and preciousness into one of the language's most familiar expressions of gratitude.

## Usage in Formal and Informal Registers, and in Contemporary Japan

In contemporary Japanese communication, 「ありがとうございます」 is ubiquitous and represents **the quintessential formal (teineigo) expression of gratitude**. It is used in polite and respectful contexts: for example, when addressing superiors, customers, people one has just met, or in public situations.

In everyday life in Japan, it is perfectly normal to hear 「ありがとうございます」 accompanied by a slight bow, emphasizing both deference and sincerity. In business and commercial settings, this expression is virtually indispensable for maintaining the expected level of politeness.

In written communication (such as formal emails or thank-you letters), the form 「ありがとうございます。」 is commonly used, as are even more formal variants such as 「誠にありがとうございます。」(*“Thank you very much”*) or 「心より御礼申し上げます」 (*“Please accept my heartfelt thanks”*), depending on the degree of formality required.

In informal settings, by contrast, people typically use 「ありがとう」 without ございます. On its own, 「ありがとう。」 is a casual and friendly expression of thanks, suitable for conversations with friends, family members, or trusted acquaintances.

For example, among classmates or colleagues of similar status, a simple 「ありがとう！」 is completely natural. Japanese speakers often add the interjection 「どうも」 (dōmo) at the beginning for emphasis: 「どうもありがとう！」 corresponds to *“thank you very much”* in an informal tone.

It is also worth mentioning that 「どうも」 by itself can function as a brief and informal expression of thanks (similar to a quick *“thanks”* in English). In youth speech, one also encounters contracted forms such as 「あざっす」 (derived from the rapid pronunciation of ありがとうございます), but these are slang expressions restricted to very informal contexts.

Japanese also has regional and culturally specific ways of expressing gratitude (for example, 「おおきに」 in the Kansai dialect), but 「ありがとう」 remains universally understood and used throughout the country as a general expression of thanks.

In the popular imagination, it is one of the first words Japanese children learn as part of basic good manners, alongside 「ごめんなさい」 (*“I'm sorry”*).

In media and popular culture, 「ありがとう」 appears as a symbol of gratitude and is sometimes even written in the Latin alphabet (“Arigato”) in visual arts to emphasize the Japanese character of the word.

In summary, 「ありがとうございます」 emerged through the historical development of an adjective originally associated with rarity and preciousness, the establishment of 「ありがとう」 as an expression of gratitude, and its combination with the polite verb 「ございます」.

Today it coexists with the more informal variant 「ありがとう」, allowing speakers to adjust their level of formality according to the situation. This expression beautifully embodies the Japanese culture of gratitude expressed through humility and respect: a *“thank you”* that treats another person's action as something rare—almost improbable—and therefore worthy of gratitude.

## The Polite Verb 「ございます」 and Its Historical Connection to 「ある」

The polite verb 「ございます」 (*gozaimasu*) has a historical connection to the verb 「ある」 (*aru*, “to exist”), although its development involved several intermediate forms.

Its origins can be traced to 「ござある」 (御座有る), a construction combining 「御座」 (*goza*) with 「あり」 (*ari*), a Classical Japanese form of 「ある」. Originally used as a respectful expression referring to someone's presence or existence, 「ござある」 subsequently developed into 「ござる」 (*gozaru*), which could function both as an honorific verb and, increasingly, as a polite equivalent of 「ある」.

The continuative form 「ござり」 later combined with the polite auxiliary 「ます」, producing 「ござります」 (*gozarimasu*). This form eventually developed into 「ございます」, which became widespread during the later Edo period.

In contemporary Japanese, 「ございます」 functions as a more polite equivalent of 「あります」 and can be used both as an independent verb and as an auxiliary verb.

For example, 「こちらに資料がございます」 means *“The documents are available here,”* while 「結構でございます」 can be translated as *“That is perfectly fine.”* In the latter construction, 「ございます」 serves to express politeness rather than making an independent statement about physical existence.

The same distinction is important when interpreting 「ありがとうございます」. Although the expression ultimately contains elements historically connected to the idea of existence, its modern meaning cannot be understood simply by translating each component literally.

Instead, the expression should be understood in relation to the historical development of 「ありがたし」, whose original meaning of something rare or precious gradually came to convey appreciation and gratitude. In modern Japanese, however, 「ありがとうございます」 functions as an established expression of thanks, not as a literal statement that something precious exists.

From the perspective of Japanese honorific grammar, 「ございます」 **belongs to 丁寧語 (*teineigo*), expressing politeness toward the listener without necessarily elevating the grammatical subject**.

This distinction is particularly important because the historical verb 「ござる」 could function as a subject-honorific expression, whereas the modern 「ございます」 is classified primarily as a polite form. Its development therefore also illustrates how expressions originally associated with honoring a particular person can gradually acquire a more general function of politeness.

As for its written form, 「ございます」 is normally written in hiragana in contemporary Japanese, particularly in established expressions such as 「ありがとうございます」 and constructions such as 「～でございます」. The kanji spelling 「御座います」 also exists, but is considerably less common in ordinary modern writing.

Similarly, 「ありがとう」 is generally written in hiragana, although the kanji spelling 「有難う」 may appear in literary, stylistic, or other contexts. The choice of kanji does not, by itself, make the expression grammatically more polite.

Ultimately, 「ありがとうございます」 brings together two elements with distinct historical developments: 「ありがとう」, derived from the Classical Japanese adjective 「ありがたし」 through ウ音便, and 「ございます」, which developed from an older verb of existence into a general marker of politeness.

## Conclusion

The expression 「ありがとうございます」 is the result of a long process of grammatical, phonological, and semantic development. Its origins can be traced to the Classical Japanese adjective 「ありがたし」, formed from 「あり」 (*ari*, “to exist”) and 「難し」 (*gatashi*, “difficult”), which originally described something rare, difficult to obtain, or exceptionally precious.

Through the continuative form 「ありがたく」 and the sound change known as ウ音便 (*u-onbin*), the expression 「ありがとう」 emerged and gradually became established as a conventional way of expressing gratitude. Its combination with 「ございます」, a polite verb historically connected to 「ある」 through 「ござる」, eventually gave rise to the familiar modern expression 「ありがとうございます」.

This development reveals how **a word originally associated with rarity and preciousness became one of the most widely used expressions of gratitude in Japanese**, while also illustrating the close relationship between linguistic change and the evolution of social conventions.

Today, 「ありがとう」 and 「ありがとうございます」 allow speakers to express appreciation with different degrees of politeness. Although their original components are no longer interpreted literally in everyday communication, their history preserves a particularly meaningful connection between gratitude and the recognition of something precious.

Understanding this background does more than explain where a familiar Japanese expression comes from: it reveals how centuries of linguistic development can be preserved within words that speakers use every day.
