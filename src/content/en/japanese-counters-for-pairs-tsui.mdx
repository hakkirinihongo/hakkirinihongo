**How do you say “a pair” in Japanese, and when should you use 対 (つい, tsui) instead of another counter?**


How you translate “a pair” into Japanese depends on the object and on the meaning you intend. 

In short:

- **対 (つい, tsui)** is used to count pairs of matching or coordinated items, such as coordinated vases or earrings; 
- **一対 (いっつい)** means one pair; 
- Shoes and socks are normally counted with **足 (そく)**, gloves with **双 (そう)** or **組 (くみ)**, and chopsticks used for eating with **膳 (ぜん)**. 

When the intended meaning is simply two objects, a single two-part item, or an approximate quantity, you need to choose the Japanese counter or expression appropriate to that meaning.




English uses pair for two items that belong together, as in a pair of shoes or a pair of gloves, and also for certain single objects conventionally conceived as two-part units, such as a pair of glasses. By contrast, approximate quantities are normally expressed with phrases such as a couple of or a few. In Japanese, these distinctions affect the choice of words and counters.


**対 (つい, tsui) can be used to present two elements as a pair**, but it is not a universal translation of “pair.” To use it correctly, you need to understand what relationship connects the two elements and how that type of object is normally counted. 

Shoes, for example, form a pair, but in ordinary counting they are normally counted with 足 (そく, soku). Whether 対 is appropriate often depends precisely on this distinction between the meaning of “pair” and conventional counting practices.


In this short article (light on theory and very much focused on practical usage), we will look at when to use **対 (つい)** to indicate a pair and when a more specific counter or a different expression is preferable.
Through comparisons and examples, we will clarify exactly what is being counted in each case and which Japanese word or counter is appropriate for each meaning.

## 対 (つい, tsui): meaning and use as a Japanese counter for pairs

In the sense relevant here, **対 refers to two elements considered together because they correspond to each other, are matched, or form a recognizable set**. Their relationship may be based on shape, function, decoration, or the way they are arranged.

**The two elements do not necessarily have to be identical.** They may differ in size, occupy complementary positions, or feature details that echo one another. Even two objects that can be used separately may form a pair: being paired does not require each item to become unusable without the other.

For this reason, 対 conveys more information than the simple number two. It presents the elements as belonging together as a pair. The mere presence of two objects of the same type is not enough to communicate this relationship: two randomly chosen objects are still just two objects.

---

## How 対 works as a counter

When used as a counter, 対 follows the numeral. **一対 is read いっつい (ittsui)** and means one pair; 二対, read につい (nitsui), means two pairs. When 対 is used to count ordinary two-item pairs, the number therefore refers to complete pairs: two pairs contain four individual elements. 

A numeral + 対 combination can modify a noun with の, following the pattern **number + 対 + の + noun**. 対 can also function as an independent noun meaning “pair” or “pairing,” without being preceded by a number. Recognizing this distinction helps separate the counting of pairs from the simple description of a relationship between two elements.

The reading to learn for this counter is つい. The kanji also has the reading たい, which appears in other uses, including comparisons and numerical ratios: the intended reading and meaning have to be determined from context.

---

## When 対 is a natural choice

**対 works well for objects conceived, recognized, or presented as a matching pair**. This includes matching vases, corresponding decorative elements, candlesticks arranged in pairs, and figures occupying complementary positions. In these contexts, the counting retains the idea of the set: what matters is how many complete pairs there are, not just how many individual pieces. 

This usage is not limited to antique or ceremonial objects. Earrings can also be described and counted as a pair with 一対. In descriptions of jewelry and accessories, this expression highlights the relationship between the two elements. Decorative differences between the right and left item do not prevent them from being treated as a pair if the pairing is intentional. 

The choice still depends on what is being counted. If the earrings are considered separately, the unit may be the individual piece; if the complete matching set is considered, the unit is the pair. The counter therefore helps clarify how the items are grouped for counting.

---

## Shoes, gloves, and chopsticks: when other counters are used

Many objects that form a pair have a more specific conventional counter. This means that recognizing a relationship between two elements and choosing the natural way to count them are two separate steps.

### Shoes and socks: 足 (そく)

For shoes, socks, and other items worn on the feet in pairs, the standard counter is **足 (そく, soku)**. 一足 is read いっそく (issoku) and, in this use, refers to a complete pair, with one item for each foot. The number refers to complete pairs.

A single shoe therefore does not normally constitute 一足 when counting pairs. Even the literal meaning of the kanji 足 as “foot” can be misleading here: as a counter, it refers to a pair of items worn on the feet. 

To express the quantity of shoes or socks in ordinary usage, 足 is the counter to learn. It is still possible to describe the two parts through their relationship as a pair: recognizing that they form a 対 does not require using 対 as the unit of counting. For this reason, choosing the appropriate counter requires distinguishing between counting the items themselves and describing them as a pair.

### Gloves: 双 (そう) and 組 (くみ)

For gloves, **双 (そう, sō)** is used: 一双, read いっそう (issō), includes both gloves in the pair. This unit also appears regularly on packaging and in descriptions of work gloves.

Another possibility is **組 (くみ, kumi)**, which presents the elements as a set. With gloves, the context makes it clear that this set normally includes the right and left glove.

対 can likewise express the pairing, but knowing its general meaning of “pair” is not enough to make it the most usual counter in every case. Nor is there any need to posit a rule according to which 双 is reserved for two perfectly identical objects while 対 is used for two different ones: the definitions of the two counters overlap, while preferences depend on the objects themselves and on established usage. 

### Eating chopsticks: 膳 (ぜん)

Chopsticks used for eating are normally counted with **膳 (ぜん, zen)**. 一膳 is read いちぜん (ichizen) and includes the two chopsticks needed to make one pair.

Individual chopsticks, by contrast, are counted with 本 (ほん, hon). The two units answer different questions: 膳 counts complete pairs, while 本 counts the individual chopsticks as long, slender objects. Confusing them can change the actual quantity being requested. 

Here too, the meaning of “pair” remains recognizable, but counting conventions favor a specific unit. For cooking chopsticks and other similar utensils, counters such as 組 are also encountered: 膳 should therefore be learned first and foremost in connection with chopsticks used for eating.

When counting disposable chopsticks that are still joined together or packaged, 本 may also be used for the unit being handed out. This is another reason to identify exactly what is being counted in context; when you want to refer clearly to the pair used for a meal, 膳 remains the standard counter.

---


## 対, 組, and ペア: what is the difference?

**組 (くみ)** refers to a set of elements treated as a single unit. 一組 is read ひとくみ (hitokumi). The set may contain two elements, but it can also contain more than two: the counter itself does not specify how many parts make up the set.

When the object or context makes it clear that the group consists of two pieces, 一組 can correspond to the English “a pair.” In other cases, it means “a group,” “a set,” or “a collection.” The number of elements within the set has to be inferred from what is being counted. 

**ペア (pea)**, a loanword from English pair, by contrast highlights a pairing of two elements or two people. It is common when talking about matching accessories, activities performed in pairs, and participants who work or play together. 

対, 組, and ペア can therefore overlap, but they direct attention differently: 対 presents the correspondence between the elements, 組 the set treated as a unit, and ペア the pairing of two. These differences help with interpretation; the actual choice also depends on the conventional expression used for each object.

---

## Pairs of people and animals: how to express them

When counting pairs of people, **組 is a normal choice**: it allows the pairs to be counted as groups. If the number of individuals is what matters, people are counted with 人. ペア is especially suitable when two people are paired for an activity; カップル, by contrast, commonly evokes a romantic relationship.

対 can also refer to people, especially when the aim is to describe how well they go together or correspond to each other. This can also be seen in 好一対 (こういっつい, kōittsui), which expresses a particularly harmonious pairing. 

This descriptive value explains why 一対 can appear in literary texts referring to a couple without thereby becoming the preferred counter for ordinary counting of people.

For animals, **つがい (tsugai)** is also used, especially for a pair consisting of a male and a female. Here too, a relationship between the two individuals is being recognized. To indicate simply that two animals are present, the numeral and the counter appropriate to the species are used, without any need to present them as a pair. 

---

## When 対 is not an appropriate translation of “a pair”

### Two objects without a pairing relationship

Two objects do not automatically form a pair simply because there are two of them. If they are being counted as separate items, Japanese normally uses the number two together with the counter appropriate to the objects.

Using 対 instead would present the two elements as forming a pair or coordinated set. The important question is therefore not simply how many objects there are, but whether a meaningful pairing relationship exists between them.

### Glasses, trousers, and scissors: a single object

English uses “a pair” for glasses, trousers, and scissors even though each of these is a single complete object. In Japanese, these objects are counted according to their respective conventions.

For **glasses and trousers, 本 (ほん) is commonly used**; for **scissors, the traditional counter is 丁 (ちょう, chō)**. In these uses, the complete object is what is being counted: a pair of trousers is one garment, and a pair of scissors is one tool. 

The presence of two lenses, two trouser legs, or two blades does not automatically make 対 appropriate. Using it simply to translate the English “pair” would impose a pairing distinction where ordinary Japanese counting already treats the item as a single complete object.

### Approximate quantities: a couple of and a few

English expressions such as a couple of and a few can indicate an approximate quantity rather than an exact count. 対 does not express this kind of approximation.

You therefore need to determine how precise the intended quantity is before choosing the appropriate Japanese expression, counter, or unit of measurement. If the topic is time, for example, hours or days are counted directly, while approximation must be expressed separately.

---

## How to indicate just one element of a pair

When only one member of a pair is relevant, **片方 (かたほう, katahō)** is useful; it refers to one of the two elements. This word allows you to refer to one shoe, one glove, or one of two matching objects while keeping its relationship to the other element explicit. 

**An individual element can also be counted with the counter appropriate to its nature**. The choice depends on what needs to be communicated: how many separate units there are, or which member of the pair is missing, available, or under consideration.

Moving from counting pairs to counting individual pieces therefore requires attention to the unit being counted. A quantity expressed with 対, 足, or 双 has to be interpreted according to the type of set being counted; changing the counter does not necessarily preserve the same total number of objects.

---

## A special use: 対 for a coordinated set of more than two elements

In artistic terminology and some traditional usages, 対 can also refer to a coordinated set containing more than two elements. One example is **三幅対 (さんぷくつい, sanpukutsui)**, a set of three hanging scroll paintings conceived or presented as a coordinated work. 

This usage preserves the sense of a “corresponding” or “complete” set and explains why an absolute definition such as “対 always means exactly two objects” would be too rigid. In ordinary usage as a counter for pairs, the unit normally consists of two elements; in specialized contexts, however, the type of set involved has to be identified from context.

## Final examples


*<p class="jp-example">このピアスは一対で五千円です。</p>*
*"This pair of earrings costs five thousand yen."*

Note: 一対 (いっつい) presents the two earrings as one complete pair. The price refers to the pair, which consists of two elements that need not be identical.

*<p class="jp-example">旅行のために靴を二足買いました。</p>*
*"I bought two pairs of shoes for the trip."*

Note: 二足 (にそく) means two complete pairs, and therefore four shoes. 足 is the standard counter for shoes and socks, even though these objects can also be described as a pair using 対.

*<p class="jp-example">この箱には作業用の手袋が三双入っています。</p>*
*"This box contains three pairs of work gloves."*

Note: 三双 (さんそう) means three pairs of gloves, and therefore six gloves in total. 双 is regularly used when counting and packaging work gloves.

*<p class="jp-example">箸を二膳用意してください。</p>*
*"Please prepare two pairs of chopsticks."*

Note: 二膳 (にぜん) means two complete pairs of eating chopsticks, and therefore four chopsticks. 膳 counts the set intended for a meal, whereas 本 can count the individual chopsticks.

*<p class="jp-example">二人一組でこの問題を解いてください。</p>*
*"Solve this problem in pairs."*

Note: 一組 (ひとくみ) refers to a group treated as a single unit. Here the group consists of two people because 二人 (ふたり) specifies the number: 組 by itself does not establish that the group must contain two elements.

*<p class="jp-example">次の練習では、隣の人とペアになってください。</p>*
*"For the next exercise, pair up with the person next to you."*

Note: ペア here describes two people being paired to perform an activity together. The expression ペアになる means “to form a pair” or “to pair up”; in this sentence, the number of pairs is not being counted.

*<p class="jp-example">池には一つがいのカモがいます。</p>*
*"There is a pair of ducks in the pond."*

Note: 一つがい (ひとつがい) presents the ducks as a male-female pair. To indicate simply that there are two ducks, without presenting them as a male-female pair, the individual birds would be counted instead.

*<p class="jp-example">箸が一本しかありません。</p>*
*"There is only one chopstick."*

Note: 一本 (いっぽん) counts a single chopstick here. The quantity is different from 一膳 (いちぜん), which includes the two chopsticks needed to make a complete pair.

*<p class="jp-example">旅行にズボンを二本持っていきます。</p>*
*"I'm taking two pairs of trousers on the trip."*

Note: 二本 (にほん) counts two complete garments. Although English says “two pairs of trousers,” in Japanese each garment is treated as one complete object: the presence of two legs does not require 対.

*<p class="jp-example">はさみを一丁貸してください。</p>*
*"Please lend me a pair of scissors."*

Note: 一丁 (いっちょう) counts one complete tool. 丁 is the traditional counter for scissors: the two blades belong to the same object rather than constituting two separate tools counted as a pair.

## Conclusion

In this article, we have seen that to express “a pair” correctly in Japanese, the fundamental distinction is between **the relationship connecting the elements and the unit used to count them**. 

対 (つい) presents the elements as a recognizable pairing, but the fact that two objects form a pair does not automatically make this counter the most natural choice. 

English distinguishes between a genuine pair, two separate objects, a single object made up of two corresponding parts, and approximate expressions such as a couple of or a few. Japanese likewise requires different counters or expressions depending on which of these meanings is intended.

Usage conventions therefore play a decisive role: shoes and socks are normally counted with 足, gloves with 双 or 組, and eating chopsticks with 膳. 

The broader alternatives also need to be interpreted carefully: 組 counts a set without itself specifying how many elements it contains, whereas ペア highlights a pairing of two. Artistic and traditional uses of 対 also remind us that context can extend the idea of correspondence to a larger coordinated set.

For learners, a useful approach is to **learn the name of an object together with its usual counter**, paying attention to whether the unit consists of a single item, a complete pair, or a set. 

Before translating “a pair,” it is worth asking what you actually want to communicate: the number of objects, the fact that they are paired, or an approximate quantity. This habit also helps distinguish a pair from just one of its members and helps prevent a change of counter from unintentionally changing the quantity being expressed.
