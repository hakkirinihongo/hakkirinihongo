What does ～させられる really mean in Japanese, and what is the difference between its standard use and its emotional use?

The Japanese form ～させられる is usually explained as the **causative-passive form**, that is, as a structure meaning *“to be forced to do something”* or *“to be made to do something by someone”*.

This is its best-known and most easily recognizable use: someone imposes an action, the subject undergoes it, and the sentence often carries a negative nuance of obligation, annoyance, or lack of control.

For example, a sentence such as 親に勉強させられた means *“I was forced by my parents to study”*. Here the mechanism is clear: the subject does not act freely, but is pushed or forced by another person to perform an action.

However, ～させられる is not limited to this meaning. In modern Japanese, there is a very important use, common both in writing and in educated speech, in which the causative-passive does not express external coercion, but an emotional, mental, or sensory reaction that arises spontaneously in the subject.

This is the case with expressions such as:

考えさせられる

感動させられる

泣かされる

驚かされる

がっかりさせられる

ドキッとさせられる

These forms do not mean *“to be forced to think”*, *“to be forced to be moved”*, or *“to be forced to cry”*.

Their real meaning is more subtle: **something affects the subject** in such a way that it triggers a reaction that is not entirely under their control.

In other words, the subject does not voluntarily decide to think, be moved, cry, or be surprised: it is the experience, the scene, someone’s words, or a particular situation that spontaneously gives rise to that reaction.

For this reason, 考えさせられる does not simply mean *“to be made to think”*, but rather *“to be led to reflect”*, *“to be unable to help thinking about it”*.

Similarly:

* 感動させられる does not indicate coercion, but the fact of being deeply moved by something;
* 泣かされる can indicate that a story, a scene, or a phrase made us cry despite ourselves;
* 驚かされる expresses surprise caused by something that struck us from the outside.

The difference between the standard use and the emotional use of ～させられる is therefore essential:

* In the standard use, the subject undergoes an action imposed by someone: “I was forced to do X”.

* In the emotional use, by contrast, the subject undergoes the effect of something that generates a spontaneous response within them: “I was led to feel X”, “I found myself X-ing”, “something made me feel/think X”.

This distinction is especially important for intermediate and advanced learners of Japanese, because many sentences with ～させられる are not well translated as *“to be forced to…”*.

Translating them this way risks distorting the meaning of the sentence and losing one of the most interesting nuances of Japanese grammar: the way an apparently passive and causative form can express emotional involvement, involuntary reaction, and personal perception.

In this article, we will therefore analyze the meaning of ～させられる, its grammatical function, the difference between the standard causative-passive and the emotional use, and the reason why forms such as 考えさせられる, 感動させられる, 泣かされる, and 驚かされる are so common in real Japanese.

We will also see why this use, often overlooked in textbooks, is essential for reading texts, listening to natural dialogues, and grasping that area of the language where grammar, emotion, and subjectivity meet.

## ～させられる (saserareru) - standard meaning, causative-passive form, and syntax rules

In modern Japanese, **～させられる** is the so-called **causative-passive form**, in Japanese **使役受身形（しえきうけみけい）**.

The very name of the construction makes its nature clear: it is the combination of **使役**, that is, causation, and **受身**, that is, the passive.

In other words, an action is not simply performed by the subject, but **is made to be performed by the subject by someone else**, and the subject presents it from their own point of view as something they undergo.

In its most classic and most commonly taught use, **～させられる** therefore indicates that someone is **forced, obliged, or made to do something**. The structure does not merely describe a sequence of events, but brings to the foreground the relationship between the person exerting pressure and the person who is forced to act.

The subject of the sentence does not appear as a free and spontaneous agent, but as someone who performs the action because another person imposes it on them, makes them do it, or creates the conditions that require them to do it.

For this reason, **～させられる** is often explained as *“to be forced to do”*, *“to be obliged to do”*, or *“to be made to do”*. However, these English translations only convey the final result.

From the Japanese point of view, the mechanism is more precise. In what can be considered, in every respect, a small “grammatical dissection”:

* The causative verb **～させる** indicates that someone makes someone else perform an action;
* The passive **～られる** then shifts the point of view to the person who receives this causation.

The sentence is therefore built from the perspective of the person who undergoes the imposition.

A very simple example is:

*<p class="jp-example">両親に勉強させられた。</p>*

“**I was forced to study by my parents.**”

In this sentence:

* The implied subject is the speaker;
* The parents, marked by **に**, are the source of the imposition;
* **勉強する** is the action the speaker had to perform;
* **勉強させられた** presents that action as something the speaker did not choose freely, but underwent.

The form does not simply say *“I studied”*, but **“I was made to study”**, with a nuance of obligation, pressure, or lack of willingness.

The typical structure can be schematized as follows:

**A は / が + B に + causative-passive verb.**

where **A** is the person forced to act, **B** is the person or force imposing the action, and the verb in **～させられる** indicates what A is forced to do.

In actual sentences, however, the subject is often omitted, especially when it coincides with the speaker:

*<p class="jp-example">先生に作文を書かせられた。</p>*

“**I was forced by the teacher to write an essay.**”

Here too, the core of the sentence is not simply *“writing an essay”*, but the fact that the action was imposed from the outside.

The particle **に** normally marks the person who causes or imposes the action, while the object of the action remains marked by **を**, as in **作文を書かせられた**.

From the point of view of formation, it is important to avoid oversimplifying. **～させられる** is not mechanically attached to the dictionary form, but arises from the causative form of the verb, to which the passive is then applied.

For Group II verbs, the formation is regular:

**食べる → 食べさせる → 食べさせられる**
**見る → 見させる → 見させられる**

For irregular verbs:

**する → させる → させられる**
**来る → 来させる → 来させられる**

For Group I verbs, by contrast, the full form is built with the **-a stem + せられる**:

**行く → 行かせられる**
**読む → 読ませられる**
**書く → 書かせられる**

In real Japanese, however, many Group I verbs also have a contracted form in **-される**, which is very common in speech and in many natural contexts:

**行かせられる → 行かされる**
**読ませられる → 読まされる**
**書かせられる → 書かされる**
**飲ませられる → 飲まされる**

These contracted forms do not change the basic meaning: **行かされた** still means “I was forced to go”, **読まされた** means *“I was forced to read”*, and **書かされた** means “I was forced to write”.

What mainly changes is the degree of naturalness and frequency: in spoken Japanese, the contracted forms of Group I verbs are often much more common than the full forms.

However, care is needed with Group I verbs ending in **-す**, such as **話す**, **出す**, **直す**, **消す**, and **返す**.

In these cases, the contracted form in **-さされる** is generally not considered standard. For this reason, one normally says:

**話す → 話させられる**
**出す → 出させられる**
**直す → 直させられる**

and not, in standard Japanese, forms such as **話さされる** or **出さされる**.

In its standard meaning, then, **～させられる** contains three fundamental elements: a subject who performs the action, an external force that induces or obliges them to perform it, and a nuance of passivity, annoyance, or lack of control.

This is why sentences such as **働かされた**, **待たされた**, **言わされた**, and **書かされた** do not merely describe what happened, but also communicate that the subject experienced the action as imposed, undergone, or not fully voluntary.

This is the traditional explanation of the causative-passive form, and it is the one most often found in textbooks: **～させられる = to be forced to do something**.

However, this definition is not enough to explain all the real sentences in which the form appears.

Expressions such as **考えさせられる**, **感動させられる**, **驚かされる**, **泣かされる**, **がっかりさせられる**, or **ドキッとさせられる** do not necessarily indicate concrete coercion.

In these cases, in fact, the causative-passive form does not describe someone physically or socially forcing the subject to perform an action, but something that triggers a mental, emotional, or perceptual reaction in them.

This is the subtler and often overlooked use that we will analyze in the following sections.


**Note**: The same causative base ～(さ)せる also appears in the ～させてもらう construction, but the perspective is structured in a completely different way. 

In this case, no passive form is added: the subject remains **the person who actively performs the action** and, through もらう, presents being able to perform it as something made possible by another person’s consent, permission, or assistance.

A comparison with [～させてもらう, its meaning and nuances of permission](/en/articles/sasete-morau-meaning-usage-japanese-grammar/) therefore reveals two very different ways in which the same causative base can develop: on the one hand, the subject **is given the opportunity to act**; on the other, the subject **receives or undergoes the effect of the causation**.

## Spontaneous reaction (emotional or mental)

With verbs that express feelings, emotions, or mental activity, ～させられる can indicate that **a certain feeling or thought has arisen spontaneously in the subject, triggered by something or someone**.

In this case, there is no negative nuance of coercion, but rather a sense of spontaneity or involuntariness in the emotion or thought that is produced.

For example:

*彼の話に考えさせられた* - *His words made me reflect*.

Literally, *“I was made to think by his words”*, meaning that they led me to think.

In sentences of this kind, the subject involuntarily experiences a certain emotional or mental state because of an external event; or, to put it in a way that captures the meaning expressed by this construction even more clearly, **the subject is pushed by an external factor toward that emotional or mental state**.

This emotional use of させられる is connected to the way Japanese expresses interpersonal relationships, as also happens with expressions such as [すみません](/en/articles/sumimasen-origin-meaning-history-difference-gomennasai-moushiwakearimasen/), and [ありがとう](/en/articles/arigatou-gozaimasu-meaning-origin-and-how-to-use-it/).

Therefore, when ～させられる is used with verbs of emotion or mental activity, its meaning moves away from the typical idea of *“making someone do something against their will”* and takes on a value of emotional spontaneity. This use is common with verbs such as:


**考える (“to think, to reflect”)** – 考えさせられる means *“to be led to reflect (by something)”*, or *“something makes me reflect”*. 


Example: *この本を読んで考えさせられた* -  *"Reading this book made me reflect deeply"*.

In other words, the book triggered a reflection in me (one that arose spontaneously).

**驚く／びっくりする (“to be surprised, to be startled”)** – 驚かされる (the causative-passive of 驚かす) or びっくりさせられる means *“to be surprised, to be startled (because of something)”*.

Example: *毎回彼のアイデアに驚かされる* - *"Every time, I am surprised by his ideas"* (lit. *“every time, I am made to be surprised by his ideas”*).

Similarly:

*あの場所の景色の美しさに驚かされました* - *"I was amazed by the beauty of the scenery in that place*".

In these sentences, 驚かされる is equivalent to *“I am involuntarily surprised”*, emphasizing the spontaneous reaction of amazement caused by something unexpected.

**感動する (“to be moved, to be deeply touched”)** – 感動させられる means *“to be deeply moved (by something)”*.

Example: *私は彼の熱のこもったスピーチに感動させられた* – *"His passionate speech moved me"*.

Here, 感動させられた emphasizes that the feeling of being moved arose spontaneously in the listener because of the touching speech (it does not imply coercion at all; **no one forces you to be moved**, the reaction is natural). An equivalent expression in English might be *“I was deeply moved by his speech”*.


**感心する (“to admire, to be impressed in a positive way”)** – 感心させられる means *“to be left admiring”* or *“to be led to admire (someone/something)”*. 


For example: *彼女の礼儀正しさには感心させられます* – *"Her politeness leaves me genuinely impressed"*.

In this case, the speaker expresses that they cannot help admiring her good manners; their admiration arose spontaneously in response to that quality.


**泣く (“to cry”) – 泣かされる** means *“to be made to cry by something/someone”*, often in the sense that something brought tears to one’s eyes. 


Example: *あの映画には本当に泣かされました* – *"That film really made me cry"*.

Here, the speaker means that the film was so sad or moving that it made them cry; 泣かされました expresses that the tears came involuntarily under the emotional effect of the film. (It should be noted that 泣かされる **does not necessarily imply an intentional act of coercion** – it usually does not mean *“to be forced to cry by force”*, but rather *“to be moved to tears”* by emotion).

### Difference between 泣かされた and 泣いてしまった

A comparison that, in our view, is especially useful and deserves closer attention is the one between:

* 泣かされた
* 泣いてしまった

Both expressions can be translated as *“I cried”*, but the point of view changes.

泣かされた shifts the focus onto the external cause that triggered the emotional reaction. The event itself—the film, the speech, the situation—is the real driving force behind the action.

泣いてしまった, by contrast, places the focus on the action performed by the subject, often with a nuance of surprise, inevitability, or slight regret.

In other words:

* 泣かされた → “something made me cry.”
* 泣いてしまった → “I ended up crying.”

This comparison helps us understand how **～させられる highlights the semantic pressure exerted by the external cause**.

In all these examples, ～させられる emphasizes that **the subject’s emotional reaction occurred outside their conscious will**, almost despite themselves, but without any negative nuance of imposition.

It is a way of shifting the focus onto the external cause that gave rise to the emotion or thought. In English, these sentences can often be rendered with constructions such as *“it made me [feel emotion]”* or *“I was left [emotional adjective]”*, which capture the idea of an emotion arising spontaneously in response to something.


In terms of syntax, as with the standard use, these sentences also follow the structure “Xに～させられる”, where X (marked by に) is the external agent or cause that triggers the reaction, and the verb in the causative-passive form describes the subject’s reaction. 


For example, in the sentence *彼の話に考えさせられた*, 彼の話 (*“his words/story”*) is the element that caused the subject’s involuntary reflection (with 私, *“I”*, left implicit).

Similarly, 映画に泣かされた indicates that the film is the causal agent that made the subject cry.


It is important to note that **not all verbs can take on this spontaneous meaning with ～させられる**. This use is limited to **verbs that express feelings, sensations, or involuntary mental activities**. 


For example, with ordinary action verbs such as 食べる (*“to eat”*) or 行く (*“to go”*), ～させられる does not indicate spontaneity, but rather the normal coercive causative-passive form.

If we tried to use ～させられる with a verb like 走る (*“to run”*) without a clear emotional context, it would naturally be interpreted as *“to be forced to run”* rather than *“to start running spontaneously”*.

To summarize: the semantics of ～させられる changes depending on the type of verb. With verbs of emotion or thought, it takes on the meaning of a spontaneous reaction, while with other verbs it retains the meaning of an imposed action that the subject undergoes.

## Context and register of ～させられる in its emotional use

In its emotional use, **～させられる** does not belong to the simplest colloquial register, but to a fairly natural, mature kind of Japanese that is often slightly more written or reflective.

It is often found in reviews, comments, short essays, articles, personal reflections, and descriptions of experiences that have left a strong impression.

The point is not to say that someone was *“forced”* to feel something, but that an event, a scene, a word, or a person produced an intense and partly involuntary reaction in the subject.

For this reason, expressions such as **考えさせられる**, **感動させられる**, **驚かされる**, **がっかりさせられる**, or **ドキッとさせられる** are very natural when the speaker wants to present their emotion not as a rational choice, but as something triggered from the outside.

In this use, the causative-passive form retains the idea of *“being led to”*, but almost entirely loses the sense of concrete imposition typical of sentences such as **勉強させられる** or **働かされる**.


The two constructions overlap in a particularly interesting way when an emotional ～させられる form is used within the ～ものがある construction:

*<p class="jp-example">この映画には、何度見ても考えさせられるものがある。</p>*  
*"There’s something about this film that makes you think, no matter how many times you watch it."*

In a sentence like this, **考えさせられる** describes the mental reaction the film elicits in the viewer without that reaction being fully voluntary, while **ものがある** presents the ability to elicit that reaction as a quality perceived in the film itself. The two constructions therefore operate at different but complementary levels: ～させられる focuses on the reaction experienced by the person affected, whereas ～ものがある presents the capacity to produce that reaction as “something” perceived as being present in the person, object, or situation being evaluated.

For a closer look at this second mechanism, see the article on [～ものがある: meaning, grammar, and nuances](/en/articles/saserareru-emotional-causative-passive-japanese/).

## In summary: when ～させられる indicates a spontaneous reaction

We can recognize the emotional use of ～させられる when:

* The verb expresses an emotion, a mental state, or a psychological reaction;
* There is no idea of voluntary imposition by an agent;
* The focus is placed on the external cause that triggers the reaction;
* The simple passive form (～られる) would sound ambiguous or unnatural.

In these cases, ～させられる does not mean *“to be forced”*, but **“to be involuntarily led to feel something”**.

## Conclusion

The emotional use of **～させられる** clearly shows why the Japanese causative-passive form cannot be reduced to the single translation *“to be forced to do something”*. This meaning does exist, and it is certainly the most classic one: sentences such as **勉強させられる**, **働かされる**, or **待たされる** indicate a situation in which the subject undergoes an imposition, pressure, or external coercion.

However, in expressions such as **考えさせられる**, **感動させられる**, **驚かされる**, **泣かされる**, **がっかりさせられる**, or **ドキッとさせられる**, the mechanism is different.

In these cases, the subject is not concretely forced to perform an action. Rather, something spontaneously leads them to think, be moved, be surprised, cry, feel disappointed, or experience an emotional jolt.

The external cause is still present, but it does not function as an imposition: it functions as a stimulus, an occasion, or an emotional force that triggers an internal reaction.

For this reason, **考えさせられる** does not simply mean *“to be forced to think”*, but *“to be led to reflect”*; **感動させられる** does not mean *“to be forced to be moved”*, but *“to be deeply moved”*; **驚かされる** does not mean *“to be obliged to be surprised”*, but *“to be strongly or unexpectedly surprised by something”*.

The central point, then, is the relationship between **external cause** and **involuntary reaction**.

The form **～させられる** preserves a trace of causativity: something *“brings about”* a thought, a perception, or an emotion in the subject.

At the same time, it also preserves a passive nuance: the subject does not fully control that reaction, but undergoes it as something that happens internally. It is precisely this double nature that makes expressions such as **考えさせられる映画**, **感動させられる話**, **がっかりさせられる結果**, or **ドキッとさせられる一言** so natural in real Japanese.

From a lexical point of view, this use must also be distinguished from the simple **～れる／～られる** form.

In Japanese, in fact, **れる／られる** does not express only the passive, but can also have a spontaneous value, indicating a thought, perception, or feeling that arises naturally in the subject.

For this reason, forms such as **思い出される** can mean *“it comes back to mind”*, and **感じられる** can mean *“it can be perceived / it is felt spontaneously”*. In these cases, the simple passive form is already enough to suggest that something emerges in the subject’s mind or sensitivity without a direct voluntary act.

With many **～する** verbs related to emotion, thought, or psychological reaction, however, the simple passive form can sound unnatural, unclear, or semantically different.

For example, **彼に感動された** is not the natural way to say *“I was moved by him”*; the correct form is **彼に感動させられた**.

Similarly, **考えられる** is normally interpreted as *“one can think”*, *“it is conceivable”*, or *“it is possible to consider”*, not as *“to be led to reflect”*. To express this latter meaning, Japanese uses **考えさせられる**.

This explains why the emotional use of **～させられる** is so common with verbs such as **感動する**, **がっかりする**, **ドキッとする**, **びっくりする**, **納得する**, or **考える**.

The form allows the speaker to say that a certain external cause has produced an intense mental or emotional reaction in the subject, without presenting it as a voluntary choice. The subject does not decide to be moved, does not decide to be disappointed, does not decide to reflect: they are led there by the force of what they see, hear, read, or experience.

In conclusion, **～させられる** has two major faces:

* In its standard use, it indicates a coercion undergone by the subject: someone makes me do something against my will;

* In its emotional use, by contrast, it indicates a triggered reaction: something leads me to feel, think, or perceive something spontaneously and often intensely.

The grammatical form is the same, but the value changes profoundly: from external imposition on a concrete action to an external stimulus that generates an inner response.

For this reason, when faced with forms such as **考えさせられる** or **感動させられる**, we should not automatically translate them as *“to be forced to”*.

We need to ask what kind of verb follows, what kind of cause is present, and what kind of reaction is being described.

* If the verb indicates a concrete action, the coercive reading is often natural;
* If, on the other hand, it indicates thought, emotion, perception, or psychological reaction, **～させられる** tends to express the idea of “being led to”, “being brought to”, or “being so affected that one” feels something.

This is where the Japanese causative-passive reveals one of its most interesting nuances: not only what someone forces us to do, but also what the world, almost without meaning to, forces us to feel.
