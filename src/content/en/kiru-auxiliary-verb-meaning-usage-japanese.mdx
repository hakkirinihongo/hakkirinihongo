**What does ～切る (きる, kiru) mean in Japanese, how do you use it in compound verbs, and how does it differ from the auxiliary verb ～終わる (おわる, owaru)?**

**～切る** expresses the idea of **completeness** in Japanese: 読み切る means “to read something all the way through,” 使い切る means “to use something up completely,” and 疲れ切る means “to be utterly exhausted.” Its meaning therefore goes beyond simply “finishing.” Depending on the verb, it can emphasize completing an action in full, exhausting a resource, or the intensity of a condition.


In practice, the auxiliary verb **～切る** (we will consistently use this spelling when referring to the auxiliary, to distinguish it more clearly from 切る as an independent verb) attaches to another verb to express that an action is carried out **completely, all the way through**, or that a change or state reaches **a complete or extreme degree**. 

Depending on the compound and its context, ～切る can therefore correspond to expressions such as **“do something completely,” “see something through,” “use something up,” or “be completely [adjective]”**. Its contribution is to emphasize the reaching of a limit: something is accomplished in its entirety, a resource is exhausted, or a condition is fully manifested.

These meanings derive from the independent verb **切る**, “to cut” or “to sever,” and from the kanji **切** used to write it. The character primarily conveys the idea of separating something by cutting it, but its range of meanings also includes delimitation, totality, and endings. Even as an independent verb, 切る can refer to something abstract: **ending a conversation, severing a relationship, or setting a limit**. 

To understand how this connects to its auxiliary function, we can start with this very idea of a boundary. In the compounds we will examine, that boundary concerns the completion of an action or the degree to which a condition has developed. The image of cutting thus offers a way to connect meanings that require quite different translations in English.

In terms of formation, ～切る attaches to the **連用形（れんようけい）** of the preceding verb, commonly introduced to learners as the stem used with ～ます. This produces compounds such as **読み切る（よみきる）**, “to read something in its entirety, right through to the last page”; **食べ切る（たべきる）**, “to eat everything, leaving nothing behind”; **使い切る（つかいきる）**, “to use something up completely”; and **走り切る（はしりきる）**, “to run the entire required distance.” 

The base verb tells us what is being done, while ～切る emphasizes that the action reaches the relevant limit: the whole text, all the food, the available quantity, or the distance to be covered. 

In some contexts, there may also be a sense of satisfaction at having completed something demanding, but difficulty and effort are not necessarily part of every use.

Completeness can also concern the degree of a condition. **疲れ切る（つかれきる）** means “to be utterly exhausted,” while **冷え切る（ひえきる）** indicates that something has become completely cold and can figuratively describe relationships that have become completely cold as well. In these cases, ～切る highlights the state that has been reached. With **信じ切る（しんじきる）**, by contrast, it expresses absolute belief or trust, without reservations. 

Some compounds also have particular nuances, such as **言い切る（いいきる）**, which can mean either “to say something all the way through” or “to state something categorically, without hesitation.” The precise meaning therefore arises from the interaction between the base verb, ～切る, and the context.

When used to express the completion of an action, ～切る is similar to **～終わる（おわる, owaru）**, but the two forms emphasize different aspects. 

- **～終わる presents the action as finished**; 
- **～切る emphasizes that it has been carried out in full**, sometimes to the point of exhausting what is being used. 

The difference is particularly clear between **使い終わる**, “to finish using something,” and **使い切る**, “to use it all up”: finishing using a product for a particular activity does not necessarily mean that none of it remains. 

With other verbs, the distinction can be subtler. Both **読み終わる** and **読み切る** can refer to finishing an entire book, but the latter places more emphasis on having read the whole thing, right through to the end. The choice therefore also depends on how the speaker wishes to present the outcome.

This variety makes it important to examine individual compounds: ～切る cannot be freely combined with just any verb, and its presence does not always indicate an auxiliary function. In **噛み切る（かみきる）**, “to bite through,” for example, the concrete meaning of “cutting” remains recognizable. 

Distinguishing such cases from uses expressing completeness or intensity helps clarify the relationship between the original meaning of 切る and the different functions it takes on in compound verbs.

In this article, we will explore the **meaning and usage of ～切る in Japanese grammar**, then examine its **formation and grammatical structure**. We will compare **base verbs with their corresponding ～切る compounds** and revisit how these compounds differ from forms ending in ～終わる. 

We will then look at **register and contexts of use**, before exploring the construction through a series of **usage examples**. In the **conclusion**, we will bring together the key points for recognizing the different meanings of ～切る and knowing when to use the construction appropriately.

## ～切る (きる, kiru): Meaning and Usage

To understand how **～切る** works in compound verbs, it helps to distinguish from the outset between its use as an **independent verb** and as an **auxiliary verb**. In the first case, **切る（きる, kiru）** has a meaning of its own, beginning with the concrete sense of “to cut.” In the second, it combines with another verb to express the completion of an action or the extreme degree of a condition.

We will therefore begin with the independent verb, examining its principal meanings and its literal and figurative uses. We will then turn to its auxiliary function to clarify what nuances **～切る** adds and how they relate to the verb's core meaning.

### 切る as an Independent Verb: Meaning and Uses

As an independent verb, **切る（きる, kiru）** primarily means **“to cut,” “to sever,” or “to separate things that are joined together”**. In its most concrete use, it describes acting upon a physical object, generally with a blade or sharp implement: **紙を切る**, “to cut paper”; **野菜を切る**, “to cut vegetables”; **髪を切る**, “to cut hair.” 

The purpose of cutting may be to divide an object into several parts, remove a portion of it, or shorten it. The precise outcome depends on the object and the context.

The kanji **切** reflects this fundamental meaning: one of its components is **刀**, “sword” or “blade,” evoking the physical act of cutting. Yet the character's range of meanings also encompasses **setting boundaries, endings, and totality**. 

To understand how the verb is used, it is especially helpful to consider how the idea of separating something can apply both to physical objects and to things that have continuity over time or involve a connection between people.

In **電話を切る**, “to end a phone call” or “to hang up,” what is interrupted is communication; in **電源を切る**, “to turn off a device” or “to switch off the power,” the electrical supply is cut off. With **縁を切る**, “to cut ties,” 切る instead refers to breaking a personal connection. 

In these expressions, the verb retains an independent meaning even when its object is abstract: it directly denotes the interruption or separation being carried out.

An interruption can also occur before an activity has been completed. **話を切る**, for example, can mean “to interrupt a conversation”: the point at which someone stops speaking is not necessarily the point at which everything has been said. 

This is a useful distinction for understanding the independent verb: the idea of breaking a continuity does not, by itself, imply that the activity has run its full course or been completed.

Another important use involves **setting a limit or defining a boundary**. In **期限を切る**, “to set a deadline,” 切る indicates that a time limit is established. Here, the verb marks off the available time: it establishes the boundary within which something must happen, without asserting that the activity has already been completed.

The idea of a limit also appears in quantitative expressions. **百メートルで十秒を切る** means “to run 100 meters in under ten seconds”: ten seconds is the threshold below which the time falls. Similarly, the verb can indicate that a price, quantity, or other value falls below a given level.

切る also occurs in expressions whose meaning must be understood as a whole: **野菜の水を切る** means “to drain the vegetables, removing the excess water”; **ハンドルを切る**, “to turn the steering wheel” or “to steer”; **トランプを切る**, “to shuffle a deck of cards.” 

These uses show just how firmly established the verb is in everyday vocabulary, and how much its translation depends on what it refers to. Understanding them requires considering the entire expression, since the English equivalent “to cut” covers only some of the ways 切る is used.

### 切る as an Auxiliary Verb: Meaning and Usage

As an independent verb, **切る** can express separation, interruption, or the setting of a boundary. When it functions as an auxiliary, the literal meaning of cutting recedes, giving way to a more abstract sense: **～切る presents an action as fully completed or a condition as fully realized or reaching an extreme degree**. 

The resulting verb still conveys the activity, change, or state denoted by the base verb, but highlights the achievement of a complete result.

To understand this shift, we can use the idea of **reaching a limit** as a starting point. With the independent verb, a cut creates a separation or breaks a continuity; in auxiliary uses expressing completeness, by contrast, the limit is the point at which the action or condition in question has been realized in full. 

What “completion” means therefore changes: the action reaches completion, the relevant quantity is exhausted, or a condition is fully manifested.

One of the main uses of auxiliary ～切る is precisely to express **the full completion of an action**. **読み切る（よみきる）** means to read a text in its entirety, all the way to the end; **食べ切る（たべきる）** indicates that all the food in question is eaten, with none left over. 

In these compounds, the emphasis falls on carrying the action through in full. Naturally, “all” must be interpreted in relation to the situation: it may refer to the book being discussed, the assigned pages, or the portion served.

When an activity involves consuming something, this meaning can extend to **using up a resource**. **使い切る（つかいきる）** means to use everything available until none remains: a product or a sum of money, for instance. Here, completeness means that none of the resource in question remains.

Completion can also be presented as a result achieved through effort. **走り切る（はしりきる）**, when referring to a race or course, expresses running the entire required distance and may emphasize the achievement of making it to the finish, especially when the context involves fatigue or difficulty. 

This nuance, however, depends on the situation: the auxiliary's core meaning concerns completeness, while the effort involved and any sense of satisfaction arise from the verb and its context.

Completeness can also concern **the full realization of a change**. In **治り切る（なおりきる）**, “to recover completely,” the emphasis is on recovery being complete, with nothing left to heal. The focus thus shifts from carrying out an activity to reaching a new condition. The result is a fully realized state that may persist after the change has occurred.

Another central use of ～切る highlights **the extreme degree of a condition**. **疲れ切る（つかれきる）** means “to be utterly exhausted”: the fatigue is presented as having reached an extreme degree. 

Here, ～切る intensifies the resulting state. The person is still tired, and it is precisely that condition which comes to the fore. Similarly, **冷え切る（ひえきる）** describes something that has grown completely cold or now seems devoid of warmth.

The limit expressed in these cases must be understood in the context of how the speaker presents the situation. A room described as completely cold might, in physical terms, become colder still: ～切る serves to portray it as having lost all warmth. The intensity can also relate to positive conditions, as in **澄み切る（すみきる）**, which describes complete clarity, for instance in the sky or in water.

The same sense of fullness can apply to beliefs and attitudes. **信じ切る（しんじきる）** expresses complete belief or trust, without reservations. With **言い切る（いいきる）**, however, the meaning depends on context: the compound can mean saying something in its entirety, all the way to the end, or **stating it categorically**, firmly and without hesitation. 

The meaning of the resulting verb must therefore be determined from the specific expression in which it appears: completeness may concern how much is said or how firmly it is asserted.

These meanings also help explain why **～切る cannot be combined freely with just any verb**. The expression's meaning must allow for completion, exhaustion, or the attainment of a full degree, and established patterns of usage also matter. 

The idea of a conceivable limit is therefore a useful guide, but does not by itself guarantee that a compound will sound natural. 

In the next section, we will look more closely at which combinations are possible and how they are formed.

## ～切る: Grammatical Structure and Formation Rules

Verbs formed with **～切る** are compound verbs: 

- The first element expresses the action, change, or condition being discussed;
- 切る, in turn, presents it as fully completed or fully realized. 

To form these compounds, we must identify the correct stem of the initial verb. Once the compound has been formed, it conjugates according to the rules for 切る. However, the regularity of the formation pattern should not be confused with whether a given verb can actually combine with this auxiliary.

### Formation: 連用形 + 切る

**～切る attaches to the 連用形（れんようけい） of the preceding verb, also known as its ます (masu) stem**. In the construction we are examining, this is the stem obtained by removing ～ます from the verb's polite form.

The pattern is therefore:

**Verb in its 連用形 + 切る**

For example, **読みます** gives us **読み**, to which 切る is added: **読み切る**, “to read something completely, all the way to the end.” The two elements join directly through this stem, without an intervening particle and without using the ～て form.

How the stem is derived depends on the conjugation class of the initial verb:

- **With godan verbs**, the final syllable changes to its corresponding *-i* row syllable: **読む → 読み → 読み切る**; **走る → 走り → 走り切る**. Godan verbs ending in ～る follow this rule as well.
- **With ichidan verbs**, the final ～る is removed: **食べる → 食べ → 食べ切る**; **疲れる → 疲れ → 疲れ切る**.
- **With する and verbs containing it**, the stem is **し**: **説明する → 説明し → 説明し切る**, “to explain something completely, covering every aspect.”

These steps show how the compound is formed formally. Semantic compatibility and the naturalness of individual combinations require further consideration, which we will address later in this section.

In writing, the kanji 切 can also be replaced by hiragana: **読み切る** and **読みきる** represent the same compound.

### Conjugating Verbs Ending in ～切る

**切る is a godan verb**, and the compound follows its conjugation pattern even when the initial verb belongs to a different class. The first part remains in the stem form used to join the two elements; only the final element, 切る, changes.

Using **読み切る** as our model, the main forms are:

The same pattern applies to **食べ切る**, producing **食べ切ります**, **食べ切らない**, **食べ切った**, and **食べ切って**. The original verb 食べる is ichidan, but the compound's conjugation is determined by 切る.

In auxiliary uses, the sentence's basic structure generally follows that of the initial verb. **本を読み切る**, “to read a book all the way through,” retains the transitive behavior of 読む; **体が冷え切る**, “for the body to become completely cold,” follows the intransitive pattern of 冷える. Thus, ～切る can be used with both transitive and intransitive verbs.

### Potential Forms, Negation, and Resulting States

The potential form of 切る is **切れる**. In compounds, it expresses the ability or possibility of reaching the completion or completeness conveyed by the auxiliary:

**読み切る → 読み切れる → 読み切れない**

**読み切れる** means “to be able to read something all the way through”; **読み切れない** means “to be unable to read it all the way through.” Here too, the first element, 読み, stays unchanged: the potential meaning is expressed by changing the final element.

It is especially important to distinguish **～切らない**, the simple negative form, from **～切れない**, the negative potential form:

- **食べ切らなかった**: “I didn't eat all of it.” The form negates full completion without itself specifying why it was not completed.
- **食べ切れなかった**: “I couldn't eat all of it.” The form indicates that completing it was not possible under the circumstances.

With ～切れない, then, what is impossible is achieving completeness. **読み切れない** can also refer to reading that has already begun or been partially completed: the issue is whether the reader can complete the entire text.

This meaning extends to feelings and attitudes as well. **諦め切れない（あきらめきれない）**, for instance, means “to be unable to give up completely”: the difficulty lies in reaching the point of fully letting go.

The ～て form can also be used to construct expressions describing **the resulting state**. **疲れ切っている** means “to be utterly exhausted” and foregrounds the person's present condition. Similarly, **まだ治り切っていない** means “not to have fully recovered yet”: the change has occurred only partially, and its full result has yet to be reached.

### Which Verbs Can Combine with ～切る?

Having a 連用形 means that a formally suitable stem can be identified, but **it does not automatically make every combination with ～切る sound natural**. The verb's meaning, as understood in context, must lend itself to the sense of completeness or reaching a limit expressed by the auxiliary.

The most recognizable combinations involve:

- **Actions viewed in relation to a defined task, quantity, or course**, as in 読み切る, 食べ切る, 使い切る, and 走り切る;
- **Changes whose full realization can be emphasized**, as in 治り切る, “to recover completely,” and 溶け切る, “to melt or dissolve completely”;
- **Conditions or attitudes presented as total or extreme**, as in 疲れ切る, 冷え切る, and 信じ切る.

These categories provide a useful guide to how such combinations are interpreted. However, the duration of an action, whether it is voluntary, or whether it can be described as more or less intense are not, on their own, sufficient to determine which compounds are idiomatic. What also matters is how Japanese expresses the particular action or condition, as well as the combinations that are established in actual usage.

A revealing example involves **分かる** and **知る**. **分かり切ったこと** is a perfectly normal expression meaning “something completely obvious, already perfectly clear.” But we cannot automatically infer from this that **知り切る** can be used in the same way. 

To express knowing something in every detail, the usual compound is **知り尽くす（しりつくす）**, which has a different final element. Verbs with related meanings can therefore form different compounds, each with its own established uses and nuances.

## Common Verbs Ending in ～切る

Compounds ending in **～切る** can express carrying an activity through to completion, using up a resource, the full realization of a change, or the extreme degree of a condition. Below is a selection of common verbs illustrating these meanings, with a brief explanation of each.

- **食べ切る（たべきる）** — To eat all the food in question without leaving anything behind. Completeness concerns the quantity being discussed, such as a serving, a dish, or the food available.

- **飲み切る（のみきる）** — To drink a given amount of liquid completely, leaving none. It can refer, for example, to the contents of a glass or bottle.

- **使い切る（つかいきる）** — To use up everything available, until nothing remains. It is used with physical resources, money, time, and other things available in limited quantities.

- **売り切る（うりきる）** — To sell the entire quantity of goods in question until all the stock is gone. It presents the outcome from the perspective of the act of selling.

- **読み切る（よみきる）** — To read a text in its entirety, all the way to the end. Figuratively, it can also mean accurately anticipating how a situation will unfold or what moves an opponent will make.

- **書き切る（かききる）** — To finish writing a text or put everything one wishes to express into writing. Completeness may concern the writing task itself or the entirety of the content to be conveyed.

- **やり切る（やりきる）** — To carry a task or activity through to completion. It can emphasize having fully accomplished what one set out to do, even when considerable effort was required.

- **走り切る（はしりきる）** — To run the entire required distance, reaching the finish. In a race, it can emphasize successfully completing the course.

- **出し切る（だしきる）** — To bring out or use everything one has available. In the expression **力を出し切る**, it means giving everything one has, using all one's strength.

- **耐え切る（たえきる）** — To endure a hardship, pain, or pressure to the end without giving in. **耐え切れない**, conversely, expresses being unable to endure or bear any more.

- **守り切る（まもりきる）** — To protect or defend something to the very end, keeping it safe throughout the relevant period. In sports, it can mean managing to hold on to a lead until the match is over.

- **隠し切る（かくしきる）** — To conceal something completely, without letting it show or be discovered. It may refer to a fact, secret, intention, or emotion.

- **乗り切る（のりきる）** — In its figurative use, to get through a difficulty, crisis, or demanding period successfully. The compound conveys emerging from a difficult situation after facing it.

- **治り切る（なおりきる）** — To recover fully, until an injury or illness has completely healed. The compound highlights the fact that recovery is now complete.

- **疲れ切る（つかれきる）** — To be utterly exhausted or reach a state of extreme fatigue. It describes a condition of severe physical or mental exhaustion.

- **冷え切る（ひえきる）** — To become completely cold, losing all warmth. Figuratively, it can describe relationships that have lost all warmth and affection or enthusiasm that has completely disappeared.

- **澄み切る（すみきる）** — To be completely clear, pure, or crystal-clear. It is used, for instance, of the sky, air, or water, presented as entirely free of cloudiness.

- **信じ切る（しんじきる）** — To believe something wholeheartedly or trust someone completely. The compound emphasizes belief or trust without doubts or reservations.

- **諦め切る（あきらめきる）** — To give up completely, letting go even of lingering hopes or attachments. **諦め切れない** expresses being unable to give up fully or come to terms with something.

- **分かり切る（わかりきる）** — To be perfectly clear or fully understood. In the expression **分かり切ったこと**, it refers to something obvious, self-evident, or already known.

- **言い切る（いいきる）** — To say something in full, all the way to the end, or to state it categorically. In the latter sense, it emphasizes the firmness and confidence with which a statement is made.

## The Difference Between ～切る and ～終わる

Both **～切る** and **～終わる（おわる, owaru）** can refer to completing an action and, in certain contexts, describe the same outcome. The difference lies mainly in what they emphasize: 

- **～終わる presents an activity as finished**; 
- **～切る emphasizes that the action is carried through completely or that a condition reaches a fully developed or extreme state**.

This distinction may amount to only a subtle difference, but it can also significantly change the meaning of a compound. Moreover, uses of ～切る expressing intensity or the full realization of a state do not necessarily have a natural equivalent with ～終わる.

### When Both Forms Indicate a Completed Action

A useful first comparison is between **読み終わる**, “to finish reading,” and **読み切る**, “to read something in its entirety, all the way through.”

When referring to a novel, both forms can indicate that the reader reached the last page. With **読み終わった**, the speaker presents reading as an activity that has now ended; with **読み切った**, the emphasis falls more strongly on having read the entire novel, right through to the end.

Thus, ～終わる can also indicate that a task has been completed in full: when someone says they have finished reading a book, we normally understand that they have read the whole thing. The presence of ～切る, however, makes the completeness of the achievement particularly salient.

If the novel was especially long or demanding, **読み切った can also convey a sense of accomplishment at having completed a challenging undertaking**. The sense of effort comes from the context: ～切る can also be used for an easy task, provided the speaker wishes to emphasize that it was carried through to completion.

### When ～切る Implies Using Up a Resource

The distinction becomes clearer when we compare **使い終わる**, “to finish using something,” with **使い切る**, “to use something up completely.”

**使い終わる** indicates that using something has come to an end in relation to the activity concerned. If we have finished using some paints to make a picture, we can put them away even if some paint remains.

**使い切る**, by contrast, indicates that the entire quantity in question has been used. In the case of paints, this means that none remains; with money, it means having spent the whole available amount; with time, having used all the time available.

Here, finishing the activity and exhausting the resource are two distinct outcomes. We can finish using something while keeping some for another occasion, or we can use it all up. The choice of compound makes it possible to specify which of these outcomes we mean.

### When ～切る Describes a Fully Developed or Extreme Condition

The comparison with ～終わる becomes even more important when ～切る expresses the degree of a condition.

**疲れ切る** means “to be utterly exhausted”: it highlights extreme fatigue. **疲れ終わる** is not the usual equivalent for this meaning, because the focus is on the condition reached, not on the ending of a bounded activity.

Similarly, **冷え切る** describes something that has become completely cold, while **治り切る** emphasizes complete recovery. These meanings are expressed with compounds ending in ～切る; replacing the final element with ～終わる does not automatically produce a natural expression with the same meaning.

The resulting state may also persist: a person who is utterly exhausted remains tired, just as something that has become completely cold remains cold. The completeness expressed by ～切る concerns the degree to which the condition has developed.

### When the Meaning of the Compound Changes

Even with a verb that allows both forms, substituting one for the other can change what is communicated.

**言い終わる** means “to finish saying something” and presents the act of speaking as completed. **言い切る**, in the sense of “to state something categorically,” instead emphasizes the firmness with which a statement is made: the speaker asserts something without hesitation or reservations.

言い切る can, however, also mean “to say something in full, all the way to the end.” In that sense, it once again comes close to 言い終わる. To choose between the two compounds, we must therefore consider the meaning they take on in the sentence: finishing what one has to say and stating a position categorically are different matters, which the context helps us distinguish.

## ～切る: Contexts and Register

**～切る belongs to standard Japanese and is used in both speech and writing**, from everyday conversations to professional communication. The specific register depends on the compound, the surrounding vocabulary, and the communicative situation. Its sense of completeness can serve to describe an outcome in entirely practical terms or to make a statement more forceful and emphatic.

The **level of politeness** is conveyed through conjugation and the overall sentence construction. **使い切った** and **使い切りました**, for example, both mean “used it all up,” but the latter expresses the result politely. 

Even an intense expression such as **疲れ切っています**, “to be utterly exhausted,” can therefore be used in polite communication: the intensity of what is said and the degree of politeness are separate matters.

- In **everyday life**, ～切る compounds allow us to speak precisely about quantities consumed, activities completed, and personal conditions. 食べ切る and 使い切る are suited to situations involving meals, shopping, or managing resources; 読み切る can refer to studying or reading; 疲れ切る describes extreme fatigue.

	In such contexts, the auxiliary may simply make a factual statement more precise: saying that a product has been entirely used up, for instance, may be nothing more than a straightforward observation.

- In **work, study, and sports**, completeness may take on a different significance. Expressions such as **やり切った**, “I saw it through to the end,” **走り切った**, “I ran the whole course,” or **力を出し切った**, “I gave it everything I had,” may convey satisfaction, determination, or awareness of the effort involved. 

	These nuances emerge when the situation presents the outcome as an achievement or emphasizes the energy invested.

- In **professional, technical, or analytical writing**, ～切る can instead help specify how thorough an analysis is, how completely a phenomenon has been covered, or where the limits of the available information lie. **把握し切れていない**, “not yet having a complete picture,” indicates that some aspects have yet to be fully understood or identified. 

	The sense of totality thus helps define precisely what is known, what has been accomplished, and what remains to be clarified.

	**言い切る**, in the sense of “to state something categorically,” deserves particular attention. This compound can emphasize the firmness of a position, because it presents a statement as being made without hesitation or reservations. 

	The form **言い切れない**, “to be unable to say something with certainty,” is instead well suited to expressing caution when making an assessment. That caution comes from the expression's meaning: the speaker acknowledges that the available evidence does not support a categorical conclusion. With **言い切れません**, the same assessment is expressed politely.

- In **personal and descriptive contexts**, some compounds foreground the intensity of a condition. **疲れ切った表情**, “an utterly exhausted expression,” makes the degree of fatigue apparent; **澄み切った空**, “a perfectly clear sky,” emphasizes clarity. 

	These expressions can be used in both conversation and narrative, with an effect that depends on the imagery and tone of the passage.

Context can also give an entire compound a specific meaning. **やり切れない**, when referring to a task, can indicate being unable to complete it; when referring to a painful or exasperating situation, however, it can express the sense that something is unbearable or emotionally overwhelming. In this second use, the compound directly conveys the speaker's emotional experience.

## Usage Examples

*<p class="jp-example">長い小説だったが、結末が気になって、一晩で読み切った。</p>*
*"It was a long novel, but I wanted to find out how it ended, so I read the whole thing in one night."*

*<p class="jp-example">料理を作りすぎてしまい、二人では食べ切れなかったので、残りは翌日のお弁当に入れた。</p>*
*"I'd made too much food, and the two of us couldn't finish it all, so I packed the leftovers for lunch the next day."*

*<p class="jp-example">洗剤を使い切ってしまったので、帰りに買ってきてもらえますか。</p>*
*"I've used up all the detergent. Could you pick some up on your way home?"*

*<p class="jp-example">苦しかったが、沿道の声援に励まされて、最後まで走り切った。</p>*
*"It was tough, but the spectators cheering me on along the route kept me going, and I managed to run all the way to the finish."*

*<p class="jp-example">結果には満足していないが、持っている力を出し切れたので、悔いはない。</p>*
*"I'm not happy with the result, but I have no regrets because I managed to give it everything I had."*

*<p class="jp-example">けががまだ治り切っていないので、今週の試合には出ないことにした。</p>*
*"I haven't fully recovered from my injury yet, so I've decided not to play in this week's match."*

*"The heating hadn't been on, so by the time the meeting started, the room was freezing cold."*

*<p class="jp-example">新しい証拠が見つかった以上、彼が無関係だとは言い切れない。</p>*
*"Now that new evidence has come to light, we can't say for certain that he had nothing to do with it."*

*"Even though I know I might fail, I still can't bring myself to give up completely on the dream I've held onto for years."*

## Conclusion

The fundamental distinction that emerges from studying ～切る concerns **the difference between an activity simply coming to an end and being carried through completely**. This difference explains why some compounds closely resemble forms with ～終わる, while others describe fully developed or extreme conditions. 

Reading an entire text, using up a resource, and being utterly exhausted are different outcomes that Japanese can highlight using the same final element.

Grammatically, attachment to the **連用形** and the compound's godan conjugation provide a regular formation pattern. Choosing which verb to use, however, requires attention to meaning and actual usage: knowing the formation rule allows us to construct a form, but whether the combination sounds natural must be checked against expressions that speakers actually use.

A further point concerns what we can infer from a compound. **読み切った** presents the reading as fully completed; how well the text was understood is a separate question. 

Likewise, **信じ切っている** describes a belief held without reservations, while whether that belief is well founded must be assessed separately. ～切る enables us to specify one aspect of a situation without necessarily telling us everything about the outcome.

For learners, it is therefore helpful to **learn compounds together with the words they commonly occur with**. Noting down expressions such as **予算を使い切る**, “to use up the entire budget,” or **力を出し切る**, “to give it everything you've got,” helps identify exactly what is being presented as fully used or expended. 

Keeping expressions such as **疲れ切っている** and **諦め切れない** in your notes also helps you become familiar with the forms through which these meanings are actually expressed.

One practical exercise is to **take a sentence you come across while reading and compare it with the base verb** and, where possible, with the corresponding form in **～終わる**. Then try describing in your own language what information is added or brought into focus. 

The comparison is especially instructive when the translations seem identical: those are precisely the cases in which it is worth looking more closely at the context and the speaker's communicative intention.

Negative forms also deserve particular attention when studying this construction. **読み切れない** does not rule out reading part of a text: what it rules out is being able to read the whole thing. **諦め切れない** can describe an incomplete act of giving up, in which some hope or attachment still remains. 

Identifying what remains to be completed, consumed, or relinquished helps us understand the auxiliary's contribution more precisely.

To use ～切る with confidence, then, the most useful point of reference is **the precise outcome you want to communicate**. Combining this sensitivity to context with learning authentic verb combinations helps learners move beyond simply knowing the rule and choose expressions that sound natural.
