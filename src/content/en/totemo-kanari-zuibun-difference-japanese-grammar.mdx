What do とても (totemo), かなり (kanari), and ずいぶん (zuibun) mean, what is the difference between these three adverbs of degree, and how are they used to say “very” in Japanese?


As a first approximation, the differences can be summarized as follows:

- とても: expresses “very” in a neutral and objective way, without any particular implications. 
- かなり: indicates a high degree perceived as substantial or above a certain threshold, with a nuance of personal evaluation (“rather,” “fairly—but by no small margin”). 
- ずいぶん: expresses a high degree perceived as remarkable in relation to expectations, often with a pronounced subjective component (“decidedly,” “to an unusually high degree”).


However, the difference between these adverbs is not merely one of intensity. It is not simply a question of **how high the degree is**, but of **how the speaker perceives it**: whether they regard it as normal, above average, or surprising in relation to what they expected.


For this reason, throughout the article we will examine とても、かなり、ずいぶん not as equivalents of *“very,”* but as linguistic tools that express different ways of evaluating reality, depending on whether the degree is presented neutrally, measured against a perceived threshold, or contrasted with expectations.

Japanese has a number of adverbs of degree: words that express what English conveys with **“very,”** **“fairly,”** or **“rather.”**

Some are ubiquitous, or at least frequently covered in grammars and textbooks for learners.

Adverbs such as とても are widely taught and serve as the standard point of reference for expressing the idea of *“very”* in Japanese.

In reality, there are many others (such as *なかなか*、*大分*、*非常に*); some, including [ほとんど and 大半](/en/articles/taihan-daibubun-hotondo-difference-usage-japanese-grammar/), have already been examined in a previous article. They differ from one another in nuance and register.

In particular, adverbs such as ずいぶん and かなり, despite being extremely common in both spoken and written Japanese, are often treated only in passing or reduced to simple synonyms.

This article will focus specifically on these two, because textbooks tend to give them somewhat less attention.


We will therefore not present a comprehensive list of adverbs of degree, but an in-depth analysis of the meaning, usage, and nuances of ずいぶん, かなり, and とても. In particular, とても will serve as our point of comparison: it is the neutral, standard adverb for saying *“very,”* and as such it will help bring out what makes ずいぶん and かなり different and more complex. 

In the sections that follow, we will first examine each adverb individually, looking at its specific meaning, intensity, and tone; we will then compare them with one another—the summary table will be particularly helpful in this regard—before concluding the article.

## とても (totemo)・かなり (kanari)・ずいぶん (zuibun): Meaning and Usage

From a grammatical perspective, *とても*, *かなり*, and *ずいぶん* belong to the category of **程度副詞** (*teido fukushi*), or adverbs of degree. Their primary function is not to describe how an action takes place, but to specify the degree of a property or state or, when the predicate allows it, the extent of a process.

An adverb of degree presupposes the existence of an **implicit scale**. In expressions such as *高い* (“high,” “expensive”), *難しい* (“difficult”), or *静かだ* (“quiet”), the property described can be present to a greater or lesser degree. The adverb places that property at a particular point or within a particular range on the scale:

* とても難しい – “very difficult”
* かなり静かだ – “fairly quiet”
* ずいぶん高い – “really very expensive/high”

This scale does not necessarily correspond to a precise numerical value. The point of reference is inferred from the context and may be the expected average, a previous situation, a point of comparison, or the speaker’s expectations. 

For this reason, two people can describe the same reality with different adverbs without necessarily referring to two objectively different degrees.

The function of these adverbs also varies somewhat depending on the type of predicate. With adjectives and *な*-adjectives, they normally indicate the degree of a quality or state, as in *とても暑い* (“very hot”), *かなり複雑だ* (“fairly complex”), or *ずいぶん元気だ* (“remarkably well”).

They can also combine with certain verbs, provided that the event can be interpreted along a gradable dimension. 

In とても疲れた, for example, the resulting state of exhaustion is intensified; in かなり歩いた, the focus is on how far or how much someone walked; in ずいぶん変わった, it is the extent of the change that is emphasized.

The adverb therefore does not modify the verb in a generic sense, but rather the measurable dimension associated with the action, its duration, its result, or the transformation involved.

The three terms do not behave in the same way before nouns either. *かなり* regularly forms constructions such as *かなりの時間* (“quite a long time”) or *かなりの人数* (“a considerable number of people”). *ずいぶんな言い方* and *ずいぶんな話* are also possible expressions, but they readily take on an evaluative meaning, often conveying surprise, criticism, or disapproval. とても, by contrast, cannot directly modify a noun in a comparable construction.

*とても*, *かなり*, and *ずいぶん* therefore share the general function of placing something on a scale of degree, but they are not simply three interchangeable levels of the same intensity. The choice between them also depends on the type of predicate, the parameter being measured, the point of reference supplied by the context, and the attitude with which the speaker presents their evaluation.

## とても (totemo) - Very

### Meaning and usage
*とても* is probably the most direct equivalent of **“very, really”** in standard Japanese. 

It simply indicates a **high degree** of a quality or condition, **without necessarily implying surprise** (an important point to bear in mind when comparing it with ずいぶん). For example: *「この本はとても面白いです。」* – *“This book is very interesting.”* Here, とても simply intensifies the adjective 面白い (“interesting”). 

It is a neutral and widely used term. It can be used with adjectives and, in negative sentences, with verbs in the construction *「とても～ない」* meaning *“cannot possibly” or “not at all”*: for example, *「とても信じられない」* – *“I simply can’t believe it.”* 

### Intensity

*とても* expresses **a very high degree of intensity**, comparable to *“very”* or *“extremely.”* It is often treated as equivalent to *非常に*—*hijō ni, extremely* (which we will return to in a future article)—placing it among the stronger intensifiers in Japanese. 

Indeed, phrases such as *「とても強い」* and *「非常に強い」* are nearly equivalent and indicate the highest degree, or something close to it. Some native speakers even perceive *とても* as stronger than *かなり* (which we will discuss shortly), regarding it as a higher-degree intensifier. 

For example, in a comparison: *「白鵬はとても強い。琴欧洲はかなり強いが、とても強いというほどではない。」* – *“Hakuhō, the sumo champion, is very strong. Kotoōshū is quite strong, but not so strong that you would call him ‘very strong.’”*

In this example, *とても強い* denotes **the highest level** (the unbeatable champion), whereas *かなり強い* **suggests a high level, but not the very top**. This illustrates how とても can imply **an extreme degree** in certain comparative contexts.




### Tone and register

*とても* is a **polite, standard** term suited to neutral or formal contexts. It can be used in both plain and polite speech *(for example, 「とても寒いです」 is perfectly natural)*.

 Interestingly, Japanese speakers **tend to use とても less often with close friends or family members** in highly informal situations.

This is not because とても is inherently formal, but because it sounds slightly *polite* or *neutral*; in highly colloquial contexts, it is often replaced by more informal expressions such as *「すごく」* *(sugoku)* or *「めっちゃ」*. 

For example, a young person speaking with friends is more likely to say *「このゲームめっちゃ面白い！」* than *「とても面白い」*. Nevertheless, *とても* remains perfectly understandable and commonly used; it simply gives the sentence a slightly more polished tone. 

In speech (with strangers, superiors, and so on), *とても* is entirely appropriate and sounds **polite without being excessively stiff**. 

In formal writing, as already noted, it is often replaced by *非常に* (which is more formal), although *とても* itself can also appear in writing that is not overly technical.




### Typical contexts of use

*とても* can be used to **intensify virtually any description**. It is ubiquitous: *「とても忙しい」 (very busy), 「とても静かだ」 (very quiet), 「とても残念です」 (it is such a shame)*. 

The situation does not have to be extraordinary: the term simply describes a high degree.

 It carries no implicit comparison or sense of surprise. 

For example, if it is hot in summer, one can say *「今日はとても暑い」* – *“It is very hot today,”* as a general observation. It is the **simplest and most direct** equivalent of *“very”*—essentially the Japanese counterpart of English **very**.



### Examples

*<p class="jp-example">この本はとても面白くて、一晩で全部読んでしまった。</p>*
 *“This book was so interesting that I read the whole thing in one night.”*

*<p class="jp-example">彼の説明はとても分かりやすかったので、すぐに理解できた。</p>*
*"His explanation was very clear, so I understood immediately."*

*<p class="jp-example">今日はとても暑いから、外に出たくない。</p>*
*"It is very hot today, so I do not want to go outside."*

*<p class="jp-example">その映画はとても感動的で、思わず涙が出た。</p>*
 *"That film was so moving that I could not help tearing up."*

*<p class="jp-example">彼女はとても優しい人で、誰にでも親切に接する。</p>*
*“She is a very kind person and is considerate toward everyone.”*

## ずいぶん (zuibun) – Remarkably, More Than Expected

### Meaning and usage

The term *ずいぶん (normally written in kana, but also found in kanji as 「随分」)* is an adverb meaning *“very”* or *“remarkably.”* Unlike a straightforward *“very,”* however, it often conveys the speaker’s surprise: it is used when something reaches a higher degree than expected.


*ずいぶん* often indicates that **a quantity or degree of intensity exceeds the speaker’s expectations**. For example: *「ずいぶん大きくなったね！」* – *“You’ve grown so much—more than I expected!”*



In this situation, the use of *ずいぶん* implies surprise at how much someone—a child, for instance—has grown since the last time the speaker saw them. 

This **sense of wonder or surprise** is characteristic of *ずいぶん*.

In similar situations, it may also be rendered as **“so”** or **“remarkably.”** 

For example: *「日本のカメラってずいぶん高いんですね。」* – *“Wow, Japanese cameras are really expensive!”* 


Here, *ずいぶん* conveys surprise at a price regarded as unusually high. Indeed, *ずいぶん* is often accompanied by interjections such as *「えー！」 (Ehh!), 「うわ！」 (Wow!)*, specifically to emphasize the speaker’s **surprise**.


### Intensity

In addition to meaning *“very (more than expected),”* ずいぶん can also mean **“a great deal”** in terms of quantity **when used with a verb**. 

In this case, the element of surprise may be less pronounced, but the connotation of a **substantial quantity** remains. For example: *「ずいぶん食べたね！」* – *“You ate a lot!”* or *「ずいぶん待たされた。」* – *“They kept me waiting for ages.”* 

These sentences emphasize the **large quantity** involved—the amount of food eaten or the length of the wait—often with a tone of surprise or complaint at the sheer amount involved.

It is also worth noting that *ずいぶん* can appear before a な-adjective almost as though it were an adjective in its own right (grammatically functioning as a [な-adjective](/en/articles/origin-na-adjectives-japanese-history/)). 

In these cases, it often takes on an **ironic or disapproving** nuance. For example: *「ずいぶん失礼な人だ。」* – *“What an incredibly rude person!”* or *「ずいぶん勝手なことを言うね。」* – *“You’re saying some pretty selfish things, aren’t you?”* 

Here, *ずいぶん* strengthens a negative judgment (much like *“terribly”*), often with a sarcastic or indignant tone. 

This is a use worth keeping in mind because it differs from a straightforward *“very”*: *「ずいぶん失礼な！」* corresponds to *“What an outrageously rude way to behave!”* with surprise or indignation, and is stronger than the neutral *「とても失礼だ」*.


### Tone and register

*ずいぶん* is widely used in **spoken language**, especially to add emphasis while expressing surprise. 

In everyday speech, it often appears in exclamations delivered with an astonished tone: *「ずいぶん暑くなってきたねえ！」* – *“Wow, it has become really hot—much hotter than expected!”*

In informal conversations, including those among family and friends, it is perfectly natural to use ずいぶん to emphasize a surprising impression.

In **written language**, the use of *ずいぶん* depends on the register. In **informal** or narrative contexts—for example, in a novel, a personal blog, or dialogue in a story—*ずいぶん* appears just as it does in speech, and may be written in kanji as *「随分」*. 

It serves to convey the narrator’s surprise or subjective involvement to the reader. For example, an author might write: *「随分昔のことだが…」* – *“It happened a very long time ago, but…”* or *「彼は随分驚いた様子だった。」* – *“He looked extremely surprised.”* 

In these cases, ずいぶん vividly emphasizes the magnitude involved—the length of time that has passed or the degree of surprise—in a written register that is **not overly formal**.

By contrast, **in formal, academic, or journalistic texts**, *ずいぶん* is less common. 

This is because *ずいぶん* has a colloquial and personal nuance; formal writing tends to favor more *“neutral”* or objective synonyms such as *「かなり」* or expressions such as *「非常に」* (very, extremely). 

For example, a news report would probably say *「その事故で負傷者がかなり出た。」* – *“Quite a few people were injured in the accident”* rather than using *ずいぶん*, or *「気温が非常に上昇した。」* – *“The temperature rose dramatically,”* to maintain a more technical register. 

*ずいぶん* would reflect the speaker’s subjective reaction more strongly—*“I was struck by the number of injured people”* or *“What exceptionally hot weather!”*—whereas *かなり/非常に* are more impersonal choices and better suited to formal writing. 

In summary, *ずいぶん* is perceived as **more characteristic of spoken language or colloquial writing**, and is generally avoided in formal documents so as not to sound overly informal or personal.

This does not mean that ずいぶん is slang in Japanese or that it is impolite: on the contrary, it can also be used in polite spoken contexts *(「ずいぶんお待たせしてしまいました」 – “I’m terribly sorry to have kept you waiting so long,” perhaps said to a customer)*. 

Other terms are simply preferred in **more formal written registers**.

We can therefore say that *ずいぶん* is appropriate in everyday speech, including polite speech, and in writing only when a conversational tone or a degree of subjectivity is desired.

An interesting difference in register is that *ずいぶん* is less common among younger speakers in informal situations than slang equivalents such as *「めっちゃ」* or *「すごく」*, which also mean *“very.”* 

For example, among friends, a twenty-year-old today is more likely to say *「めっちゃ暑い！」* than *「ずいぶん暑い！」* 

*ずいぶん* is not old-fashioned, however: it is still used, but sounds slightly more measured or mature than slang such as *めっちゃ*. 

In formal spoken contexts—meetings, polite conversation, and so on—it is entirely acceptable. The difference between 「今日はずいぶん冷えますね」 and 「今日はとても冷えますね」 (both meaning *“It is really cold today, isn’t it?”*) is minimal: ずいぶん adds a slight note of surprise—*“unusually cold”*—whereas *とても* is a neutral “very cold.”

### ずいぶん and ずいぶんと

Alongside the simple form *ずいぶん*, the form *ずいぶんと* is also very common. The two have the same basic meaning and, in most contexts, can be used interchangeably:

* *今日はずいぶん寒いね。*
* *今日はずいぶんと寒いね。*

Both sentences mean *“It is really very cold today”* and suggest that the cold is remarkable or greater than the speaker expected. The presence of *と* therefore does not introduce a new meaning, nor does it automatically indicate a higher degree of intensity.

Grammatically, *ずいぶん* is already a complete adverb and does not require any particle in order to modify an adjective, a verb, or another gradable expression. In *ずいぶんと*, *と* is an adverbial particle that makes the connection between the adverb and what follows more explicit. 

In essence, this is the same *と* that can appear after other adverbs or expressions of manner, as in *ゆっくりと歩く* (“to walk slowly”) or *しっかりと確認する* (“to check carefully”).

In the case of *ずいぶん*, however, this grammatical function is largely redundant because the word already functions as an adverb on its own. The difference between the two forms therefore lies mainly in rhythm and in the way the speaker presents their evaluation:

* *ずいぶん変わったね。* – “You’ve changed quite a lot.”
* *ずいぶんと変わったね。* – “You’ve really changed quite a lot.”

In the second sentence, と can give the expression a slightly more deliberate and emphatic cadence, bringing the speaker’s observation into greater relief. This added prominence should not, however, be interpreted as a regular semantic difference: *ずいぶんと* does not necessarily mean *“more”* than *ずいぶん*. Any impression of greater emphasis depends on pronunciation, sentence rhythm, and context.

The form with *と* is particularly natural when the speaker wishes to dwell on the remarkable nature of a situation, quantity, or change:

*<p class="jp-example">この町もずいぶんと変わりましたね。</p>*
*“This town has really changed a lot too, hasn’t it?”*

*<p class="jp-example">完成までには、まだずいぶんと時間がかかりそうだ。</p>*
*“It looks as though it will still take quite a long time to finish.”*

*<p class="jp-example">昨日はずいぶんと遅くまで起きていたんだね。</p>*
*“You stayed up really late last night, didn’t you?”*

In all of these examples, *と* could be removed without making the sentence incorrect or substantially changing its content:

* *この町もずいぶん変わりましたね。*
* *まだずいぶん時間がかかりそうだ。*
* *ずいぶん遅くまで起きていたんだね。*

The choice therefore depends mainly on the desired cadence. The form without *と* is shorter and more immediate; the form with *と* can sound fuller, more deliberate, or more expressive, especially when *ずいぶん* conveys an evaluation to which the speaker wishes to draw attention. There is, however, no clear-cut distinction in register: both forms can occur in speech and in writing.

Finally, it is important not to confuse *ずいぶんと* with the construction *ずいぶんな*. In *ずいぶんと失礼だ*, the entire expression functions adverbially and means *“really very rude.”* In *ずいぶんな人だ*, by contrast, *ずいぶん* behaves like a *な*-adjective and readily takes on a critical or ironic meaning, such as *“what an extraordinary person,”* *“what an absurd person,”* or *“what kind of person is that?”*

For learners, the practical rule is therefore simple: *ずいぶん* can safely be used on its own as the complete, unmarked form. When encountering or using *ずいぶんと*, learners should not look for a precise difference in degree, but should instead notice the different cadence and the possible additional emphasis on the speaker’s evaluation.




### Examples

*<p class="jp-example">ずいぶん日本語が上手になったね。最初とは全然違うよ。</p>*
 *"Your Japanese has improved tremendously; it is completely different from when you started."*

*<p class="jp-example">今日はずいぶん寒いね。昨日と比べて急に気温が下がった。</p>*
 *“It is remarkably cold today; the temperature has dropped sharply since yesterday.”*

*<p class="jp-example">彼はずいぶん遅れて来たのに、まったく謝らなかった。</p>*
*"He arrived extremely late and did not apologize at all."*

*<p class="jp-example">ずいぶん考えたけど、やっぱり答えは出なかった。</p>*
*"I thought about it for a long time, but I still could not come up with an answer."*

*<p class="jp-example">この町もずいぶん変わったね。昔の面影がほとんどない。</p>*
*"This town has changed a great deal; almost nothing remains of what it used to look like."*

*<p class="jp-example">しばらく会わない間に、ずいぶんと日本語が上手になったね。</p>*
*“Your Japanese has improved tremendously since we last saw each other.”*

*<p class="jp-example">この辺りは夜になると、ずいぶんと静かになります。</p>*
*“This area becomes remarkably quiet at night.”*

## かなり (kanari) – Considerably Above the Norm

### Meaning and usage

*かなり* also means *“very, fairly, considerably.”* It indicates a high degree and is often translated as **“rather”** or **“considerably.”** The distinctive feature of *かなり* is that it suggests that the degree **exceeds the norm or average expectations without reaching an extreme**. 

Dictionaries typically define it as *“not extreme, but above average; more than expected.”*

For example: *「この問題はかなり難しいです」* – *“This problem is fairly difficult—more difficult than one might expect.”* The implication is that it is more difficult than usual, though perhaps not impossible.

As noted above, *かなり* is used when the speaker wishes to emphasize that something is considerably above the norm while retaining a factual tone. It also appears with quantities and measurements:「かなりの数の人が反対した」 – *“A considerable number of people opposed it”*; 「かなり長い間待ちました」 – *“I waited for quite a long time.”*

It is also used with adjectives: 「この服、高かったの？」「うん、かなり高かったよ」 – *“Was this outfit expensive? — Yes, it was pretty expensive.”* 
Here, かなり conveys something like *“yes, more expensive than I might have liked—quite pricey.”* If the phrase were *ずいぶん高かった*, the implication would instead be *“I was surprised by how expensive it was!”*



### Intensity

*かなり* expresses a **high, but not maximum, degree of intensity**. We might say that it covers a range from *“rather”* to *“very.”* In terms of a rough *“ranking,”* some native speakers place *かなり* **slightly below** *とても/非常に* in strength. 

For example, as seen above, *かなり強い* (*quite strong*) was used for a wrestler who was strong but not the strongest. Nevertheless, *かなり* carries the idea that something is **noteworthy** in relation to a standard. 

In other words, whereas *とても* simply means *“very,”* *かなり* carries the sense of **far exceeding normal values** or what would ordinarily be expected. On a typically hot summer day, for example, a Japanese speaker might say *「今日はとても暑い」* – *(“It is very hot today”)*, because hot weather is normal in summer; they would not use *かなり暑い* unless the heat **clearly exceeded** the usual level for summer.

If, however, it is so hot that the weather is exceptional—with people even collapsing from heatstroke—one might say *「今日はかなり暑いね」* – *“It is really quite hot today—hotter than usual.”* Thus, *かなり* comes into play when the speaker wishes to indicate *a degree outside the ordinary*, without claiming that it is the absolute maximum.


### Tone and register

*かなり* is perceived as slightly **more formal or objective** than *とても*. It is extremely common in both spoken and written Japanese, including everyday conversation. In particular, かなり frequently appears in informative, expository, and journalistic contexts because it expresses a noteworthy degree in a relatively **neutral** manner. 

For example, a news report might say *「今年のインフルエンザはかなり深刻です」* – *“This year’s flu outbreak is quite serious.”* Here, *かなり* emphasizes that the severity is above the norm. In conversation among friends, *かなり* can also be used, although younger speakers tend to use *すごく* or similar alternatives for *“very”*; *かなり* gives the statement a slightly more measured or serious tone.

 It is by no means archaic or excessively formal: it can also be heard in casual conversation, for example *「あの映画、かなり面白かったよ」* – *“That film was really/quite interesting.”* In a lighter context, someone might instead say 「結構面白かった」 (which is somewhat milder) or 「すごく面白かった」 (which is more emphatic and colloquial). 

*かなり* therefore belongs to the **standard** register: it is neither markedly colloquial like *めっちゃ* nor stiff like *非常に*. It can appear in polite conversation, meetings, and similar situations without sounding out of place.

*<p class="jp-example">この問題はかなり難しいから、時間がかかると思う。</p>*
 *"This problem is quite difficult, so I think it will take some time."*

*<p class="jp-example">彼はかなり疲れているみたいで、話しかけても反応が薄い。</p>*
*"He seems quite tired; even when you speak to him, he barely responds."*

*<p class="jp-example">その店はかなり人気があって、いつも混んでいる。</p>*
*"That shop is quite popular and is always crowded."*

*<p class="jp-example">日本語はかなり話せるようになったが、まだ自然とは言えない。</p>*
 *"I can speak Japanese fairly well now, but I still would not say I sound natural."*

*<p class="jp-example">ここから駅まではかなり遠いので、タクシーで行ったほうがいい。</p>*
*“The station is quite a long way from here, so it would be better to take a taxi.”*

## Comparing かなり and ずいぶん

 Both *かなり* and *ずいぶん* share the idea of *“more than normal.”* The main difference is that **かなり is more objective and evaluative**, whereas **ずいぶん is more subjective and expresses greater surprise**. We can say that *かなり* often communicates a factual assessment—“considerably”—perhaps implying that an outside observer would agree. *ずいぶん*, by contrast, emphasizes the speaker’s personal impression: *“Wow, that really is a lot!”* Consider the following sentences:

- 「東京はかなり物価が高い。」 – “The cost of living in Tokyo is quite high.” 
	
	Here, かなり suggests a recognized fact: Tokyo is notoriously expensive, well above average, though not necessarily the most expensive place imaginable. The sentence is informative and sounds like an observation that others could readily share.

- 「東京はずいぶん物価が高いね。」 – “Wow, the cost of living in Tokyo really is high!”

	In this case, ずいぶん implies that the speaker is struck by the prices—perhaps they have just arrived in Tokyo and find them higher than expected. The basic content is similar—the cost of living is high—but the tone conveys more personal surprise.



It is also worth noting that *かなり* can modify nouns through の/な constructions (*「かなりの額」 – “a considerable sum”*), whereas *ずいぶん* is not used with *の*. 

When used adjectivally (*ずいぶんな〜*), as noted above, it often takes on a negative (*“awful,” “outrageous”*) or ironic connotation, and therefore cannot simply replace かなり as a neutral intensifier before a noun. In general, かなり is more versatile in formal and descriptive contexts, whereas ずいぶん remains more colloquial and exclamatory.


---


## Comparing ずいぶん and とても

Both *とても* and *ずいぶん* mean **“very,”** but *ずいぶん* adds the nuance **“more than I expected.”** 

*「この町はとても静かです」* means *“This town is very quiet”* in a neutral sense; by contrast, *「この町はずいぶん静かですね」* means something like *“This town is really—unusually—quiet, isn’t it?”* perhaps implying that the speaker expected more noise and is therefore surprised. 

In general, *とても* is more neutral and descriptive, whereas *ずいぶん* is more subjective and expresses greater surprise. Moreover, *とても* is not used directly with affirmative verbs: saying *「とても食べた」* to mean *“I ate a lot”* sounds unnatural, whereas *ずいぶん* can be used this way: *「ずいぶん食べた」* – *“I ate quite a lot.”* 

With actions or quantities, it is natural to use other adverbs, such as *たくさん* for *“a lot.”* In short, we use *とても* to say “very” in a general sense and *ずいぶん* when we want to add the idea *“very—and I am surprised by just how much!”*



---


## ずいぶん vs とても vs かなり: A comparison

The following comparative examples show how each of the three adverbs relates to the other two in terms of intensity, tone, and typical contexts of use. All three broadly mean *“very,”* but they differ subtly in semantic nuance and in the contexts in which they are used. It is important to emphasize that these differences are not mathematical: they are often slight and subjective, and native speakers choose among the adverbs according to the context or the emphasis they wish to convey.


* とても難しかった – “It was very difficult” (a neutral statement of a high degree).

* かなり難しかった – “It was fairly/rather difficult” (more difficult than normal, though not necessarily to the highest possible degree; an objective evaluation).

* ずいぶん難しかった – “It was really very difficult” (surprisingly difficult, more so than expected; a subjective comment expressing surprise).





Another example from everyday speech:


- 「昨日の試験はとても難しかったです。」 – Yesterday’s exam was very difficult. (Neutral: it emphasizes a high level of difficulty in general terms.)

- 「昨日の試験はかなり難しかったです。」 – Yesterday’s exam was rather difficult. (It implies that the exam was more difficult than expected or than the average exam; an objective assessment.)

- 「昨日の試験はずいぶん難しかったです。」 – Yesterday’s exam was really difficult. (It suggests “it struck me as unusually difficult,” conveying the speaker’s surprise.)

In all three sentences, English could use **“very difficult,”** but in Japanese the choice of adverb changes the register and nuance: とても is neutral, かなり adds the idea of “more than normal,” and ずいぶん adds surprise.

## Summary of the differences between とても, かなり, and ずいぶん

In conclusion, *とても*, *かなり*, and *ずいぶん* are all adverbs of degree that express *“very,”* but with subtle differences:

- とても (very) 

  – **Very high intensity**; it can also indicate extremes (非常に). 

  – **Tone: neutral and standard, with a slightly polite feel**; somewhat more formal than slang, but still commonly used.

  – **Usage: broadly used to express a high degree**, without any implication of surprise. In highly colloquial speech, it is often replaced by forms such as すごく and めっちゃ.

- かなり (fairly, considerably) 

  – **Intensity: high and above the norm**, though usually not the absolute maximum. 

  – **Tone: slightly more serious/objective**, also suitable for formal and written contexts. 
         
  – **Usage: indicates a considerable degree in a factual manner**; it is common in news reports, explanations, and speech when measured emphasis is desired. It may imply that the fact would surprise others as well, not just the speaker, suggesting an assessment that others could reasonably share.


- ずいぶん (very, a great deal) 

  – **Intensity: high**, comparable to かなり in quantitative terms, but used especially when the degree exceeds the speaker’s personal expectations. 

     – **Tone: colloquial and exclamatory**, with emotion—surprise, admiration, and sometimes indignation.  

     – **Usage: typical of speech and informal writing** when emphasizing the speaker’s surprise: “really a lot”—with an implicit “wow.” It is often used in comparisons involving change over time: 「ずいぶん良くなった」 – “It has improved enormously since then.” It is uncommon in formal writing, where it would sound too subjective.

## Conclusions

*とても*, *かなり*, and *ずいぶん* can all be translated, depending on the context, as *“very,”* *“quite,”* *“rather,”* or *“decidedly.”* This overlap in translation, however, should not lead us to think that they are interchangeable. As we now know, the fundamental difference concerns not only the intensity expressed, but also **the point of view from which the speaker evaluates that intensity**.

*とても* simply indicates that a quality or state is present to a high degree. It is the most direct choice when the speaker wishes to intensify an adjective or condition without necessarily adding a comparison with the norm, the past, or personal expectations. 

In *とても難しい*, for example, the speaker communicates that something is very difficult, but does not specify whether it is more difficult than expected or whether that difficulty surprised them. For this reason, *とても* is generally the safest starting point for learners: it is standard, widely understood, and appropriate for most neutral descriptions.

*かなり*, by contrast, becomes particularly useful when the degree is perceived as **considerable in relation to an implicit parameter**. That parameter may be the average, the usual situation, a prior expectation, or a threshold regarded as significant.

Saying *かなり難しい* does not necessarily mean that something is less difficult than what would be described as *とても難しい*: rather, it presents that difficulty as significant rather than ordinary or negligible. Its relatively measured nuance also makes it suitable for explanations, evaluations, quantitative descriptions, and contexts in which the speaker wishes to make a relatively detached assessment.

Finally, *ずいぶん* brings **the speaker’s reaction** more clearly to the foreground. It is often used when a quality, quantity, or change appears remarkable in relation to what was expected. 

In *ずいぶん難しい*, the speaker does not merely register a high level of difficulty, but conveys something like *“it is much more difficult than I imagined.”* The reaction expressed may be surprise, admiration, disappointment, irritation, or simply the recognition of a clear difference. 

For this reason, *ずいぶん* is also natural when commenting on changes over time, as in *ずいぶん上手になった* (“you have become so much better”) or *ずいぶん変わった* (“it has changed considerably”).

When choosing among the three, it is therefore more useful to ask **what one wishes to communicate** than to try to establish which adverb is always the strongest. 

If the aim is simply to say that a quality is present to a high degree, *とても* is normally sufficient. If the speaker wishes to emphasize that the degree reached is substantial, above the norm, or relevant to the discussion, *かなり* is often the most appropriate choice. If, by contrast, the speaker wants to convey that the degree has made an impression on them or differs markedly from what they expected, *ずいぶん* makes this subjective component explicit.

The communicative situation can also guide the choice. In a general description or polite conversation, *とても* functions as the standard intensifier. 

In a report, an explanation, or a relatively analytical observation, *かなり* makes it possible to indicate a considerable degree without foregrounding personal emotion.

When reacting in conversation to an unexpected price, a major change, a long wait, or unusual behavior, *ずいぶん* often sounds more expressive and natural. This does not mean that it is necessarily informal or impolite: it can also appear in polite sentences, provided that expressing one’s impression is appropriate.

For learners, **it is advisable not to start from the English translation**, because words such as *“very,”* *“fairly,”* and *“rather”* can vary considerably in force depending on the sentence. It is more effective to examine the Japanese context and identify the implicit parameter: is the speaker simply intensifying a quality? 

Are they comparing it with a threshold or with the norm? Or are they reacting to something they consider surprising? The answers to these questions lead respectively to とても, かなり, and ずいぶん.


In the early stages of learning, using *とても* as the neutral choice avoids many unnatural formulations. With greater exposure to the language, learners can begin to replace it deliberately with *かなり* when it is important to suggest that a reference level has been exceeded, and with *ずいぶん* when they wish to convey the gap between reality and expectation. 

The three adverbs should therefore not be learned as synonyms with different English equivalents, but as **three ways of presenting and interpreting a high degree**.

The final choice depends on the communicative intention: *とても* primarily describes intensity, *かなり* highlights its significance in relation to a parameter, and *ずいぶん* reveals the impression it makes on the speaker. Recognizing these different perspectives makes it possible not only to understand Japanese sentences more accurately, but also to express oneself more precisely, naturally, and with greater sensitivity to context.
