What do 性格 (seikaku), 人格 (jinkaku), 人柄 (hitogara), and 個性 (kosei) mean, and what is the difference between these Japanese words for “character” and “personality”?


All four words can be associated with the idea of *“personality,”* but they are by no means equivalent. 



- 性格 (seikaku) focuses primarily on stable tendencies in feeling, motivation, and behavior; 

- 人格 (jinkaku) refers to the person as a whole, often in a moral, educational, psychological, or legal sense; 

- 人柄 (hitogara) concerns the “kind of person” someone appears to be, in other words, the human qualities that come through in their relationships with others; 

- 個性 (kosei), by contrast, identifies the distinctive qualities of a person or thing: what sets them apart from others.

Japanese lexicographical and psychological sources also show that these terms overlap only partially and do not correspond perfectly to the Western concepts of *“character,” “personality,” and “individuality”.* 

Another important clue lies in the form of the words themselves. 性格, 人格, and 個性 are Sino-Japanese compounds with on’yomi readings; 人柄, by contrast, has the native reading ひとがら and is attested as early as classical Japanese, which helps explain why it generally sounds softer, more concrete, and less technical than the other three terms today. 

人格 and 個性, on the other hand, emerged as modern conceptual terms and are firmly established in educational, psychological, and institutional vocabulary.

In the following sections, we will examine each of these four words, which may appear similar but differ in meaning and usage and are often not interchangeable.

We will begin with a brief analysis of the kanji used to write them in order to understand their meanings in greater depth, before moving on to their nuances, contexts of use, and register. 

Each section includes usage examples specific to the word under discussion.


We will then compare the four terms directly to bring their differences in nuance and usage into even sharper focus.

## Meaning and function of 性格, 人格, 人柄, and 個性

To understand the difference between 性格, 人格, 人柄, and 個性 properly, it is not enough to assign each word a fixed English translation. What matters is understanding **the perspective from which the person is being viewed** whenever one of these terms is used.

The four words do not indicate different degrees of the same quality, as though 人格 were simply a deeper or more formal version of 性格. Nor do they form a scale running from outward character to inner personality. 

Instead, each one **selects a different dimension of the individual** and performs a specific function in the way the speaker describes, evaluates, or distinguishes that person from others.

**性格 (seikaku)** primarily has a **descriptive and dispositional function**. It identifies the relatively stable tendencies that emerge in a person’s habitual way of feeling, thinking, reacting, and behaving. The implicit question is therefore: *“What is this person generally like, and how do they tend to behave?”*

When someone is said to have a cheerful, shy, stubborn, or impulsive 性格, this does not necessarily amount to a judgment about their dignity or worth as a human being. It describes recurring patterns of behavior and inner experience.

Precisely because its function is mainly descriptive, 性格 can also be applied to problems, organizations, phenomena, and situations, where it indicates their nature or underlying characteristics.

**人格 (jinkaku)**, by contrast, performs a **holistic and, in many contexts, normative function**. It does not isolate a single character trait, but considers the person as a whole: as a psychological unity, an autonomous subject, a being endowed with dignity, or an individual capable of moral and legal responsibility.

For this reason, 人格 readily appears in discussions of respect, education, development, dignity, rights, and human worth. Expressions such as 人格を尊重する, *“to respect the person and their dignity,”* and 人格形成, *“the formation of the person,”* do not merely describe someone’s character. They frame the individual as a complete human subject.

The difference between 性格 and 人格 is therefore not merely one of register. The two terms may come close in psychology, but in ordinary language 性格 focuses on particular tendencies, whereas 人格 broadens the perspective to the person considered as an organized whole or as possessing inherent worth.

**人柄 (hitogara)** has a **relational and evaluative function**. The focus is not primarily on internal psychological structure, but on the kind of person revealed through their behavior, treatment of others, words, gestures, and attitudes.

The implicit question therefore becomes: *“What human qualities does this person convey?”* or *“What impression do they make through the way they conduct themselves?”*

人柄 can concern kindness, sincerity, trustworthiness, warmth, composure, or nobility of character. For this reason, it often carries a positive and complimentary tone, although this does not mean that every use must necessarily express approval. 

Its defining feature is rather that the person is perceived **through what they reveal of themselves in their relationships with others**.

**個性 (kosei)**, finally, performs a **distinctive and contrastive function**. It does not necessarily describe the whole personality or determine an individual’s moral worth: it highlights what sets that person apart from others.

The implicit question is: *“What makes this person, work, or object recognizable and different from all the others?”*

A feature may be entirely ordinary when considered part of someone’s 性格, yet become 個性 when treated as an identifying element. Being reserved, energetic, ironic, or exceptionally meticulous may constitute a trait of someone’s 性格; the same trait is perceived as 個性 when it helps make that person immediately recognizable.

個性 is also not limited to human beings. It can characterize a work of art, a style, a product, a building, a project, or even a particular sporting technique. Its basic meaning is neutral: it indicates a distinctive property. Its frequent positive nuance arises primarily from educational, artistic, and creative contexts, where originality and individual difference are generally valued.

The four words can therefore refer to the same person without contradiction, because they do not necessarily describe the same dimension:

* 性格 focuses on **what the person is generally like and how they tend to react in everyday life**;

* 人格 considers **the person as a whole in human, psychological, moral, or legal terms**;

* 人柄 highlights **the human qualities that emerge in relationships with others**;

* 個性 identifies **what makes that person distinctive and recognizable**.

A person might therefore have a difficult or very forceful 性格, yet still convey a sincere and trustworthy 人柄, possess a particularly strong 個性, and be respected for their 人格. There would be no inconsistency: each word would highlight a different dimension.

The translation must therefore adapt to the function the term serves in each context. 性格 may correspond to *“character,” “disposition,”* or *“nature”*; 人格 to *“personality,” “person,” “dignity,”* or *“moral qualities”*; 人柄 to *“humanity,” “disposition,” “human qualities,”* or *“personal nature”*; and 個性 to *“individuality,” “originality,”* or *“distinctive trait.”*

Their meaning therefore depends not only on what they describe, but above all **on the way the speaker chooses to frame the person**: as a set of tendencies, as a complete human subject, as a presence perceived by others, or as an individual distinct from everyone else.

## 性格 (seikaku)
### Kanji analysis
In 性格, the first kanji, 性, indicates *“nature,”* what is innate, a person’s basic disposition, or the innate workings of the heart or mind.   
The same kanji also appears in 個性 and is used in modern Japanese as a derivational element in the [suffix ～性](/en/articles/japanese-sei-suffix-meaning-usage/), which forms nouns denoting properties, qualities, and tendencies. 

In 性格, however, 性 should not be interpreted merely as a productive suffix: it forms part of an established lexical compound and retains its semantic core of “nature” or “disposition.”
 



The second kanji, 格, carries meanings such as *“standard,” “form,” “quality,” “structure,” “status,”* and *“level.”*   



Taken together, the compound suggests a person’s underlying disposition as expressed in stable patterns of thought, attitude, and behavior. It is not a fleeting emotion, but a relatively consistent tendency. 

### Meaning 

Dictionaries define 性格 first and foremost as **the individual disposition** reflected in the way a person feels, thinks, forms intentions, and acts. 



It is therefore the closest of the four terms to everyday English *“character”* or *“disposition.”* The word is not limited to human beings, however: it can also indicate the *“nature”* or *“type”* of a problem, issue, organization, or phenomenon. 

In this sense, 性格 is broader than the English word *“character”* and can also mean *“specific nature”* or *“underlying characteristics.”* 



### Nuance, context of use, and register

Of the four words, 性格 is the most **neutral and most common in conversation** and everyday writing. It is the natural term when describing traits such as *“cheerful,” “shy,” “stubborn,” “kind,” “introverted,” “strict,” or “sociable.”*   



It can carry either a positive or negative evaluation without sounding marked: expressions such as 性格がいい, 性格がきつい, and 性格が合わない are therefore entirely natural. 



In more technical contexts, it can also appear in expressions such as 性格検査, meaning a *“personality/character test.”*   



Historically, major lexicographical works record examples as early as the eighteenth century, so, unlike 個性 and 人格, it is not primarily a modern conceptual term, although it remains fully current today.

### Examples



The following sentences illustrate the most typical uses of 性格: describing personal character, compatibility between people, and, by extension, the *“nature”* of issues or problems. 



*<p class="jp-example">彼は明るい性格で、初対面の人ともすぐ打ち解ける。</p>*  *“He has a cheerful personality and quickly opens up even to people he has just met.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">私たちは性格がかなり違うが、仕事ではうまく補い合っている。</p>*  *“Our personalities are quite different, but we complement each other well at work.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">子どものころは内気な性格だったが、大学に入って少し積極的になった。</p>*  *“I was shy as a child, but I became a little more outgoing after starting university.”*


*<p class="jp-example">その二つの問題は性格が異なるので、同じ方法では解決できない。</p>*  *“Those two problems are different in nature, so they cannot be solved using the same method.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">面接では能力だけでなく、性格の傾向も見られている。</p>*  *“In an interview, not only a candidate’s abilities but also their personality traits are assessed.”*

## 人格 (jinkaku)



### Kanji analysis



In 人格, the kanji 人 means *“person” or “human being”*; 格 once again carries meanings such as *“quality,” “structure,” “rank,” “standard,” and “dignity.”*   



Within the compound, the overall idea is more *“elevated”* and abstract than in 性格: not so much a person’s everyday character as **the quality of the person as a human being**, or the overall structure of the personality in the psychological sense. 

 
### Meaning 



Lexicographical definitions of 人格 operate on several levels. 



In a general sense, it indicates **the humanity or mode of being characteristic of a person as an autonomous individual**; it can also refer to outstanding human qualities.  

 In psychology, it denotes the unified whole of an individual’s behavioral tendencies, a concept close to 性格 but broader. 



In ethics, it refers to the moral subject endowed with free will; in law, to a subject capable of holding rights and bearing duties.



It is therefore a highly polysemous term, but its different senses are united by a common core: the person considered as a whole and in their status as a human being.



### Nuance, context of use, and register

In contemporary Japanese, 人格 has a much more formal and abstract register than 性格. It occurs naturally in educational, human-rights, ethical, psychological, and legal discourse: 人格の完成, 自己の人格を磨く, 人格形成, 人格権, 相手の人格を尊重する.

According to psychological dictionaries, compared with 性格, 人格 tends to emphasize the unified totality of the person and **may include the cognitive or intellectual sphere more explicitly**, rather than focusing only on emotional and volitional aspects.

For this reason, in ordinary language it is not the most natural word for describing everyday traits such as *“shy,” “stubborn,” or “sociable”*: in those cases, 性格 remains the normal term.   



Historically, moreover, Japanese lexicographical sources record 人格 as a modern term that became established in the late Meiji period and was used, among other things, to translate *“personality.”*



### Examples



The following examples reflect the best-documented uses of 人格: respect for the person, personal development, moral evaluation, and general psychological usage. 



*<p class="jp-example">どのような立場の人でも、その人格は尊重されなければならない。</p>*  *“Whatever a person’s position, their dignity as a person must be respected.”*

*<p class="jp-example">教育は知識だけでなく、人格の形成にも関わる。</p>*  *“Education concerns not only knowledge, but also the development of the whole person.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">その発言は相手の人格を傷つけるおそれがある。</p>*  *“That remark could harm the other person’s dignity.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">彼は能力だけでなく、人格の面でも信頼されている。</p>*  *“He is trusted not only for his abilities, but also for his qualities as a person.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">心理学では、人格を個人の統一的な特徴として捉えることがある。</p>*  *“In psychology, personality is sometimes understood as the unified set of characteristics belonging to an individual.”*

## 人柄 (hitogara)



### Kanji analysis



In 人柄, the first element, 人, means *“person”*; the second, 柄, in the reading がら, can mean *“nature,” “type,” “condition,” or “essential quality.”*  



人柄 therefore literally suggests something like **“the kind of person someone is”** or **“the human qualities that define them.”**   



Unlike 性格 and 人格, the native reading and the word’s longer history contribute to a more concrete and less theoretical impression. 



### Meaning



Dictionaries define 人柄 as **the quality, nature, or dignity possessed by a person**; many definitions also include the idea of *“good human qualities”* or *“excellence of character.”* 



This is crucial: 人柄 does not simply mean *“character”* in a neutral and analytical sense, but very often the *“kind of person”* someone appears to be, especially in their relationships with others.   



Depending on the context, it can therefore be rendered as *“disposition,” “character,” “humanity,” “human qualities,”* or *“moral fiber.”* 

 
### Nuance, context of use, and register



**人柄 is a strongly relational word**. Very often, rather than describing an internal psychological structure, it describes the impression someone gives of the kind of person they are: warmth, trustworthiness, nobility of character, graciousness, helpfulness, or composure.   



For this reason, it often carries a positive or at least gentle tone: typical expressions include 人柄がいい, 温かい人柄, 誠実な人柄, and 人柄がにじみ出る. 



It also readily appears in the respectful form お人柄, common in polite, commemorative, or solemn registers; a particularly clear institutional example can be found in an address from the Imperial Household, where the expression 真面目な温かいお人柄 appears. 





Historically, it is the oldest term in the group, already attested in classical literature, and this partly explains its warmer and less technical tone than 人格. 



### Examples

The following sentences show the most natural uses of 人柄: appreciation of someone’s human qualities, the impression they make in relationships with others, and the polite form お人柄.



*<p class="jp-example">先生は厳しいが、温かい人柄で学生に慕われている。</p>*  *“The teacher is strict, but the students are fond of them because of their warm nature.”*


*<p class="jp-example">数回お会いしただけでも、誠実な人柄がよく伝わってきた。</p>*  *“Even after meeting them only a few times, their sincerity came through clearly.”*


*<p class="jp-example">彼女の手紙には、細やかな気配りのできる人柄が表れている。</p>*  *“Her letters reveal the thoughtful, considerate person she is.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">あの人は能力以上に、その穏やかな人柄で信頼を集めている。</p>*  *“They are trusted above all for their gentle nature, even more than for their abilities.”*


*<p class="jp-example">ご丁寧なお返事から、お人柄の温かさを感じました。</p>*  *“Your gracious reply conveyed the warmth of your character.”*

## 個性 (kosei)



### Kanji analysis



In 個性, the kanji 個 means *“a single individual” or “one by one”*; 性 means *“nature,”* *“disposition,”* or *“inherent quality.”* The compound therefore indicates the specific nature of an individual or thing—that is, what distinguishes it from others. 





If 性格 asks “What are they like as a person, and how do they tend to behave?” 個性 instead asks **“What makes them recognizable, different, or unique?”**



### Meaning



Dictionaries explain 個性 as the distinctive quality specific to a person, organism, object, or other entity.



The word therefore applies to human beings but is not limited to them: it can also refer to styles, works of art, designs, products, and even a particular sporting technique. 



In English, the best equivalent in most cases is not “character,” but **“individuality,”** **“distinctive quality,”** or **“distinctive identity.”** 



### Nuance, context of use, and register



In contemporary Japanese, 個性 is very common in educational, artistic, creative, and social contexts that value what makes each person different: 個性の尊重, 個性を生かす, 個性的なデザイン, 個性が強い. 





Sources from Japan’s Ministry of Education clearly show that **個性 is a key term in modern educational discourse**, where expressions such as 個性の伸長 and even 学習の個性化 are used. 



The relationship with 人格 is also very important: when referring to human beings, the two terms may appear close, but **人格 can also refer to the overall structure shared by every person as a human subject**, whereas 個性 always emphasizes what makes that subject *“this particular individual”* rather than another.



Historically, lexicographical sources trace its spread as a modern term to the early twentieth century.



### Examples



The following examples show the central uses of 個性: personal uniqueness, the value placed on individuality in education, and its application to objects, works, and styles.



*<p class="jp-example">彼女はおとなしいが、作品にははっきりした個性がある。</p>*  *“She is reserved, but her work has a clearly recognizable artistic identity.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">学校は子どもの個性を伸ばす場であるべきだ。</p>*  *“Schools should be places where children can develop their individuality.”*


*<p class="jp-example">この店の内装は個性的で、一度見たら忘れない。</p>*  *“The interior of this shop is highly distinctive and impossible to forget once you have seen it.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">同じ制服を着ていても、着こなしにそれぞれの個性が出る。</p>*  *“Even when everyone wears the same uniform, each person’s individuality comes through in the way they wear it.”* 

*<p class="jp-example">チームには性格の違う人がいるが、その違いが個性として生きている。</p>*  *“The team includes people with different personalities, and those differences become valuable expressions of individuality.”*

## Direct comparison



### Essential differences



In simple terms, the distinction can be summarized as follows: 

- 性格 describes a person’s **stable disposition** (or the nature of a thing or problem);

- 人格 refers to the **person as a whole in human, moral, psychological, or legal terms**;

- 人柄 describes the **human qualities perceived in relationships with others**;

- 個性 indicates the **distinctive element that makes someone or something unique**. 



 This is the most important semantic difference. 

 

A very practical criterion is to look at typical combinations:





- With 性格, verbs and adjectives such as 合う／合わない, 明るい, and きつい are natural; 

- With 人格, typical combinations include 尊重する, 形成, 磨く, and 疑う; 

- With 人柄, words such as いい, 温かい, and 誠実な appear very frequently, as does the polite form お人柄; 

- With 個性, common combinations include 尊重する, 伸ばす, 生かす, 強い, and 個性的.   





In practice, therefore, paying attention to collocations is extremely helpful when choosing the right term. 



### When they are interchangeable—and when they are not



性格 and 人柄 can overlap when referring to positive personal qualities: in many situations, either term can come close to the English words “character” or “disposition.” 



However, 性格 is more analytical and neutral, whereas 人柄 sounds warmer, more personal, more relational, and often more complimentary.



優しい性格 means that kindness is a trait of the person’s character; 温かい人柄 or 誠実な人柄 places greater emphasis on the kind of person revealed in their dealings with others.





性格 and 人格 overlap most clearly in psychological terminology, where sources explain that the two can be almost synonymous. 



In ordinary language, however, they are not interchangeable without a change in nuance: 



- 性格 remains the natural choice for describing everyday personality traits; 

- 人格 is used when the focus shifts to the person as a whole—their dignity, development, or moral and legal status. 



This is why 明るい性格 and 人格を尊重する are natural, whereas 性格を尊重する is far less idiomatic.

性格 and 個性 can come close when a character trait is also what distinguishes someone from others. 



Someone who is very energetic, has a strongly ironic manner, or is extremely reserved may display that quality both as part of their 性格 and as an aspect of their 個性.

 But the implicit question changes: 



- 性格 answers “What is this person like?”;

- 個性 answers “What makes this person different or recognizable?” 



This is why it is natural to say that a school **“develops individuality”** (個性を伸ばす), rather than simply developing *“character”* in the sense of 性格. 



人格 and 人柄 can both point to someone’s goodness or worth as a human being, but the difference in register is clear:



- 人格 belongs to a more solemn, institutional, ethical, or legal register; 

- 人柄 is closer to the concrete impression someone makes in personal interaction.

## Conclusions

Once the differences between 性格, 人格, 人柄, and 個性 have been understood, the main difficulty is no longer remembering their definitions. The real next step is learning to recognize which perspective a particular sentence adopts and, above all, to make the same choice naturally without consciously reconstructing the entire distinction each time.

The first piece of advice is not to begin with the English word *“personality.”* A bilingual dictionary may list it as a translation for all four terms, but this correspondence is not a sufficient criterion for deciding which Japanese word to use.

The English translation should be the result of interpretation, not its starting point. The first question should be: *“From what perspective does this sentence present the person?”* Only afterward can we determine which English rendering is most natural in context.

For a learner of Japanese, therefore, the safest way to choose among the four is not to ask *“Which one means personality?”* but rather: **Are we talking about character, the person as a whole, human qualities, or individuality?** 

Another particularly useful method is to observe the words that accompany the noun. 性格, 人格, 人柄, and 個性 do not occur in isolation: they tend to combine with different verbs, adjectives, and expressions, and those combinations often reveal the perspective being adopted.

Expressions such as 性格が合わない, 人格の完成, 人柄が出る, and 個性を生かす are not merely random examples. They show how each term typically functions in combination: 性格 can describe compatibility, 人格 can be developed or respected, 人柄 can emerge through behavior, and 個性 can be expressed, developed, or brought to the fore.

For this reason, it is better not to memorize bare equivalences such as:

* 性格 = character;
* 人格 = personality;
* 人柄 = disposition;
* 個性 = individuality.

It is far more effective to learn complete combinations, recording not only the noun but also the verb, adjective, or construction with which it appears. In this way, the distinction gradually ceases to be an abstract explanation and becomes a network of recognizable linguistic combinations.

When reading, it is not always necessary to choose an English translation immediately. It may be more useful to suspend translation for a moment and observe the structure of the sentence:

**What is being attributed to the person? Who is making the judgment? Is the sentence describing, evaluating, respecting, perceiving, or highlighting something? Which verb accompanies the noun? Could the word also refer to a work, an organization, or an object, or must the referent necessarily be a person?**

Questions of this kind make it possible to reconstruct the sentence’s point of view without depending on English equivalents that are inevitably imperfect.

It is equally important not to turn the differences described in this article into absolute boundaries. Words in a natural language do not occupy perfectly sealed compartments. In some contexts, more than one term may be grammatically possible, but replacing one with another changes what is brought to the foreground.

In such cases, the most useful question is not always *“Which of the two words is correct?” but “What would change if I chose one rather than the other?”* The sentence might remain understandable and correct while shifting from the description of a tendency to an evaluation of the person, from the observation of a perceived quality to the appreciation of a distinctive trait.

The composition of the kanji can also provide guidance, but it should not be used as an automatic formula. The characters suggest important conceptual associations, yet they cannot by themselves predict every restriction on usage, evaluative nuance, or natural collocation of a word. Etymology and graphic structure explain part of the meaning; actual usage completes the picture.

To consolidate the distinction, an effective strategy is therefore to collect authentic examples and record them together with their context. For each occurrence, one can note the referent, predicate, register, any judgment being expressed, and the reason why that term appears more suitable than the others.

There is no need to accumulate hundreds of sentences. A limited number of carefully observed examples, selected from different contexts, is generally more useful than a long list of decontextualized translations.

You will know that the distinction has truly been internalized when 性格, 人格, 人柄, and 個性 are no longer perceived as four possible translations of the same English word. At that point, when you encounter them in a sentence, you will immediately recognize the particular perspective through which the person—or, in some cases, the thing—is being presented.

The ultimate goal, therefore, is not to learn four equivalences, but to become accustomed to seeing four different ways of framing reality.
